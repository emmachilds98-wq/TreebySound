Where your two careers meet

Bioacoustics & passive acoustic monitoring

The fastest-growing survey method in ecology is leaving recorders in a habitat and letting software identify what’s calling. Everything you’re now learning about mics, gain, clean recording and disciplined metadata transfers straight into it — you’d arrive already fluent in the audio half most ecologists have to learn from scratch.

How to ask for help without freezing
  1. Make the situation safe: mute, stop or keep clear where appropriate.
  2. State the symptom in one line: “Channel 12 has no signal at the desk.”
  3. State what changed and what you checked.
  4. Ask for the next authorised action, not a vague rescue.
  5. Watch the fix and note the reason, not only the button pressed.
How to learn an unfamiliar piece of kit at work
  1. Ask whether you may explore it and when—not during a pressured build.
  2. Find the exact model and open its official manual.
  3. Identify inputs, outputs, power, network/control and normal status lights.
  4. Follow one safe, reversible task with an experienced person nearby.
  5. Write a small “what this box does here” note; never save over a live show file.
How to ask for the equipment-system information

Ask the person who owns warehouse systems rather than exporting data yourself:

  1. “I’m building a private learning app for my audio development. May I have a read-only list or screenshots of the labels and categories I am allowed to use?”
  2. Ask specifically for the fields TreebySound needs: stock name, manufacturer/model, internal label/barcode, category, case or shelf location, connector/cable type, prep notes, service status and hire-in flag.
  3. Confirm what must not be copied: client data, supplier prices, serial numbers, access credentials, job contacts or live availability.
  4. Start with one small category—microphones, DIs or common cable types—and compare the export against physical labels with a supervisor.
  5. Only then decide whether to import a cleaned reference list or simply link out to the company system. Keeping one source of truth is more important than copying everything.

0.3 What this book assumes

It assumes UK working practice, because that's where the regs and the radio frequencies come from. It assumes you're working for a hire company or in-house AV team doing a mix of corporate, live music, theatre and events — the broad "technical event" world rather than one narrow specialism. And it assumes you'd rather understand why than memorise a sequence, because the sequence changes every time and the why doesn't.

Where a section is thin because the answer depends entirely on your specific kit, it says so and points you at the manual. That's not a cop-out — reading the actual manual for the actual box is the single highest-value habit in this job and almost nobody does it.

0.4 Where you're actually starting from

Six months of logging kit in and out, testing it, seeing what everything is, and being shown a setup once or twice for fun rather than for real. That's a specific and genuinely useful starting point, and it's worth being honest about what it does and doesn't give you, because this book is written to build from exactly here.

What it gives you: you already know what the kit is — what a stagebox looks like, roughly what lives in which flightcase, what a DI box does even if you've never wired one into a show yourself. That's the vocabulary most beginners spend their first month just acquiring. You skipped that month.

What it doesn't give you yet: the muscle memory of doing it under a bit of pressure, with someone waiting, without someone more experienced quietly fixing your mistakes before you notice them. That only comes from reps — and you haven't had many yet. That's completely normal at six months, whatever it might feel like watching someone who's done it for years make it look easy.

Standard

Read this book in the order it's written the first time through, even the bits that feel basic — the basics are exactly where "I sort of know this" quietly turns into a real gap under pressure. After that, use it as a reference: search for what you need, when you need it.

Two parts are worth flagging specifically for where you are right now. Part 12 (Fault-finding) is written to be usable even before you have deep intuition for this stuff — it gives you an actual procedure to follow, not just "get a feel for it", because a script beats a feeling every time when someone's looking at you expectantly. And §9.8 onwards covers Avolites Titan in real depth, because you've already got a head start there that's worth building on properly rather than leaving half-used.

Also seen

Nobody who's good at this job was born knowing it. Every engineer whose fault-finding looks like a magic trick got there by fixing the same handful of problems dozens of times each until the pattern-matching became automatic. You're not behind — you're at the very start of building exactly that.

PART 1

How a job runs & who owns what

The working day, the people around you, paperwork and the habits that make you dependable.

1.1 What the role actually is

"Audio technician" in the event world is really four jobs wearing one coat, and which one you're doing changes by the day:

Warehouse
Prep, test, pack, unpack, fault-find, maintain, keep the stock honest. You're doing this bit already. It's the foundation — a well-prepped job barely goes wrong on site.
Crew
Load in, push boxes, run cable, build stacks, take direction. Physical, fast, and where you learn the most by watching.
Tech
Actually setting things up and making them work — patching, powering, testing, line-checking, fixing. This is the step you're moving into.
Operator
Mixing the show, or running playback, or looking after monitors. The visible job, and the one everyone thinks is the whole job. It isn't.

Most people move roughly along that line, but not cleanly — you'll be crewing on Monday, teching on Wednesday, and back in the warehouse Friday. That's normal and it's a good thing. The best techs are the ones who've done every part of the chain, because when something breaks on site they know what happened to it in the van.

1.2 The lifecycle of a hire

Everything you touch is somewhere on this loop. Knowing where you are on it tells you what your job is right now.

  1. Enquiry & spec — client asks for something, someone works out what kit answers it. Produces a kit list.
  2. Prep — kit pulled from shelves, tested, assembled into racks and cases, consumables added. Produces a packed job.
  3. Load out — onto the van/truck, in a sensible order (last on, first off).
  4. Load in & build — on site, unpacked, positioned, rigged, cabled, powered.
  5. Test — system check, line check, RF walk. Part 11 is entirely this step, because it's the one that decides whether the show is calm or awful.
  6. Show — operate, monitor, stay alert, fix quietly.
  7. Derig — down, coiled, cased, counted, loaded.
  8. Return & check-in — back on the shelf, but only after it's been checked. Damaged or suspect kit gets flagged, not shelved.
Watch it

Step 8 is where companies quietly lose money and reputation. A faulty mic that goes back on the shelf untested becomes someone's emergency in three weeks' time. Never shelve something you're not sure about — see §11.5 on quarantining.

1.5 The paperwork, and why it matters

Every document below exists because someone once got it wrong without one. Learn to read all of them; learn to write the first three.

Input list
Numbered list of every source: 1 Kick In, 2 Kick Out, 3 Snare Top… It includes the mic type and whether it needs phantom or a DI. This is the single most useful bit of paper in live sound.
Patch list
Where each input physically lands — which stagebox, which socket, which console channel. Sometimes merged with the input list.
Stage plan
A top-down drawing of who stands where, where the wedges point, where the DIs and power are. Lets you build the stage before anyone arrives. Example below.
Kit list
What's going on the job. Your warehouse world. Check it against what's physically in the cases, not against what the system says.
Tech spec / rider
The artist's or client's requirements. Riders are aspirational; specs are contractual. Read them and flag anything you can't supply early.
Schedule
Times for load-in, soundcheck, doors, curfew. Curfew is usually a legal condition, not a suggestion.
RAMS
Risk Assessment & Method Statement. The safety paperwork for the job. See §13.7.
Line check sheet
Your own tick list for proving inputs. See Part 19.
Standard

Get a copy of the input list and stage plan on your phone before you leave the warehouse. Half of prep mistakes are "we didn't know they needed four DIs" and that's a five-second check.

1.6 Site etiquette — the unwritten stuff

This gets you rebooked more reliably than technical skill does, especially early on.

  • Don't touch what isn't yours. Never repatch, unplug, move or "tidy" someone else's kit without asking. Especially not a console. Especially not during a show.
  • Ask before you assume. "Do you want this here?" costs four seconds. Moving a stack twice costs twenty minutes.
  • Say when you don't know. Nobody minds "I've not done this before, show me once". Everyone minds finding out at line check that you guessed.
  • Wear black. Blacks on stage from soundcheck onwards, always in theatre. Steel toes on site.
  • Radio discipline. Listen before you talk, say who you are and who you want, keep it short, say "copy" not a monologue. Don't editorialise on the ring — everyone hears it, including the client.
  • Keep your area tidy as you go. Cable off walkways, cases stacked, tape off the floor. Tidy sites are safe sites and it's noticed.
  • Don't disappear. If you're going for a break or to the van, tell someone.
  • Eat and drink. Twelve-hour days will find you out. Water, food when catering's open, and don't let the first thing you consume all day be at wrap. The safety side of long days is §13.9.
  • Look busy is not the same as be busy. But when there's genuinely nothing to do, ask if anyone needs a hand rather than sitting on a case with your phone out.
Also seen

House style varies a lot on formality. A West End theatre and a festival stage have very different tolerances for chat, phones and clothing. Read the room in the first hour — watch what the experienced crew do and match it.

1.7 Advancing a show

"Advancing" is the work that happens before anyone loads a van — a call or email exchange with the client, venue or artist to find out what you're actually walking into. Early on, you probably won't run this call yourself, but you'll be handed its output (the input list, stage plan and schedule in §1.5) and you'll increasingly be the one someone asks "did anyone check X?" — so it's worth knowing what a good advance actually covers.

  1. Confirm the basics first — date, times, venue address, load-in access (loading bay, stairs, lift size, parking restrictions).
  2. Get the input list and stage plan, even a rough one — see §1.5 for what good versions of these look like.
  3. Ask what power is available and where it is relative to FOH and the stage — a venue that says "there's plenty of power" has usually not actually checked.
  4. Ask what's already in the room — house PA, house console, existing rigging points — so you know what you're bringing versus what's provided. Assuming a "fully equipped venue" has what you need, without checking, is a common and expensive mistake.
  5. Confirm who else is on site — other departments, their call times, whether you're sharing power or cable routes with them (Part 9).
  6. Check any venue-specific restrictions — noise limits (§6.10), curfews, no-fly zones for rigging, restricted load-in hours.
  7. Flag anything you can't supply, early — a rider requesting kit that's not in stock, a schedule that doesn't leave enough time to build safely. Raising this a week out is a conversation; raising it on the day is a crisis.
Standard

A good advance answers questions before they become problems on site. If you're ever handed an advance that's clearly thin — missing power info, no stage plan, vague on access — it's worth flagging to whoever's running the job rather than just quietly hoping it works out.

PART 2

Audio essentials

The signal-flow mental model everything else hangs on. Learn this properly before chasing specialist knowledge.

2.1 The chain, start to finish

Every single audio system, from a lectern in a meeting room to a stadium, is this:

SOURCE a voice, a guitar amp, a laptop ↓ TRANSDUCER microphone / DI — turns sound or an instrument signal into a small electrical one ↓ PREAMP console input — brings that tiny signal up to a usable working level ↓ PROCESSING EQ, dynamics, effects, routing, mixing ↓ AMPLIFIER power amp or the amp inside an active speaker ↓ TRANSDUCER loudspeaker — turns electricity back into sound ↓ EARS the only part of the system that actually matters

When something doesn't work, you are always asking one question: where along this chain did it stop? That's it. That's fault-finding (Part 12). Everything else is detail.

Standard

Learn to picture the chain physically — "the signal is currently in that XLR, going into socket 4 of that stagebox, arriving on channel 4 of the console". People who can do this fix things in seconds. People who can't press buttons hopefully.

2.3 Balanced vs unbalanced, and why XLR won

Unbalanced (a guitar lead, an RCA, a 3.5mm jack) carries the signal on one conductor with a shield around it. Any interference the cable picks up along the way gets added to the signal. Fine for two metres, hopeless for twenty.

Balanced (XLR, TRS) carries the same signal twice — once normal, once polarity-flipped — plus a shield. At the far end, the receiving device flips one back and adds them together. The wanted signal doubles; anything picked up equally by both wires cancels out. This is called common-mode rejection, and it's why you can run a balanced mic line 100 m past a dimmer rack and still get clean audio.

Practical rule
Balanced for anything long or anything mic level. Unbalanced only for short runs of line-level, and get it onto a DI as soon as you can.
Pinout
XLR pin 1 = screen/earth, pin 2 = hot (+), pin 3 = cold (−). Memorable as "one is ground, two is hot". This is the AES standard and effectively universal now.
Direction
Signal flows out of male pins and into female sockets. So a cable has a female end that goes on the source, and a male end that goes into the destination. "Female follows the source" is one way to remember it.
Also seen

Some vintage gear (older Japanese consoles, some 70s/80s equipment) is wired pin 3 hot. If you meet a polarity problem you can't explain on one specific old box, this is why. Modern kit is pin 2 hot without exception.

2.4 Gain vs volume vs trim vs fader

People use these interchangeably and it causes endless confusion. They are not the same control.

Gain / Trim
At the input. Sets how much the preamp amplifies the incoming signal to bring it to a healthy internal working level. Set once, correctly, then mostly leave alone. Too little = noise. Too much = distortion.
Fader
After the processing. Sets how much of that channel goes into the mix. This is your musical control — move it constantly.
Volume / Master
At the output. How loud the whole thing is.

The distinction matters because turning up the wrong one has completely different consequences. If a vocal is too quiet, the fader is almost always the answer. If a vocal is quiet and hissy, the gain was set too low. If it's distorting even with the fader down, the gain is too high — the damage already happened at the input.

2.5 Gain structure & headroom

This is the concept that separates people whose systems sound clean from people whose systems sound rough, and it's genuinely simple: at every stage of the chain, the signal should be comfortably in the middle of what that stage can handle.

Too low and you're amplifying noise later on to compensate. Too high and you clip. The space between your normal level and the point of clipping is headroom, and you want plenty of it because live sources are unpredictable — the singer who soundchecked politely will be twice as loud with an audience in.

Standard

On a digital console, aim your input gain so normal programme material sits around −18 dBFS average, peaking to maybe −10 dBFS. That leaves 10–18 dB of headroom for surprises. On most desks that's the boundary between the green and the yellow on the meter. If you're regularly touching red, you've gained it too hot.

Also seen

Plenty of engineers gain hotter than that — chasing the sound of a preamp being driven, or working on kit where the noise floor is worse than the clipping. And analogue desks are a different game entirely: there, 0 VU is your target and the meters mean something different. The −18 dBFS figure is a digital-console rule of thumb, not a law.

Go deeper

Search "gain staging live sound" and read anything from Rational Acoustics or ProSoundWeb. It's a concept worth understanding rather than memorising — see §18.4.

2.7 Impedance, briefly

Impedance is resistance to an AC signal, measured in ohms (Ω). You mostly need three facts:

  • Mic inputs are low impedance and expect a low-impedance source. Mics are typically 150–600 Ω, inputs around 1.5–3 kΩ. This mismatch is deliberate — it's called bridging and it's correct.
  • Instrument pickups are high impedance. Plug a guitar straight into a mic input and it'll sound thin and lifeless because the input loads the pickup down. That's what a DI box is for (§3.9).
  • Speakers have impedance too — usually 4, 8 or 16 Ω. This matters when you're wiring multiple passive speakers to one amp channel: two 8 Ω boxes in parallel make a 4 Ω load, which draws more current from the amp. Go too low and the amp overheats or shuts down. Modern active boxes make this a non-issue, which is one reason they took over.

2.8 Polarity vs phase

Used interchangeably in conversation; technically different, and the difference occasionally matters.

Polarity
Whether the waveform is the right way up. Flipping it inverts the whole signal at all frequencies. The Ø button on your console does this. Instant, frequency-independent.
Phase
A time relationship between two signals. Because a fixed time delay is a different fraction of a cycle at different frequencies, phase problems are frequency-dependent — which is why two mics on the same source at different distances sound hollow, not just quiet.

Practically: if two mics on the same source sound thin and weedy together but fine alone, try the polarity flip on one. If it gets fuller, you had a polarity problem. If it changes but doesn't fix it, you've got a distance/timing problem and the answer is moving a mic, not pressing a button.

Standard

Classic cases where you'll reach for the polarity button: snare top vs snare bottom (bottom mic almost always gets flipped), kick in vs kick out, a guitar cab mic'd front and back, and DI vs mic on the same bass rig.

2.9 Analogue, digital, and the clock

Digital audio samples the waveform many thousands of times a second and stores each sample as a number.

Sample rate
How many snapshots per second. 48 kHz is the live/video standard; 44.1 kHz is the CD/music standard; 96 kHz exists and is rarely worth the I/O cost live. Everything on one network must agree.
Bit depth
How precisely each snapshot is measured. 24-bit is standard and gives you a huge dynamic range. This is why digital headroom is generous — but clipping is still absolute and ugly.
Latency
The delay caused by converting, processing and transporting. A few milliseconds is invisible; stack up enough conversions and processors and musicians start to notice, especially on in-ears.
Clock
Every digital device must agree on exactly when a sample happens. One device is the master and the rest follow. Get this wrong and you get clicks, pops, or dropouts.
Watch it

Two clock masters on one network is a classic and infuriating fault — intermittent ticking that comes and goes. When you build a digital system, decide what the clock master is (usually the console, or a dedicated clock source) and check every other device is set to follow. See §8.2 for how this looks in Dante Controller.

The dB is a ratio, not a fixed amount, which is why it always has a suffix telling you what it's a ratio to.

Rules of thumb worth memorising

  • +3 dB = double the power. Barely audible as a change.
  • +6 dB = double the voltage. Clearly audible.
  • +10 dB = roughly twice as loud to a human. Needs ten times the power.
  • Double the distance = −6 dB from a point source. The inverse square law. This is why the front row is deafened while the back is quiet, and why line arrays and delay stacks exist.
  • Doubling the number of identical sources = +3 dB (if uncorrelated) or +6 dB (if perfectly correlated, e.g. coupled subs).

The fact that the connector fits doesn't mean the cable is right.

3.3 speakON — and why speaker cable is different

Neutrik speakON is the standard for connecting amplifiers to passive speakers. It twists to lock, the contacts are shrouded so you can't touch live terminals, and it handles serious current.

NL2
Two poles (+1/−1). One channel.
NL4
Four poles (+1/−1/+2/−2). Two channels down one cable — used for bi-amped boxes, or for a top and a sub, or to link through.
NL8
Eight poles. Multi-way, mostly on line array and larger systems.

Speaker cable is not signal cable. It's unshielded, heavy-gauge (typically 2.5 mm² or 4 mm² for long runs) because it's carrying real current. Using thin cable on a long run loses power and damping — the bass goes soft. Using signal cable will, at best, sound bad; at worst it melts.

Watch it

An NL4 will physically plug into an NL8 socket and vice versa in some combinations, and a two-pole cable in a four-pole socket may give you nothing, or may give you the wrong driver. If a top is silent but the sub works, check you haven't got a cable wired on 2+/2− going into a box expecting 1+/1−. Label your speaker cables by pinout, not just length.

3.6 Network cable — now core audio infrastructure

A huge amount of modern live audio travels over ordinary-looking network cable, which means network faults are now audio faults.

  • Cat5e is adequate for most audio networking; Cat6 and Cat6a give more margin. Use shielded (STP/FTP) for touring — the electrical environment is hostile.
  • etherCON is Neutrik's ruggedised housing around a standard RJ45. It latches, it survives being trodden on, and it's what you should use on site. A bare RJ45 on a floor is a fault waiting to happen.
  • 100 m is the hard limit for copper Ethernet, per hop. Beyond that you need a switch in the middle or fibre.
  • Fibre (usually with opticalCON connectors) for long runs, and it's immune to electrical interference. Treat it gently — the minimum bend radius is real and a kinked fibre is a dead fibre.
Watch it

Network cable does not like being coiled tightly, crushed by a case wheel, or kinked. It'll often still pass a link light while dropping packets — which sounds like intermittent clicks and dropouts and is a nightmare to trace. If you suspect a Cat5 run, swap it before you spend an hour in software.

3.10 Cable care — coiling, labelling, storage

You already do a lot of this, so this is mostly the why.

Over-under coiling

The correct way to coil any cable: alternate loops, one twisted one way and the next the other. The cable stores with no net twist and pays out flat with a straight pull. The wrong way — winding it all the same direction round your elbow — builds a twist into the cable every single coil, which over months breaks the conductors inside and turns a good cable into an intermittent one.

Standard

Over-under, every time, and coil in the cable's natural loop size. Tie with the cable's own velcro or a proper cable tie — never a knot in the cable itself, and never a tight zip tie which crushes the jacket.

Also seen

Some people figure-of-eight long runs instead, and some theatre departments coil onto drums for permanent installs. Both are fine. What's not fine is elbow-winding, and you'll see people do it constantly.

Other habits

  • Pull from the connector's cable, never the connector. The strain relief exists but it's not infinite.
  • Never pull a cable round a sharp corner under tension.
  • Label both ends if it's part of a system. Numbered heatshrink or a wrap of LTC (paper tape) with a Sharpie.
  • Don't run cable over doorways at head height, don't tape it across fire exits, and get it out of walkways or under a ramp.
  • Store coiled and hung, not in a heap in the bottom of a case where the weight crushes the ones underneath.

4.3 Proximity effect & other physical realities

Proximity effect
All directional mics boost low frequencies as the source gets closer. A singer eating the mic gets a big warm boom; backing off two inches thins it out. Vocalists use this deliberately. You compensate with the high-pass filter.
Off-axis colouration
Sound arriving from the side isn't just quieter, it's differently coloured — usually duller. Good mics have smooth off-axis response; cheap ones sound honky when the singer turns their head.
Plosives
"P" and "B" sounds fire a blast of air at the diaphragm, producing a low thump. Fixed with a pop shield, a foam windshield, slight off-axis positioning, and a high-pass filter.
Handling noise
Vibration through the body of the mic. Worse on condensers and on mics without internal shockmounting. Shockmounts and stand isolation help.
Wind
Outdoors, a foam windshield is not enough. You need a proper furry ("dead cat", "windjammer"). Wind noise is huge, low-frequency, and eats your headroom.
Standard

Engage the high-pass filter on almost every channel that isn't kick, bass or a full-range source. Somewhere around 80–100 Hz on vocals, 100–120 Hz on most other things. It removes rumble, handling noise, stage vibration and plosive energy that contributes nothing but eats headroom. This is genuinely one of the highest-value habits in live sound.

4.6 The 3:1 rule, and stereo techniques

4.8 Handling & care

  • Don't blow into a mic to test it. It's crude, it tells you almost nothing, and on a condenser or ribbon it can genuinely cause damage. Speak into it, or scratch the grille gently.
  • Don't tap the grille of a condenser either — it's a big impulse straight into a sensitive diaphragm.
  • Condensation kills condensers. Bringing a cold mic from a van into a warm room causes moisture on the diaphragm — you get crackling and low output. Let it acclimatise; don't panic and bin it.
  • Store in their cases/pouches, not loose in a drawer. Foam-lined mic cases exist for a reason.
  • Silica gel in mic cases is worth doing for expensive condensers and worn mics.
  • Clean grilles. Vocal mics get genuinely disgusting. Most unscrew — wash the grille and the foam in warm soapy water, dry completely before reassembling. This is a proper warehouse task and it matters for both sound and hygiene.
  • Handle by the body, not the grille. Grip oils and dents both cause problems.
Standard

Clean and check every vocal mic on return, not at the next prep. It's five minutes when it's part of the check-in routine and it's a crisis when you discover it at 8am with a van to load.

PART 5

Wireless & RF

Its own discipline, its own vocabulary, and the thing most likely to make you look bad in front of an audience.

5.1 Why RF gets its own part

Everything else in this book is a chain you can trace with your hands. RF is invisible, shared with everyone else in the building and the surrounding area, legally regulated, and fails in ways that are hard to diagnose while a presenter is mid-sentence. It's also the area where a bit of knowledge makes you disproportionately valuable — a lot of technicians never get past "put a battery in it and hope".

The good news: 90% of RF reliability comes from about six habits, and they're all in §5.9.

Diversity

Almost all professional receivers are true diversity: two antennas, two receiver sections, and the unit continuously picks whichever is getting the better signal. This defeats "dropouts" caused by multipath — the same signal arriving via two paths and cancelling itself at one point in space. Two antennas, spaced apart, are very unlikely to both be in a null at once.

Standard

Space diversity antennas roughly a wavelength apart (about 0.5 m at UHF) — or more — and angle them outwards at about 45° from each other. Get them above head height and with line of sight to the performers. Bodies are mostly water and water absorbs UHF; an antenna behind a crowd is an antenna in a swamp.

Watch it

Coax loses signal, and thin coax loses a lot. A long run of cheap RG-58 to a paddle can lose more than the paddle gains, making things worse. Use proper low-loss coax for long runs, keep runs as short as you can, and use an active antenna or in-line amplifier if you genuinely need distance.

Antenna distribution

Running more than about four receivers? Don't give each one its own pair of whips — they interfere with each other and it's a forest of aerials. Use an antenna distro: one pair of good antennas feeds a splitter which feeds all the receivers, and it usually passes DC to power active antennas and daisy-chains power to the receivers too. Cleaner, more reliable, less cable.

5.5 Coordination & intermodulation

You can't just pick frequencies at random. Two transmitters don't only occupy their own frequencies — they mix together in the front end of receivers and produce intermodulation products at predictable mathematical relationships (the classic being 2×f1 − f2). Those products can land squarely on a third channel you're using, and that channel will be noisy or drop out even though nothing is obviously "on" its frequency.

This is why manufacturers supply coordination groups — pre-calculated sets of frequencies that are intermod-clean with each other. Use them.

  1. Scan the spectrum on site to find what's already in use — TV transmissions, other companies' kit, the venue's own systems.
  2. Coordinate — pick a clean group, either using the receiver's built-in group/channel scheme or software.
  3. Deploy the frequencies to the transmitters, usually by IR sync from the receiver.
  4. Document what's on what. Write it down. You will need it.
Standard

Always scan on site, on the day, with the venue's own systems and the other departments' kit switched on. A scan done in an empty hall at 8am is a different spectrum from the one you'll have at 8pm. On multi-company events there is usually a designated RF coordinator — find them and get your frequencies from them rather than grabbing your own.

Go deeper

Shure Wireless Workbench and Sennheiser WSM are both free and both do proper coordination and monitoring. Learning one of them is a big, visible step up in capability. Links in §18.5.

5.6 Batteries

The most common cause of a wireless failure is a flat battery, and it's completely preventable.

  • Fresh batteries for every show, not every day, not "it looked fine". Standard
  • Good alkalines (Duracell Procell, Energizer Industrial) if you're on disposables. Cheap alkalines have wildly variable capacity and dump their voltage suddenly.
  • Never use zinc-carbon and be careful with generic rechargeable NiMH in analogue systems — their lower nominal voltage (1.2 V vs 1.5 V) means the receiver's battery meter reads wrong and you get much less warning.
  • Proper rechargeable systems (Shure SB900 series, Sennheiser BA series) report accurate remaining time in hours and minutes and are far better. If your company has them, use them and learn the charging discipline.
  • Battery contacts get corroded and bent. Clean them; check the pack door closes firmly. A pack that cuts out when the performer moves is often a mechanical contact problem, not RF.
Standard

Build a battery routine: new cells go in at a fixed point (e.g. at the top of soundcheck), used-but-good cells go in a separate labelled box for rehearsals and testing, and dead ones go straight in the recycling tub. Never let used and fresh mix in the same bag — that's how a "fresh" battery dies in Act Two.

IEMs sit directly in someone's ear canal. A feedback squeal, a dropped mic, or a mis-patch at full level can cause real hearing injury. Build the habit of bringing IEM levels up from zero, always limiting the IEM outputs, and never soloing something into a live IEM mix.

5.8 Setting up and proving a wireless system

  1. Position antennas first — line of sight, above head height, away from metal, away from LED walls and lighting racks (both are RF-noisy).
  2. Power up receivers only, all transmitters off, and scan. This shows you the noise floor and anything already occupying the band.
  3. Assign frequencies from a clean coordinated group.
  4. Sync each transmitter to its receiver (usually IR). Label the transmitter with the channel name — gaffa and a Sharpie, or proper channel tape.
  5. Set transmitter gain so that the loudest expected input peaks near but not into the transmitter's limiter. This is set on the pack, and getting it wrong here cannot be fixed at the desk — too low is noisy, too high is distorted.
  6. Check RF and AF meters on the receiver. Strong steady RF, healthy audio.
  7. Walk test. Walk the entire performance area — stage, wings, backstage route, into the crowd if they go there — talking into the mic while someone watches the meters and listens. Find the dead spots before the show does.
  8. Set squelch as low as reliably possible. Too high and you get dropouts on weak signal; too low and you get noise bursts when the transmitter is off.
  9. Document the frequencies, the pack assignments and the gain settings.
Standard

Do the walk test with the show conditions in place as far as possible — audience is the thing you can't replicate, so bias your margin generously. A system that's marginal in an empty room will fail in a full one.

5.10 RF exposure & EMF safety

Genuinely low on the list of things to worry about day to day, but worth actually knowing about rather than never hearing of it until someone asks you a question you can't answer. In the UK, the Control of Electromagnetic Fields at Work Regulations 2016 require employers to assess workplace exposure to electromagnetic fields — including RF — against exposure limits based on international (ICNIRP) guidance, and to control it where a real risk exists.

Standard

At the power levels and distances you'll actually work with — a handheld transmitter a performer is holding, a bodypack against their skin, a wedge-mounted receive antenna — this is not a realistic hazard. The situation actually worth knowing about is higher-power multi-channel IEM transmit combiners and antenna racks, where several transmitters' output is combined into one feed at meaningfully higher total power. Manufacturers of that class of kit publish a minimum safe working distance from the transmit antenna for exactly this reason — check the manual for anything you're not personally familiar with, and don't lean against or work for extended periods pressed right up against a live high-power transmit antenna.

Also seen

You are far more likely to be asked about this by a venue's own health and safety officer doing due diligence than to encounter an actual problem. Being able to say "our transmit power and distances are well within manufacturer guidance" is the correct answer, not a shrug.

PA & sound systems

The output end: what the boxes are, how they go together and how a system serves a room safely.

6.1 Anatomy of a loudspeaker

Driver
The moving part. A cone driver (woofer) for low and mid; a compression driver on a horn for high.
Horn / waveguide
The flare in front of the HF driver. It controls where the high frequencies go — the "dispersion pattern", quoted as e.g. 90° × 60° (horizontal × vertical).
Crossover
Splits the signal so each driver only gets the frequencies it can handle.
Passive
Crossover is inside the box, after the amplifier. One amp channel feeds the whole box. Simple, needs external amps.
Active / powered
Amplifier and DSP built into the box. Plug in mains and a signal. Dominant now for small and mid-scale work.
Bi-amp / tri-amp
Separate amp channels per driver, crossover done before the amps in DSP. More control, better performance, more cabling.
Watch it

A passive box wired to an amp with the wrong preset, or a bi-amp box fed a full-range signal on both inputs, will destroy the HF driver in seconds. Compression drivers handle a few watts; a woofer handles hundreds. If you're ever unsure whether a box is passive, active or bi-amp, find out before you send it signal.

6.5 Rigging & flying — the honest version

Watch it

Rigging is a qualified discipline and this section will not teach it to you. Flown loudspeakers weigh hundreds of kilos and hang over people. What follows is so you understand what's happening around you and can be a useful pair of hands, not so you can do it yourself.

Ground stack
Boxes stacked on the floor or on subs, often on a frame. Simpler, no rigging, but sightlines and coverage suffer.
Flown
Hung from the venue's structure via motors or fixed points. Better coverage, better sightlines, more skill and paperwork.
Bumper / fly frame
The top piece an array hangs from.
Pull-back
A second line pulling the bottom of an array backwards to set its angle.
SWL / WLL
Safe Working Load / Working Load Limit — the rated maximum. Never exceeded, never guessed.
Secondary / safety
A backup attachment that catches the load if the primary fails.
LOLER / PUWER
The UK regulations covering lifting equipment and its inspection. See §13.3.
Standard

Universal rule on any site: nobody stands under a suspended load. Not "briefly", not "just to plug this in". If a motor is moving, the area below it is clear. Learn to notice this and step out of the way without being told — people notice.

6.6 Coverage, aiming and subs

The job of system design is getting roughly the same experience everywhere in the audience. That means aiming boxes at people, not at walls, and accepting that the front will always be louder than the back.

Point source aiming

Aim the box so its on-axis line points at the furthest listeners, not the nearest. The near listeners are already close (loads of level from proximity) and are covered by the off-axis spill. Aiming at the front row wastes your throw and deafens them.

Sub configurations

ArrangementWhat it does
Left/right stacksSimple, but creates a "power alley" of coupling down the centre and cancellation at the sides.
Centre cluster / mono subsAll subs together in the middle. Even coverage, no power alley. Very common now.
Cardioid subsOne or more subs reversed and processed so the energy behind the stack cancels. Keeps bass off the stage and out of the neighbours' windows.
End-fireSubs in a line front-to-back, each delayed, so energy adds forwards and cancels backwards. Needs depth but works beautifully.
Sub arc / gradientCurved or delay-shaded lines to widen or shape the low-frequency coverage.
Standard

Cardioid or end-fire subs are worth understanding early, because they solve two problems at once: they clean up the stage (happier musicians, less low-end mud in the mics) and they cut the noise complaint risk from behind the stage. On a lot of jobs they're the difference between passing and failing the noise conditions.

Also seen

At the top end of touring and festival work you'll increasingly meet immersive or object-based audio — systems like L-Acoustics L-ISA or d&b Soundscape, which use many individually-controlled boxes across the width of a room (rather than one stereo pair) to place sounds precisely and move them around the space, plus ambisonic approaches for fully surrounding a room. It's a specialism of its own, well beyond what this book covers — worth knowing it exists so a job spec mentioning it doesn't come as a surprise, not something you're expected to already understand.

6.8 System tuning — what's actually happening

You won't be tuning systems yet, but knowing what the systems tech is doing means you can help rather than hover.

  1. Verification — prove every box works, is on the right preset, and is polarity-correct. Boring, essential, and often the bit you'll be doing.
  2. Level matching — get all the subsystems (mains, fills, delays) to comparable levels.
  3. Time alignment — delay the nearer subsystem so its sound arrives at the same moment as the further one. A delay stack 30 m back needs roughly 87 ms of delay (sound travels about 344 m/s, so ~2.9 ms per metre).
  4. Crossover alignment — get the subs and tops working together at the crossover point rather than cancelling.
  5. EQ — correct broad tonal problems in the system's response. Not fixing the room; you cannot EQ a room (§6.15).
  6. Listen — with known reference material, from several places in the room. Measurement gets you to sensible; ears get you to good.
Also seen

There's a real philosophical split here. One camp measures extensively with Smaart or similar and tunes to a target curve. Another camp tunes almost entirely by ear with familiar tracks. Most good engineers do both — measure to find and fix real problems, then listen to make it sound right. Be suspicious of anyone who says one method is the only method.

Go deeper

This is the deepest rabbit hole in live audio and it's genuinely fascinating. Rational Acoustics (Smaart) run training; the Bob McCarthy book Sound Systems: Design and Optimization is the standard text. See §18.6 and §18.7.

6.9 Monitor world

Stage monitoring is a separate craft from FOH and arguably harder — you're building six to sixteen different mixes simultaneously for people who can't tell you what's wrong except by pointing.

Wedges
Angled floor boxes. Aimed at the performer's head, positioned so the vocal mic's null faces them.
Mix count
How many independent monitor mixes you're providing. Every performer wanting their own is expensive in outputs and time.
Pre-fade sends
Monitor sends are pre-fade so FOH fader moves don't change what the band hears. This is close to universal and getting it wrong is very obvious.
Ringing out
Deliberately pushing a monitor until it feeds back, identifying the frequency, and cutting it — repeated a few times to raise gain before feedback.
Graphic EQ
Traditionally a 31-band GEQ per wedge. Still the standard on many desks.
Also seen

Ringing out a wedge with a graphic before soundcheck is the traditional approach and still widely done. Others prefer to leave the monitor flat and only cut narrow parametric notches reactively when something actually rings, arguing that pre-emptive ringing out just makes everything sound worse for a problem that might not occur. Both are legitimate; the second is increasingly common with modern boxes and good mic technique.

Watch it

Never point a wedge at the back of a vocal mic, never let someone stand between the wedge and their own mic, and never turn a monitor up "just to check" while someone's face is next to it. Feedback at wedge level is painful and can damage hearing.

6.10 Loudness, limits and the law

dBA
A-weighted — approximates human sensitivity, discounts bass heavily. What most noise conditions are written in.
dBC
C-weighted — much flatter, includes the low end. Better for measuring what actually annoys neighbours.
Leq
Equivalent continuous level — an average over a period, e.g. "Leq 15 min". This is how limits are usually written, not as an instantaneous peak.
Venue noise limiter
A device that cuts the power to the stage if the level exceeds a threshold. Common in function rooms and hotels. Find out if there is one, where it is and what it's set to before you build.
Licence conditions
Many events have legally binding noise limits and curfews as conditions of the premises licence (or, for smaller one-off events, a Temporary Event Notice) granted under the Licensing Act 2003. For bigger or higher-risk events, a Safety Advisory Group — the local authority, police, fire and other agencies working together — reviews the event and can set or tighten these conditions, often drawing on the guidance in the Purple Guide. That matters practically: a limit breach isn't just "the neighbours are annoyed", it's a breach of a legal condition the promoter's licence depends on, which is why the SAG or an environmental health officer can shut a show down over it. Breaching them has consequences for the promoter, not just embarrassment.
Watch it

Protect your hearing from day one. Hearing damage is cumulative, permanent and completely invisible until it's done. The UK Control of Noise at Work Regulations 2005 set action levels at 80 dB(A) and 85 dB(A) daily exposure — a loud gig blows past both. Carry decent filtered earplugs (Alpine, Etymotic, EarPeace — around £20 and they don't muffle) and get custom moulds when you can afford them (£150–200, last years). Every experienced engineer you meet either wears them or wishes they had.

6.11 Assistive listening & hearing loops

Not part of the PA, but genuinely part of the job — audio techs are very often the ones who end up responsible for these, simply because they're the closest thing to "audio" on site.

What it is
A hearing/induction loop is a wire run around a room (or a smaller area, sometimes called a counter loop) driven by a dedicated loop driver amplifier. It creates a magnetic field carrying the audio, which a hearing aid's built-in T-coil picks up directly — bypassing the room's background noise entirely, rather than just amplifying it along with everything else.
The legal side
Under the Equality Act 2010, venues are expected to make reasonable adjustments for disabled customers, which in practice includes providing a working induction loop. Building Regulations Part M requires hearing enhancement systems in new or altered buildings at reception points, performance areas and meeting spaces — marked with the standard blue "ear and T" symbol.
Feeding it
The loop driver wants a dedicated mono line-level feed of the primary programme audio — usually a matrix or aux send from the console — not a stray mic picking up the room. A room mic gives loop users the room's echo and noise along with the speech; a proper feed gives them exactly what everyone else is hearing, cleanly.
The standard
IEC 60118-4 sets the technical requirements — field strength, frequency response and signal-to-noise ratio — that a compliant loop has to meet. You're not expected to certify a system yourself, but knowing the standard exists is enough to recognise when a "we've got a loop" claim needs an actual test, not just a knowing nod.
Standard

If a venue tells you they have an induction loop, ask when it was last tested and who feeds it — a genuinely surprising number of installed loops are silently unplugged, mis-patched, or fed from the wrong source. If you're asked to feed one yourself, a dedicated send from the console, checked with a level that's neither buried nor distorted, is the whole job.

Go deeper

Ampetronic and Contacta both publish clear, freely available technical guides on loop systems aimed at installers rather than end users — genuinely useful if you end up dealing with these regularly. See §18.11.

6.12 Interpretation & conference systems

The conference-world cousin of the hearing loop — and the same pattern applies: audio techs very often end up owning it, again simply because it's the closest thing to "audio" on site, even though it's really its own small discipline.

What it is
Simultaneous interpretation lets a speaker be heard live in another language — an interpreter in a soundproofed booth listens on headphones and speaks the translation into a mic, which is transmitted to delegates on wireless IR or RF receivers with earpieces, channel-selectable if there's more than one target language.
Your part in it
Almost always just the feed — the interpreters need a clean, dedicated feed of the source language audio (the same discipline as a broadcast submix, §14.8), and their translated output needs to be received back into the system correctly if it's being mixed into a recording or a specific zone. You are not expected to manage the booths or receivers themselves — that's normally a specialist interpretation-equipment supplier's job.
What to check
Which channel is which language, that the interpreter feed is live and clean before delegates arrive, and — if it's your job to supply it — that the receiver count matches the expected audience, with spares. A silent interpretation channel five minutes into a conference is a much bigger problem than a silent audio channel, because there's no way for that portion of the audience to follow anything until it's fixed.
Standard

Treat the interpreter feed with the same care as a broadcast feed — test it properly before doors with the interpreters actually present, not just by yourself. They'll tell you immediately if the level or source is wrong, and they're the only people in the building who can.

6.13 Audio description

The theatre-world accessibility service that's easy to miss entirely if nobody's ever pointed it out to you — a describer narrates the visual action (movement, expressions, set changes) into the gaps between dialogue, for blind and partially-sighted audience members listening on a headset. In the UK, specialist providers like VocalEyes train describers and often run the service itself; the venue's job, and by extension yours, is mostly making sure the infrastructure works.

What you're actually asked for
A clean feed of the show mix to the describer's booth or position (so they can hear what the audience hears while they narrate), and a transmission system — usually the same IR or RF infrastructure as an interpretation system (§6.12) — carrying the describer's voice out to headset receivers in the audience.
Touch tours
Many venues offer a pre-show "touch tour" letting blind patrons handle set pieces, props and sometimes costumes to build a mental picture before the performance. Not really an audio job, but you'll sometimes be asked to support it with a portable PA or just a quiet, working comms channel.
What to check
Same discipline as the interpreter feed — test it live, with the describer present, before doors. A describer who can't hear the show clearly can't do their job at all.
Standard

6.14 Outdoor & weather-exposed work

Everything above assumes a roof. A genuinely large share of UK live audio doesn't have one — festivals, outdoor corporate, marquees with open sides — and weather causes its own specific category of problems worth knowing before you meet them for the first time.

IP ratings
The two-digit code on outdoor-rated boxes (IP54, IP55, IP65…) — first digit is protection against solid objects/dust, second is against water. IP54 (dust-protected, splash-resistant) is a common minimum for touring outdoor boxes; IP55 adds stronger jet protection; IP65 (fully dust-tight, low-pressure jet resistant) is typical for permanent outdoor install. A box with no IP rating at all almost certainly isn't meant to get rained on, whatever it looks like.
Electronics in the wet
Consoles, racks and anything with an unsealed connector are the actual risk, not the speakers — rain covers (fitted covers, gazebos, or simply a tarp rigged with a run-off slope so water doesn't pool) over FOH and any exposed patch bays are standard on any outdoor job, wired or not.
Wind
Rumbles and thumps on open mics, especially outdoor vocal or speech mics — a foam windshield helps a little, a proper wind muff ("dead cat") helps a lot more. Wind also physically moves line array elements and lightweight boxes on stands, which is a rigging and PUWER concern (§13.3) as much as an audio one.
Temperature
Cold weather drains batteries faster and makes wireless receivers slightly less predictable at the margins of range — carry more spares than you think you need on a cold outdoor day. Direct sun on a black console or amp rack for hours can push internal temperatures high enough to matter; shade it if you can.
Watch it

Never work on live electrical connections in the rain, and if lightning is genuinely close, that's a stop-the-show safety call for the Event Safety Officer or Production Manager, not something to quietly push through. Outdoor events should have a written severe weather plan — ask to see it if one job feels like it might need it.

Standard

Tickable version of all of the above at §19.14, the outdoor & weather-exposed job checklist.

6.15 The room is part of the system

Everything up to here has been about the boxes. But the audience never hears the boxes on their own — they hear the boxes plus the room, and in a difficult building the room is the louder partner. This is the bit that explains why the same PA can sound superb in one venue and like a swimming pool in the next, and why §6.8 insists you can't EQ a room.

Direct sound
What arrives straight from the loudspeaker, first. It carries the intelligibility — the consonants that let someone understand a word rather than just hear a noise.
Reflections
The same sound arriving later, having bounced off a wall, the ceiling or the floor. Early reflections (within roughly 30 ms) mostly reinforce the direct sound. Later ones smear it.
Reverberation
What's left once the reflections are too many and too close together to hear individually — a decaying wash. Measured as reverberation time (RT60): how long it takes to decay by 60 dB after the source stops.
Room modes / standing waves
At low frequencies, wavelengths are comparable to the room's dimensions, so the room resonates at particular frequencies. That's why bass can be overwhelming in one seat and almost missing three metres away, with nothing wrong with the system.
Absorption
Soft things — curtains, carpet, seating, and above all people — turn sound energy into a tiny amount of heat instead of reflecting it. This is why a room measured empty is not the room you'll be mixing in.

Rough expectations, so you know what you're walking into: a well-treated studio or a small meeting room might be a few tenths of a second of RT60; a decent theatre around a second; a big church, atrium or sports hall three seconds or more. Above roughly two seconds, speech intelligibility is genuinely hard to achieve and the system design has to fight for it.

Standard

You can't remove reverberation, so you avoid feeding it. That means directivity — using boxes whose pattern puts energy on people and not on walls and ceilings (§6.6), getting sources closer to listeners with fills or delays rather than turning the mains up, and choosing column arrays or tight-pattern boxes in reverberant rooms (§6.2). Turning it up in a live room makes the reverberation louder too, so it makes things worse, not better.

Watch it

An empty room and a full room are different acoustic spaces. A few hundred people are a large amount of absorption, mostly in the mid and high frequencies — so the room gets drier and slightly duller once doors open. A system tuned to sound perfect in an empty hall at 3pm will usually want a little more top end at 8pm. Don't panic-EQ during the first song; expect the change and leave yourself somewhere to go.

Also seen

You'll hear people talk about the critical distance — the point where the reverberant field is as loud as the direct sound. Closer than that and intelligibility is mostly fine; further away and it drops off fast however loud you go. It's a useful mental model for why a delay speaker halfway back fixes a problem that another 3 dB at the front never will.

Watch it

Acoustic treatment is not your call to make. Hanging drapes, moving absorbers or altering a listed building's surfaces belongs to the venue and the production designer. What you can reasonably do is point out that a hard, reflective room will limit what the system can achieve, early enough for someone to decide about drapes — and that conversation lands far better at the advance stage (§1.7) than at soundcheck.

Go deeper

Room acoustics is a full discipline and this is deliberately only the working tech's share of it. If it grabs you, it's the natural companion to system tuning (§6.8) — the Bob McCarthy book in §18.7 covers the interaction between systems and rooms properly, and modelling a familiar room in EASE Focus (§6.7) makes the effect visible rather than theoretical.

7.1 The channel strip — the unit of everything

Whatever the desk, whatever the decade, a channel does the same things in the same order. Learn this once and every console makes sense.

Pad
A fixed attenuator (usually −20 dB) for very hot sources, before the preamp.
High-pass filter (HPF)
Removes everything below a set frequency. Use it constantly — §4.3.
Gate
Mutes the channel when the signal drops below a threshold. Cleans up drum bleed and open mics.
EQ
Tonal shaping. Typically 4-band parametric on a decent desk.
Compressor
Reduces dynamic range — quiet bits up relative to loud bits. See §16.4.
Pan
Position in the stereo image.
Fader
Level into the main mix.
Aux send
A separate feed to somewhere else — a wedge, an IEM, a reverb, a recording. Pre-fade ignores the fader (monitors); post-fade follows it (effects).
PFL / AFL
Pre/After Fade Listen — soloing a channel to your headphones without affecting the mix.
Mute
Off. The most under-used control in live sound — anything not in use should be muted.
Standard

Pre-fade for monitors, post-fade for effects. Nearly universal, and the reasoning is simple: the band's wedge mix shouldn't change when FOH pulls a fader, but a vocal's reverb should get quieter when the vocal does.

7.3 Digital console concepts

Layers / banks
A desk with 16 physical faders and 64 channels shows you them 16 at a time. The fader you're moving is not necessarily the channel you think — always check the layer.
Channel vs socket
The physical input socket and the console channel are separate things, connected by a patch. Socket 12 does not have to be channel 12. This is powerful and it's also the single biggest source of confusion for people coming from analogue.
DCA / VCA
A master fader that controls a group of channels' faders remotely, without the audio passing through it. Perfect for "all drums", "all vocals".
Group / subgroup
An actual audio bus that channels are routed into, so you can process them together (e.g. compress the whole drum kit).
Matrix
A mixer of mixes — takes your main mix and other buses and creates additional outputs. Used for delay stacks, fills, broadcast feeds, recording feeds, the foyer.
Scene / snapshot / cue
A stored state of the console you can recall. Essential in theatre and corporate; used more sparingly in live music.
Safe / recall scope
Settings excluded from scene recall, so recalling a scene doesn't undo your gain changes. Learn this before you use scenes in anger.
Show file
The whole console state, saved to internal storage or USB.
Watch it

Loading a show file changes the output patch. If you load an unfamiliar show while the PA is on, you can send a full-range signal to an HF driver, unmute something into a live IEM, or dump 0 dB of pink noise into a full room. Habit: mute the outputs / turn the amps down before you load anything, then bring it back up and check. Every time.

Also seen

[2026] Some newer digital consoles and standalone products (Waves eMotion LV1 with its automixer, among others) offer AI-assisted automixing — automatically adjusting gain-sharing between open mics to reduce bleed and rider fatigue on multi-mic speech jobs. Genuinely useful on the right corporate/panel job with a competent operator supervising it. It is not yet, and shouldn't be treated as, a substitute for understanding gain structure and bleed control yourself — you still need to recognise when it's getting it wrong.

7.4 Show file discipline

  1. Save early, save often, save under a new name. Not show.scn repeatedly — ClientName_2026-08-25_v3.scn.
  2. Back up to USB at the end of every build and before any big change. Console internal storage fails.
  3. Take your own copy at the end of the job if it's yours to keep. Rebuilding a show from scratch because the file was on the hire desk that went out again is a bad afternoon.
  4. Start from a known base. Keep a clean template show for your common console — inputs named sensibly, HPFs in, standard routing, monitors on pre-fade. Loading that takes 30 seconds and saves 40 minutes.
  5. Name your channels. Not optional. An unnamed desk at line check is chaos, and if you get pulled away someone else has to be able to take over.
Standard

Naming convention that works: source first, position second. Vox Lead, Vox BV SL, Gtr Amp SR, Kick In, Laptop L. Keep it short enough to read in the console's tiny scribble strip.

Go deeper

On bigger or higher-stakes shows, the show file discipline above is only half the protection — the other half is console redundancy: a second, identical console mirroring the primary in real time (most modern digital desks support this natively) so a hardware failure on the main console is a seamless changeover rather than a dead PA. A cut-down version of the same idea, within reach on smaller shows, is a hot-spare — a second console loaded with the same show file, powered and patched, ready to swap to manually if the primary dies. Neither replaces the habit of saving and backing up; the show file is what a spare console actually runs.

7.5 Dynamics & EQ on the desk

Gate
Threshold (level to open), range/depth (how much it attenuates when closed), attack (how fast it opens), hold and release (how it closes). Used on toms and to control bleed.
Compressor
Threshold (where it starts working), ratio (how hard), attack (how fast it grabs), release (how fast it lets go), knee (how gradually it engages), makeup gain (to restore level).
Limiter
A compressor with a very high ratio, used to stop signals exceeding a ceiling. On outputs, protective. On IEMs, essential.
Parametric EQ (PEQ)
Choose frequency, gain and Q (bandwidth). Surgical. Your main tool.
Graphic EQ (GEQ)
Fixed bands, usually 31. Fast and visual. Traditional on monitors and system outputs.
Shelf
Boosts or cuts everything above (high shelf) or below (low shelf) a point.
RTA
Real Time Analyser — shows you the frequency content. Useful for identifying feedback frequencies fast. Not a substitute for ears.
Standard

Starting compressor settings for a live vocal: ratio around 3:1, medium attack, medium-fast release, threshold set so you're seeing 3–6 dB of gain reduction on the loudest phrases and none on the quiet ones. Then adjust by ear. Compression on live vocals is mostly about consistency, not effect.

7.6 Talkback, tone and solo

  • Talkback — a mic at the desk that can be routed to monitors, comms, or the stage. Check where yours is routed before you press it. Watch it Talkback accidentally routed to the main PA is a classic and very public mistake.
  • Oscillator / pink noise generator — built-in test signal. Invaluable for proving outputs. Sine wave for finding a specific driver, pink noise for general checks.
  • Solo modes — PFL (pre-fade, hear it regardless of fader position, standard for line checking), AFL (after fade), and Solo In Place (SIP), which mutes everything else in the actual mix.
Watch it

Solo In Place silences the PA for everyone. On desks where it's available, it should be disabled or locked during a show. If you press it by accident in front of an audience, everything except one channel vanishes. Know where that button is so you can avoid it.

8.2 Dante — the one to actually learn

Dante moves audio over standard networks. If you understand it, you're immediately useful on a huge range of jobs.

The three applications

Dante Controller
Free. The routing and management tool. You use it to see every Dante device on the network and connect outputs to inputs.
Dante Virtual Soundcard (DVS)
Paid, cheap. Turns a laptop into a Dante device — record multitrack, play back, run virtual soundcheck.
Dante Via
Paid. Routes any application's audio onto the network. Handy for corporate playback and conferencing.

Core concepts

Transmitter / Receiver
Dante calls outputs "transmit channels" and inputs "receive channels". A subscription is a connection between them, made in the grid in Dante Controller.
Device names
Rename everything meaningfully on day one — Console-Main, Stagebox-SL, not DLI-a1b2c3. Future you will be grateful.
Clock
One device is the leader. Dante mostly sorts this automatically, but you can and sometimes should set a preferred leader. A green tick in Clock Status is what you want.
Latency
Set per device, typically 0.25–5 ms. Higher settings tolerate more switches and worse networks. If you get dropouts, raising latency is a legitimate fix.
Sample rate
Every device must match. A device at 44.1 kHz will simply not connect to one at 48 kHz — it'll show as unavailable and you'll waste twenty minutes.
Device Lock
Password-protects a device's config so nobody can change your routing. Useful; also a great way to lock yourself out. Note the password.
Unicast vs multicast
Unicast sends a separate stream per receiver; multicast sends one stream to many. Use multicast when several devices need the same channels — it saves bandwidth.
Redundancy
Many Dante devices have primary and secondary ports for a fully duplicated network. Two physically separate switches, two cable runs. Standard on anything that matters.
Watch it

Never connect the Dante network to the venue's WiFi/office network or to a router handing out addresses, unless you know exactly what you're doing. Rogue DHCP servers, IGMP snooping misconfiguration, and general internet traffic will break audio in confusing ways. Audio networks are dedicated networks with dumb-but-configured switches, kept separate. Green cable for Dante, clearly labelled, is a habit worth adopting.

Dante isn't the only network audio ecosystem

It's the one to learn because it's by far the most common in hire stock, but you will meet the others, and it's worth knowing they're not just "other versions of Dante" — they're separate, generally non-interoperable ecosystems.

Milan (AVB)
An open standard built on the IEEE AVB/TSN networking layer, used natively by d&b, L-Acoustics and Meyer among others. It needs AVB-compatible switches (not just any network switch, unlike Dante) and doesn't talk to Dante directly. If you turn up to a d&b or L-Acoustics rig and Dante Controller can't see the PA, this is usually why — it's not broken, it's a different network.
AES67
Not a competing product but an interoperability standard — a common language that lets Dante, and other AES67-capable systems, exchange audio directly without a converter, most often seen at the boundary with broadcast infrastructure (§14.5). Recent Dante devices generally support AES67 mode alongside native Dante.
MADI
Older and simpler than either — a fixed 56 or 64 channels down one coax or fibre link, no network configuration at all. Still common as a straightforward point-to-point link between a console and a stagebox or recording rig, precisely because there's nothing to configure.
Standard

If a PA or console you're patching into "isn't showing up" in Dante Controller, check what network ecosystem it actually uses before assuming a fault — a Milan-native rig simply isn't going to appear there, and that's normal, not broken.

Go deeper

Do the free Dante Certification Level 1 today. It's a couple of hours online, it's free, and it's a recognised credential. Level 2 is also free and covers switch configuration and redundancy. Level 3 is classroom-based. §18.3.

8.3 Measurement software

Smaart (Rational Acoustics)
The industry standard for live system measurement. Paid, and the training is genuinely worth doing. Dual-channel FFT — compares what you sent with what you measured.
Open Sound Meter
Free and open source. Does transfer function measurement, RTA, spectrogram. Astonishingly good for the price. Start here.
Room EQ Wizard (REW)
Free. Aimed at rooms and studios but hugely capable. Excellent for learning what measurements mean.
SysTune, ARTA, EASERA
Other options in the same space.
Measurement mic
You need one — an omni condenser with a known flat response. Behringer ECM8000 is the cheap standard; Earthworks M23 and iSEMcon are the proper ones. An individually-calibrated mic with a cal file is much better than an uncalibrated cheap one.
Also seen

SPL meter apps on a phone are everywhere and they are indicative only — the phone mic is not calibrated, compresses at high levels, and rolls off the bass. Fine for "is this roughly 95 or roughly 105". Useless for anything you have to certify. If noise limits are contractual, use a proper Class 1 or Class 2 meter.

Go deeper

Reading a spectrogram — frequency up the side, time along the bottom, level as brightness — is a skill that pays off twice. It's how you spot a ringing frequency or a noise floor here, and it's exactly how wildlife recordings are analysed in bioacoustics, where the same plot is used to identify a species by the shape of its call. Same tool, same eye, different subject. See §18.15.

8.4 Playback

QLab (Mac)
The theatre and events standard for cue-based playback — audio, video, lighting cues, all triggered in sequence with a spacebar. Free to learn, paid for advanced features. Learn this if you touch theatre or corporate at all.
Show Cue System / Multiplay (Windows)
Windows alternatives to QLab. SCS is the serious one.
Ableton Live
Backing tracks, click, and playback for bands. Very common on tours — you'll meet a band with a laptop running Ableton and a multi-output interface.
Reaper
Cheap, extremely capable DAW. Popular for virtual soundcheck and multitrack recording of shows.
Pro Tools
Studio standard; used live where Avid consoles are involved.
Watch it

Streaming services are not a playback system. Spotify/Apple Music will drop out, play ads, lose connection, autoplay something unexpected, and normalise your levels. For anything that matters, use local files on a machine with notifications off, WiFi off, sleep off, and auto-updates off. And always have the files on a second device.

Standard

Any playback machine going to a show: notifications off, screen sleep off, auto-update off, volume at a fixed known level (usually 100% with level set downstream), and a physical copy of the files on a USB stick in your pocket.

8.5 Comms

Intercom is how the crew talks during a show, and on bigger jobs you'll be responsible for building it.

Wired party-line
Clear-Com and Tecpro — belt packs on a single cable loop, everyone hears everyone. Simple and bulletproof.
Digital matrix
Riedel, Clear-Com Eclipse. Big shows, routed channels, individual keys.
Wireless comms
Clear-Com FreeSpeak, Riedel Bolero, Green-Go. Full duplex, roaming.
Green-Go
Network-based (runs over Ethernet/PoE). Increasingly common and good value.
Unity Intercom
Runs on phones over WiFi. Cheap and cheerful; depends on the WiFi being good, which it never is.
Standard

Comms discipline: identify yourself and your target ("Sound to Stage"), wait for acknowledgement, be brief, and get off. And remember that everything you say on the ring is heard by everyone including the client's production team.

8.6 Networking you actually need

You don't need to be a network engineer. You do need these:

IP address
Four numbers, e.g. 192.168.1.50. Every device on a network needs a unique one.
Subnet mask
Usually 255.255.255.0. Devices can only talk directly to others in the same subnet — so 192.168.1.x devices talk to each other but not to 192.168.2.x.
Static vs DHCP
Static = you set the address manually. DHCP = a server hands them out. Audio networks are usually static or link-local so addresses don't change between days.
Link-local
169.254.x.x. What devices give themselves when there's no DHCP. Dante uses this happily by default.
Ping
The basic "can I reach it" test. ping 192.168.1.50 from a command prompt.
Switch
The box everything plugs into. Unmanaged = plug and play. Managed = configurable, needed for bigger Dante networks (QoS, IGMP snooping).
VLAN
Splitting one physical switch into separate logical networks. Used to keep audio, lighting and control apart on shared infrastructure.
OSC (Open Sound Control)
A lightweight, open messaging protocol sent over the same IP network as everything else — used to trigger cues and remote-control parameters between completely different systems (a lighting console firing a QLab cue, a show-control system nudging a mixer's scene, a tablet driving a console's channel faders). Not an audio-transport protocol like Dante — it carries commands and values, not sound. See §8.4 and §10.4 for QLab, and §9.8 for Titan, which can both send and receive it.
Standard

Keep a laptop with a known static IP config saved as a profile, plus a USB-to-Ethernet adapter in your bag. Half of "the network is broken" calls are actually "the laptop is on the wrong subnet". Free tools like Fing or Angry IP Scanner show you everything on the network in seconds and are worth having installed.

Watch it

A production audio network is not the internet, and it should stay that way. Never bridge a Dante or other audio switch into a venue's guest WiFi or public network — one careless patch cable turns your isolated, low-latency audio network into something anyone in the building can see and potentially interfere with. If a job genuinely needs internet access for something (a streaming feed, remote monitoring), keep it on a clearly separate, dedicated connection or VLAN rather than the same switch your audio lives on, and ask before plugging anything unfamiliar — a guest's laptop, an unknown access point — into a switch that's carrying your show.

Rentman
Very widely used in European/UK event hire. Equipment, crew, planning, quotes.

Cloud-based, big in the UK.
HireHop

inspHire
Broader plant/equipment hire, used by some AV companies.

It also means when you say "we haven't got four of those", people believe you.

PART 9

Working with other teams

Lighting, video and staging share your space, routes, power and comms. Clear boundaries and good handovers make every department better.

9.1 Why this gets its own part

Everything so far has been your chain: mic to speaker. But you build that chain in a room where two or three other departments are building theirs at the same time, often through the same doorway, up the same truss, and off the same distro. Most of what goes wrong between departments isn't technical — it's someone not knowing what someone else needed, or two cable runs fighting for the same route. The technical bits (this part covers three real ones: electrical interference, timecode, and DMX) are the minority of the problem. The majority is just talking to people early.

Standard

On any multi-department build, find out in the first hour: who's the LX/video/staging equivalent of your crew chief, where their cable is planning to run, and whether anyone's sharing your power. Five minutes of that conversation saves an afternoon of re-routing.

9.2 Lighting — noise, truss and real estate

You'll spend more time coordinating with LX than any other department, because you're fighting over the same physical space: the same truss to hang things from, the same cable tray or floor route, often the same power.

Dimmer noise
Old-style dimmers chop the AC waveform to control brightness, and that chopping radiates as buzz. It couples into anything running near it — which is the practical reason (alongside sharing a hum-prone supply, §3.8) that audio and lighting cable shouldn't be run in the same tray for long distances. LED fixtures with switch-mode drivers can do the same thing at a higher, whinier frequency.
Cable routing
Where you can't avoid a crossing, cross lighting and audio cable at right angles, not parallel — a 90° crossing picks up almost nothing, a long parallel run picks up plenty. Where you're both running the same direction for any distance, ask for separate trunking or at least separate sides of the tray.
Truss and rigging points
Delay speakers, hanging mics and comms often go on LX truss, not your own. That's a rigging conversation with whoever's calling the motors — know your box's weight and always confirm before you hang anything on someone else's point.
Comms
On bigger shows LX will have their own channel — know how to reach it. "Sound to LX" gets used constantly during a get-in, mostly to ask who's got the last free spot on the truss.
Watch it

If a wireless mic or IEM system starts buzzing or dropping the moment the lighting rig is powered up and tested, don't assume it's coincidence — it usually isn't. Report it early rather than fighting it all afternoon; a moved cable run or a different dimmer curve often fixes it in minutes once someone knows to look.

9.3 Video & AV

Video is the department you'll most often be feeding audio to, or taking it from.

Timecode
Where playback needs to stay locked to a video track — a corporate film, a themed show, a broadcast — video usually generates LTC (linear timecode, an audio-rate signal you can literally patch down an XLR) or sends MTC (MIDI timecode) to your playback system. Your job is normally just to receive and chase it, not generate it.
Embedded audio
Broadcast and streaming feeds often carry audio embedded inside the video signal (SDI) rather than as a separate cable. If a broadcast truck is involved, expect to be handing them a clean mix to embed, on their schedule, not yours.
Playback content with its own audio
VT, adverts and video content usually arrive with a soundtrack that needs to land on a specific console channel, at a level nobody's checked. Ask for it in advance and gain-stage it like anything else — video editors mix for headphones, not a PA.
Screens as a noise source
LED walls and their processing gear can be electrically noisy in the same way dimmers are. If you're running mic cable near a screen wall, treat it the same as a lighting run.
Standard

Get any video content with embedded audio onto your system and through a level check before doors, the same as you would a band. "It'll be fine, it's just a video" is how a screaming-loud advert happens live.

9.4 Staging, rigging & backline

The staging and rigging crew build the physical platform everything else sits on, and backline is the world of the instruments themselves — often the band's own tech, sometimes yours to provide and mic.

Backline
Guitar and bass amps, drum kits, keyboard rigs. If it's your job to mic it, that's Part 4 territory (§4.5 has placement); if it's the band's own tech's job, your role is patching their DIs and mic feeds into your system and agreeing stage volume with them — loud backline is one of the most common reasons a mix fights itself at FOH.
Risers & decking
Drum risers in particular need cable routed through them, not just to them — plan that before the deck goes down, because pulling a riser apart mid-build to feed a cable through is a bad afternoon.
Rigging calls
If any of your kit is flying (delay speakers, hung mics, truss-mounted anything), you'll be in the room for "hands in the air" — everyone confirms clear before a motor moves. Never wander off during a lift, and never assume someone else has checked your gear is clear.
Barrier & FOH position
Staging or event crew usually build your FOH riser and the barrier in front of the stage. If your desk position, sightline or cable route isn't what you were expecting, raise it as soon as you see the plan, not once it's built.

9.5 Shared infrastructure — routes, trays and colour

On anything bigger than a small corporate, several departments' cable ends up sharing the same physical path: a cable ramp across a doorway, a tray up a wall, a route through a riser. Getting this right is mostly etiquette plus a bit of planning.

  • Plan routes together at the start, not cable-by-cable as each department gets to it. Whoever's coordinating the build should know roughly where power, audio, lighting and video each want to go before the first cable hits the floor.
  • Colour-code where you can. Many companies use coloured tape or coloured cable for different disciplines — green for Dante/network, a specific tape colour for audio versus lighting DMX versus power. Learn your company's convention on day one; it saves enormous confusion at derig when three departments are coiling cable off the same tray.
  • Label at both ends. A cable that's clearly yours saves someone else's time as much as it saves your own.
  • Don't block a shared route with your own kit — a flightcase left across a tie-line ramp becomes everyone's problem, not just yours.
Also seen

On a small job this whole section is one conversation over a cup of tea before the get-in. It's only on multi-day, multi-department builds that formal cable route plans and colour conventions really earn their keep — but the habit is worth building early regardless of scale.

9.6 Power beyond your own rig

You'll usually only be responsible for your own audio distro, but it helps to recognise what's happening around it.

Generators
Off-grid events run on generators rather than mains. Generator power sags under sudden load — a big lighting cue hitting at the same moment as a bass-heavy song can occasionally cause an audible dip. It's not something you fix; it's something worth recognising so you don't chase a phantom console fault.
Load balancing
Three-phase power needs roughly equal load across all three legs, or one phase trips while the others sit comfortably under capacity. This is normally the power department's job to manage, but if you're asked which phase your rig is on, know the answer.
Separate audio supply
As covered in §3.8, the standard ask is a power feed dedicated to audio, ideally off its own phase, so lighting and motor loads aren't sharing your earth reference. Ask for this at the advance stage, not on the day — by the time you're on site the distro's usually already built.

9.7 DMX — the protocol you'll keep meeting

You don't need to operate lighting, but DMX turns up often enough at the edges of your world — shared connectors, shared trunking, the occasional "can you just patch this through your snake" — that it's worth knowing what it actually is.

What it is
DMX512 is a one-way digital control protocol carrying up to 512 channels of control data (dimmer levels, moving-light positions, colour, whatever) down one line, refreshed dozens of times a second. It's control data, not audio — there is nothing to listen to.
The cable
Electrically it's RS-485, a balanced differential signal, run on cable rated at 110 Ω. That's the number to remember: it's why DMX cable and mic cable, though both often on 3-pin XLR, aren't really interchangeable for anything but the shortest, least critical run (§3.1).
The connector confusion
The DMX spec calls for 5-pin XLR; a huge amount of fixture-level gear ships with 3-pin instead, because it's cheaper and 3-pin XLR is everywhere. You'll meet turnaround adaptors (5-pin to 3-pin) constantly. It's not your fault when it doesn't just work — it's a known mess in the industry.
Daisy chain & termination
DMX devices link in a chain, in to out, not a star like a Dante network. The last fixture in the chain needs a 120 Ω terminator (a resistor across the data pins) or the signal reflects back down the line and things flicker or misbehave, especially over longer runs.
Universes
One DMX line carries one "universe" of 512 channels. A big rig with hundreds of fixtures needs several universes, usually distributed over the lighting network (often Art-Net or sACN over standard Ethernet) rather than run as physical DMX the whole way.
Watch it

Never patch a DMX line down a spare pair in an audio multicore as a favour without checking with whoever owns the snake. Even where it technically works over a short run, the impedance mismatch (§3.1) can cause exactly the kind of intermittent flickering that's miserable to fault-find later, and it's now tangled up with your audio cable when someone needs to trace it.

Go deeper

You don't need to learn to programme a lighting desk, but understanding DMX addressing and universes well enough to have an intelligent conversation with an LX tech is a genuinely useful hour of reading. See Part 18.

9.8 Avolites Titan — what it actually is

If you've got a Titan licence, this is worth a proper section rather than a passing mention, because it's a real, marketable skill on top of your audio work, not just a curiosity.

Titan is Avolites' lighting control software — it's not audio kit, and it's worth being precise about that distinction even though this is an audio handbook: it's the operating software that runs on Avolites' own consoles (Tiger Touch, Arena, Sapphire, Quartz and others), and it also runs as PC software so you can build and rehearse a whole show without a physical console in front of you.

Titan PC Suite
Free software from Avolites that puts the full Titan environment on a normal Windows PC — patching, programming, the works.
Titan Simulator
Part of the same suite — shows an on-screen virtual version of a specific console's physical control surface, so what you learn on your laptop maps directly onto the real hardware later.
AvoKey
A USB licence dongle. This is almost certainly the "licence" you already have — it's what lets Titan PC Suite and the Simulator run at full capability rather than in a limited demo mode.

Why this is worth your time: on any job with LX, being the audio tech who can also patch a fixture, understand what "the desk's not seeing that dimmer" actually means, or sensibly troubleshoot a DMX chain (§9.7) makes you noticeably more useful than someone who only knows their own department. It won't make you a lighting designer, but it removes the "not my department" ceiling on a lot of small, useful jobs.

9.9 Getting started with Titan PC Suite

  1. Download Titan PC Suite from Avolites' own site — search "Avolites Titan PC Suite download" and get it from avolites.com directly rather than a third-party mirror.
  2. Plug in your AvoKey before or during install — the software checks for it to unlock full functionality.
  3. Pick a console to simulate. If your company uses a specific Avolites desk, match that model in the Simulator so what you learn transfers directly. If not, any mid-range console (Tiger Touch or similar) is a sensible default to learn the concepts on.
  4. Start with a small, fake rig — patch four or five generic fixtures rather than trying to recreate a whole show. The goal early on is fluency with the workflow, not a finished show file.
  5. Work through §9.10 below in order, actually doing each step in the software rather than just reading it. This stuff is genuinely hands-based knowledge — reading about patching without patching anything barely sticks.
Standard

Treat this exactly like the console offline editors in §8.1 — a few real evenings of deliberate practice on a fake show file will put you ahead of most people who only ever touch a lighting desk live, under pressure, with no time to explore.

9.10 Titan's core concepts

Five ideas that everything else in Titan is built from. Learn these properly and the rest of the software makes sense; skip them and every screen looks like an unrelated wall of buttons.

Patching & fixture personalities
Before Titan can control a fixture, it needs to know what that fixture is — how many DMX channels it uses and what each one does. That definition is called a fixture personality, and Titan ships with a library of them for real fixtures, organised by manufacturer, model and mode (many fixtures can run in different modes using different numbers of channels).
The Programmer
A temporary workspace, not a permanent one. When you select a fixture and change something — intensity, colour, position — that change lives in the Programmer until you either record it into a cue or press Clear to wipe it. Selecting a new fixture after making changes deselects the old one, but the changes you made stay in the Programmer until cleared. This trips up every beginner at least once: forgetting the Programmer is holding leftover values from an earlier fixture, and recording them into a cue by accident.
Palettes
Saved, reusable settings — colour palettes, position palettes, and beam/gobo palettes are the three main types. Critically, a cue doesn't store a copy of the palette's values — it stores a reference to the palette. Change the palette later and every cue using it updates automatically. This is exactly why touring lighting rigs can be rebuilt fast in a new venue: reset the position palettes for the new room, and every cue that uses them follows along.
Cues & cue lists
A cue is one recorded look — a snapshot of fixture states, which can include fade-in and fade-out times and even movement. A cue list is a sequence of cues triggered in order, usually from a "Go" button on a playback fader — the lighting equivalent of your show file's scene recall (§7.4), stepping through a show moment by moment.
Grand Master & playback faders
The Grand Master is a single fader controlling the overall intensity of everything the console is outputting — pull it down and the whole rig dims proportionally, regardless of what any individual cue says. It's often disabled by default on a new show specifically so a beginner doesn't get confused wondering why nothing's appearing on stage. Individual playback faders each run a cue list or a look, with their own Flash (momentarily add to the output) and Swop (solo this playback, suppress the others) buttons alongside them.

How to patch your first fixture

  1. Open the Patch menu.
  2. Choose the fixture's manufacturer and model from the personality library, and the correct mode for how it's set on the fixture itself.
  3. Set the DMX line and start address — or let Titan auto-assign a sensible one if you don't need a specific address.
  4. Repeat for each fixture, or patch several identical fixtures at once rather than one at a time.
Watch it

The single most common beginner mistake in Titan is exactly the audio equivalent of forgetting what's in a channel's processing (§7.4): leftover values sitting in the Programmer from a fixture you're no longer thinking about, which then get baked into the next cue you record. Get into the habit of pressing Clear before you start a new piece of programming, the same way you'd check for solo before trusting a mix (§7.7).

9.11 Where this actually helps you on an audio job

  • DMX fault-finding stops being a mystery — you'll recognise a patching problem, an addressing clash, or a termination issue rather than just reporting "the lights have stopped" (§9.7).
  • You become a useful extra pair of hands during an LX build or a quick fixture swap, which is exactly the kind of cross-department usefulness §9.1 talks about.
  • It's a genuine second specialism. Plenty of small companies and venues want one person who can cover both audio and basic lighting on quieter jobs — that combination is rarer and more valuable than being deep in audio alone.
Go deeper

Avolites' own manual (manual.avolites.com) is thorough, free, and kept current with each Titan version — it's the authoritative source once you're past these basics, including exact button references for whichever console you end up on. See Part 18.

9.12 Special effects — pyro, confetti & haze

Not your department, but its effects land on your gear and its cues sit in your running order, so it's worth knowing the boundaries.

Who fires it
Always a licensed pyrotechnician or a specifically trained operator — never you, and never anyone without the qualification, whatever the time pressure. Confetti and CO2 cannons carry their own competence and risk-assessment requirements too, even though they're not explosive.
Protecting your kit
Confetti and streamer residue gets into everything within its throw radius — open mic capsules, fader mechanisms, DI box connectors. Cover or relocate anything you can before a cannon fires nearby, and budget time afterwards to actually clean gear rather than packing it away dirty.
Cueing
Pyro and confetti cues are usually fired from a dedicated, separate firing system, not your console — but the cue to fire it often comes from the same show-control chain as your own cues (§8.4), so know where in the running order it happens even though you're not the one pressing the button.
Watch it

Never agree to fire, rig, or "just hold" a pyrotechnic device, however informally you're asked, and however confident the person asking sounds. It's licensed, regulated work with its own legal competence requirements — exactly the same boundary as §13.2 draws around electrical work.

PART 10

Types of event & what changes

Corporate, touring, theatre and broadcast use the same fundamentals but reward different priorities and habits.

10.1 Why context changes everything

Everything in Parts 2–9 is true everywhere — signal flow doesn't care what kind of gig you're on. But what good looks like, how formal the site is, how much you're expected to improvise versus follow a document exactly, and even how you dress and speak on the radio, shifts a lot between contexts. Turning up to a black-tie AGM with festival-stage energy reads as unprofessional; turning up to a punk show in a suit reads as clueless. This part is a quick map of the main worlds so you know what to expect walking in.

10.2 Corporate AV

Conferences, AGMs, product launches, award dinners. High client visibility, low tolerance for anything visibly going wrong, and the audience is usually not there for the sound — it just needs to be invisible and correct.

Priorities
Speech intelligibility above everything. A slightly boring but flawless mix beats an exciting one with a single dropout.
Typical sources
Lecterns, handheld/lav radio mics on presenters, laptop playback for VT and slides, panel discussions on multiple mics at once.
What's different
Tight rehearsal time, strict running order, a producer or stage manager calling cues, and often a client watching you directly. Presentation and calm matter as much as technical skill.
Common pitfalls
Presenters who wander off-axis from a lav mic, laptop audio nobody checked, and multiple panellists on live mics causing feedback or comb filtering if gains aren't managed.
Standard

Get every presenter mic'd and sound-checked before doors, including a moment of them actually talking at a normal volume — not just "check check". Nerves make people speak differently on the day than in rehearsal.

Go deeper

A growing share of corporate work has a remote-participant element built in from the start — see §10.8 for hybrid meetings and video conferencing specifically.

10.3 Live music & touring

Bands, festivals, club shows. The audience is there specifically for the sound and the performance — expectations for impact and quality are high, and so is the pace.

Priorities
Energy, impact, and a mix that serves the music. Perfect isn't always the goal — the right amount of excitement is.
Typical sources
Full band inputs — drums, bass, guitars, backline, multiple vocals — often changing completely between acts on a multi-band bill.
What's different
Fast changeovers (sometimes minutes between acts), visiting engineers who may want to run their own desk or bring their own show file, and a much more physical, high-tempo build.
Common pitfalls
Under-running changeover time, not having a solid house patch that visiting engineers can drop into quickly, and stage volume from backline overwhelming the FOH mix.
Also seen

On touring shows with their own engineer and system tech, your job shifts to advancing, translating local knowledge (venue quirks, power, load-in access) and supporting their show rather than mixing it yourself. Being genuinely useful in that support role gets you rebooked by touring companies just as much as mixing skill does.

10.4 Theatre & musicals

The most heavily documented, cue-based world you'll work in. Everything happens on a call, from a defined script, at a defined moment — audio design here is closer to a technical performance in itself than a live mix.

Priorities
Precision and repeatability. The show must sound the same on night 40 as night 1.
Typical sources
Radio mics on performers (often a lot of them, with fast changeovers backstage), playback/sound effects cues, orchestra or band in a pit.
What's different
Everything is cued — the Stage Manager calls "SQ42, go" and a sequence fires. A show file with every scene, every cue, painstakingly built and rehearsed. Radio mic changeovers between actors happen in the wings in seconds, silently, in the dark.
Common pitfalls
Missing a cue, a radio mic pack not swapped in time for the next scene, wig/costume interference with a lav mic, and — as covered in §1.4 — anything audible once doors are open and the house is dark.
Go deeper

If you want to move into theatre sound specifically, learning a cueing system like QLab or a theatre-standard console workflow, and understanding how a Deputy Stage Manager calls a show, is time very well spent. See §18.13.

10.5 Broadcast & streaming

The audience isn't in the room — they're watching a screen, and the room's acoustics are irrelevant to what they hear. Different rules apply.

Priorities
A clean, consistent, correctly-leveled feed for the broadcast mix — not necessarily what sounds best in the room. Broadcast engineers often want a drier, more controlled sound than a PA mix.
Typical sources
The same inputs as the live show, split or fed in parallel to a broadcast desk via a splitter or a Dante/AES67 feed, plus your own comms tying into theirs.
What's different
Loudness standards (broadcasters commonly work to defined LUFS targets rather than dBFS peak), strict timing, and a whole extra layer of people — a broadcast sound supervisor — who need a split of your signal, usually via an isolated multicore split or digitally via §9.3.
Common pitfalls
Ground/earth issues introduced by an improperly isolated broadcast split, level mismatches between what sounds right in-room and what the broadcast desk wants, and forgetting the broadcast feed needs to keep working even if the PA doesn't.
Also seen

A proper broadcast split uses an isolation transformer or a digitally isolated feed so a fault on the broadcast side (or a dodgy earth on their truck) can never affect your system, and vice versa. Never just parallel-wire an unbalanced feed off your desk for a broadcaster without isolating it.

10.6 Conferences, houses of worship & permanent install

A smaller but common world: spaces where the system is already built and mostly stays the same week to week — a church, a conference centre, a council chamber. You're not building a rig from scratch; you're operating and maintaining one.

  • Install audio is judged on reliability and consistency over years, not peak performance on one night. Ceiling speakers, wall-mounted DSPs, and systems tuned once and left alone are the norm.
  • You'll meet DSP processors (BSS, Biamp, QSC Q-SYS) doing the job a console does elsewhere — routing, EQ, automatic mixing for multiple lecterns/panel mics.
  • Expect less troubleshooting-on-the-fly freedom — changes often need sign-off, because the system has to keep working correctly for the next hundred users who won't have you there to fix it.

10.7 Reading the room

None of this is about being a different person — it's about matching your energy, formality and communication style to what the job actually needs. A few tells that help you calibrate fast on arrival:

When in doubt, match the most experienced person in the room in the first hour rather than guessing. It's the fastest way to calibrate, and nobody minds being quietly copied.

10.8 Hybrid meetings & video conferencing

Increasingly the default expectation on corporate work (§10.2) rather than a special request — some or all of the audience is joining remotely, and the room audio has to work both ways: the room needs to hear the remote participants, and the remote participants need to hear the room cleanly.

Teams Rooms / Zoom Rooms
Purpose-built hardware and software combinations (a small PC or appliance, a camera, a DSP-based audio bar or a ceiling mic array) certified to run Microsoft Teams or Zoom natively in a room. Increasingly what you'll find already installed in a client's own conference rooms rather than being asked to build from scratch.
USB audio
The most common way your own desk or a room's DSP hands off to a laptop running the call — a USB audio interface or a room's built-in USB output appears to the laptop as a standard microphone and speaker, no drivers or special software needed on the laptop end.
Acoustic echo cancellation (AEC)
The processing that stops the remote participants hearing their own voice come back a moment later through the room's speakers and back into the room mic. Built into proper conferencing DSPs and soundbars; a bare laptop mic and a room PA are a recipe for exactly this problem.
What's actually yours to check
Confirm the remote feed can actually hear the room at a sensible level (not just that the room can hear them), confirm there's no echo or feedback loop before the call starts, and know where the mute button is for both directions. A silent remote participant nobody can hear is a very common, very avoidable failure.
Watch it

Never feed a live room mic straight into a laptop's built-in mic input alongside PA playback without checking for echo first — it's the single most common cause of a hybrid meeting becoming unusable, and it's completely preventable with five minutes of testing before anyone's watching.

10.9 Private parties, weddings & smaller functions

A very common way into paid audio work, and a genuinely different context from anything else in this part: no producer, no stage manager, and often nobody on site who's ever worked with a sound crew before.

Priorities
Reliability and reading the room over technical ambition. A background music level that creeps up through the evening without anyone checking is a far more common complaint than anything technically wrong.
Typical sources
A speech mic or two (best man, father of the bride), background/DJ playback, sometimes a small live act for a set. Often your own compact PA and desk rather than a big hired system.
What's different
You're frequently the only production person on site, dealing directly with the client or a wedding/event planner rather than a crew chief — decisions that would normally go up a chain land on you directly. There's usually no RAMS culture, no formal running order, and timings move constantly as the event runs long or short.
Common pitfalls
A curfew that's genuinely a licensing condition of a residential venue, not just a courtesy (§1.5) — going over it can cost the client their licence for future events, not just annoy a neighbour. Also: an unbriefed guest asking to "just say a few words" on a live mic, and background music left running unattended and creeping louder.
Standard

Agree who has authority to change the plan — timings, volume, what's played — before the event starts, and get it in writing if you can (an email is enough). Without a producer in the room, "the client" can mean four different family members with four different opinions by 9pm.

PART 11

Test before doors

System check, line check and sign-off: the sequence that decides whether the show is calm or a scramble.

11.1 System check vs line check — the difference

System check
Proving the PA itself works — every box making sound, correct polarity, sensible level, no buzzes or rattles. Usually the systems tech's job, done before anyone's plugged an input in. Covered from the speaker end in §6.8.
Line check
Proving every input — every mic, DI and line source — arrives cleanly at the console, on the correct channel, with sensible gain. This is very often your job, and it's what this part is mostly about.

Do the system check first. There's no point proving forty inputs sound correct through a PA you haven't confirmed is working.

11.2 Before you start: setting up channels, from equipment to patch

A line check only tells you the truth about what's already built. Before §11.3 makes any sense, every channel needs to actually exist as a patched, gained, named path from source to console. Do this properly and the line check becomes a formality; skip steps here and the line check turns into fault-finding with an audience.

  1. Get the input list and patch list (§1.5) and actually read them before you touch a cable — you need to know what's supposed to be where, on which socket and which channel, before you can build it correctly the first time.
  2. Position and cable every source against the stage plan — mic or DI at the stand, cable run tidy, taped down or out of the walkway, with enough slack to survive a mic being nudged (§1.5).
  3. Land each cable on the correct stage box socket — the one the patch list says, not the next free hole. A source on the wrong socket is invisible until someone specifically goes looking for it.
  4. Confirm the run to the console is actually up — an analogue multicore is either connected or it isn't, but a digital stage box over a network needs to actually be showing in the console's device list before you patch anything to it. Don't assume; check (§7.3; §8.2 covers clocking on a Dante network).
  5. Patch each socket to its console channel to match the patch list. Socket 12 does not have to be channel 12 — that's the entire point of a patch (§7.3) — but if you deviate from the paperwork for a good reason, say so out loud and update the sheet, or the next person to look at it will trust a patch list that's now wrong.
  6. Set phantom power only where the input list says it's needed — condenser mics and some DIs need it, dynamic mics and ribbons generally don't want it (§2.6).
  7. Set an initial gain on every channel — this doesn't need to be perfect yet, just safe: enough that you can hear it, nowhere near clipping. Proper gain structure is §2.5; the fast version is bring it up while the source makes a normal-level sound, and stop once the meter's comfortably in the green.
  8. Name every channel as you patch it, not afterwards — an unnamed desk at line check is chaos, and colour-coding by section (drums, vocals, band, playback) if your desk supports it makes the whole show easier to read at a glance (§7.4).
  9. Do one rough pass for "is anything obviously dead" as you finish each area — not a full line check, just enough to catch a dead cable or a mispatch while you're still standing next to it with spares in reach, rather than discovering it later with everyone waiting.
  10. Save the show file before you move on to formal testing (§7.4) — a build's worth of patching, gain and naming is exactly the kind of work you don't want to redo from a crash.
Standard

Build in batches, not one channel start-to-finish at a time: cable every source first, then patch the whole stage box, then gain and name the whole desk. It's faster, and problems tend to cluster by area — one bad multicore pair, one dodgy DI box — which is much easier to spot when you're looking at ten channels at once instead of one.

Watch it

The rough "is it alive" pass above is not the line check. It's tempting to treat a channel that clearly made a sound as done — but wrong polarity, bleed from the wrong mic, or a gain that's technically working but badly set all sound "fine" on a rough pass and only get caught by properly working through §11.3.

Once every channel is patched, gained and named, you're ready to test properly:

  • Agree a method with whoever's helping — either you're at the stage end and someone's on the desk confirming, or you're doing both yourself with talkback into your own ears on a smaller job.
  • Have a line check sheet ready, on paper or the one in Part 19, to tick off as you go — trying to hold forty channels in your head is how things get missed.

Then move to §11.3 and prove every channel, one at a time, in patch order.

11.3 Running a line check, start to finish

  1. Work in patch order, not the order that's convenient. Starting at channel 1 and moving up means nothing gets skipped and nothing gets checked twice.
  2. Announce the channel before you make noise on it. "Checking one, kick in" over talkback, or a hand signal in a no-talkback situation, so the person at the desk knows what they're listening for.
  3. Make a real sound, not a tap. Speak or sing into a vocal mic at a normal working level; tap a drum properly, don't just brush the mic capsule with a finger — a tap tells you the mic is connected, not that it sounds right or is positioned correctly.
  4. Confirm from the desk end — signal present, correct channel, sensible gain (not clipping, not buried), and that it's actually the source you expect and not bleed from something else.
  5. Check polarity where it matters — kick in/out, snare top/bottom, DI vs mic on the same source (§2.8).
  6. Tick it off and move to the next one. Don't trust memory over forty-plus channels.
  7. Re-check anything that was touched after — a mic repositioned, a cable re-routed, a connector reseated. If it moved, it needs proving again.
Watch it

A channel that was fine an hour ago isn't guaranteed to still be fine now — cables get kicked, connectors get knocked during other work, gain gets bumped by an elbow. A line check the moment before doors, even a quick one, catches the handful of things that changed since the real check.

11.4 Line checking without talkback

Not every job has comms between stage and desk. In theatre especially, or on a small corporate with no radios to spare, you'll line check with hand signals and eye contact instead.

  • Agree the signal set before you start: thumbs up for "good", flat hand rocking for "borderline", a cut-throat gesture for "nothing there".
  • Work slowly enough that the desk end can actually see and respond — rushing this defeats the point.
  • If you genuinely can't get eye contact or a clear sightline, a phone call or a runner beats guessing.

11.5 Quarantining faulty kit

Something will fail a line check. What you do next matters as much as catching it.

  1. Swap it immediately for a spare rather than trying to fault-find mid-build — that's what Part 12 is for, later, with time to actually think.
  2. Physically separate the faulty item from the good stock — a different case, a labelled box, anything that stops it accidentally going back out.
  3. Tag it: what's wrong, who found it, when. "Noisy, crackles when cable's wiggled, found at line check 14/03" is worth more than a bare "faulty" sticker.
  4. Log it in whatever system your company uses (§8.7) so it doesn't quietly reappear on a shelf next week.
Watch it

This is the practical version of the warning in §1.2: kit that goes back on the shelf "probably fine" becomes someone else's emergency later. Quarantine anything you're not sure about — the five extra minutes now is cheaper than a dead channel on someone else's show.

11.6 Wireless proving

Wireless systems get their own pass within the line check — not just "does it make sound" but "is it clean, and will it stay clean". Walk every performer's likely path on stage while checking their pack, watch the RF meter for dropouts, and confirm IEM mixes are actually reaching the performer at a sensible level. Full detail in §5.8.

11.7 Signing it off

"Done" means every input has been individually proved, not that you pressed play on a song and it generally sounded fine. A line check that skips inputs because "that one's always fine" is the one that bites you — the input that's always fine is exactly the one nobody double-checks when it stops being fine.

Standard

Tell whoever's running the show explicitly when line check is complete, and flag anything still outstanding by name — "everything's good except channel 22, we're waiting on a replacement DI" — rather than a vague "mostly done".

11.8 Comms check

Line check proves your audio inputs. Before doors, prove the crew's own communication path too — it's easy to skip because it doesn't make a sound out front, but a dead comms channel mid-show is its own kind of emergency.

  • Every beltpack or headset that'll be used tonight actually powers on, holds charge, and is on the correct channel or group (§8.5 for the intercom hardware itself).
  • Everyone who needs to talk to everyone else actually can — stage, FOH, monitor world, backstage — not just that the desk end works.
  • Spare batteries or a spare beltpack are actually in reach, not back in a case in the van.
Also seen

On a small job this is one sentence: "can you hear me, over" into a single radio. On a bigger multi-department show with a wired party-line it's worth its own five minutes before doors, the same way the PA gets a system check before the line check runs.

PART 12

Diagnose & fix faults

One calm method beats twenty guesses. Follow the route, know your boundaries, and escalate with useful information.

12.1 The method

Back in §2.1: every fault is the same question — where along the chain did the signal stop, or start sounding wrong? The method is divide and conquer: pick a point roughly in the middle of the suspect chain, check if the signal is correct there, and that tells you which half the fault is in. Repeat inside that half. Each check should eliminate roughly half of what's left.

Mic → Cable → Stagebox → Snake/network → Console input → Processing → Output patch → Amp → Speaker ↑ "Is it good HERE?" — splits the chain in two, repeat inside the faulty half
Standard

Say what you're testing and why, even just to yourself: "if I get signal at the stagebox, the fault's in the console or beyond; if I don't, it's the mic or the cable." Fault-finding out loud, even alone, keeps you from randomly retrying the same thing.

12.2 The universal fault-finding kit

Half of fault-finding is having the right five things in your pocket so you can test without walking back to the truck.

Known-good cable
A short XLR you trust completely. Swap it in — if the fault follows the cable, it was the cable.
Known-good mic or DI
Same logic at the source end.
Headphones
To listen at intermediate points in the chain without needing the PA or a whole channel of the desk.
Multimeter
Continuity testing on a cable in seconds — beats guessing by ear every time. §12.4.
Torch, small screwdriver set, gaffer tape, spare fuses, cable ties
The unglamorous stuff that solves 80% of physical faults. Tickable version of the whole personal bag at §19.17.

12.3 Ground loops & hum — the practical fix

Referenced from §3.8: hum, usually at mains frequency (50 Hz) or a multiple of it, most often caused by two pieces of equipment referencing mains earth at two physically different points, creating a loop that picks up interference.

  1. Confirm it's actually a ground loop — it's typically a low, steady hum that changes when you touch a chassis or connect/disconnect another earthed device, not a hiss or crackle.
  2. Get everything onto one power source where possible (§3.8) — often fixes it outright.
  3. If that's not possible, use a DI box's ground lift switch on the offending line — it disconnects pin 1 from the DI's output side while keeping the safety earth on the mains plug fully intact. This is the correct, safe way to break a loop.
  4. For a more permanent or larger-scale fix, use an isolation transformer in the signal path, which breaks the electrical connection between two pieces of gear while still passing audio.
Watch it

The ground lift on a DI box lifts the audio shield, not the mains safety earth — the equipment's plug is still properly earthed for safety at all times. This is completely different from pulling the earth pin out of a mains plug, which is never acceptable (§3.8) and is actually dangerous rather than a fix.

12.4 Substitution & isolation

The two techniques that solve almost everything:

Substitution
Swap the suspect part for one you know is good. If the fault goes away, the swapped part was faulty (or was fine, and something downstream changed — check both).
Isolation
Remove everything from the chain except the minimum needed to test one link — a mic straight into a known-good channel on a different console, bypassing everything else, to prove the mic itself works.

A multimeter earns its place here: continuity mode (usually a beep) confirms a cable's cores are connected end to end and not shorted to each other or the shield — the fastest way to clear or confirm a suspect cable without needing a whole signal chain to test it in.

12.5 Intermittent faults

The hardest category, because the thing you're trying to catch isn't there when you look at it.

  • Reproduce it deliberately. Wiggle the cable at each connector, one at a time, while listening — a fault that appears when you flex a connector specifically is almost always a dry joint or a damaged core right there.
  • Note the pattern. Only when someone moves? Only when it's warm? Only on that one channel regardless of what's plugged in? Patterns point at causes.
  • Don't declare it fixed after one clean test. Intermittent faults often "fix themselves" for ten minutes. Test longer and harder than feels necessary before you trust it.
Also seen

A genuinely stubborn intermittent fault sometimes just gets swapped out wholesale — cable, DI, whatever — rather than fully diagnosed, because a show can't wait. That's a legitimate call under time pressure. Just make sure the suspect item gets properly fault-found later rather than quietly going back into general stock (§11.5).

12.7 When to stop and ask for help

There's no shame threshold here, only a time one. If you've worked the method for a few minutes and you're not narrowing it down — you're just trying things — stop and get a second pair of eyes, or hand it to whoever's more senior, especially against a clock before doors. Explaining exactly what you've already ruled out ("it's not the mic, not the cable, definitely getting signal to the stagebox") makes you look competent, not incapable, and saves the next person from repeating your steps.

12.8 The first sixty seconds — a script, not a feeling

Everything from here to §12.16 is written so you can follow it as an actual procedure, step by step, without needing to already have a feel for what's wrong. That feel comes later, from doing this enough times — for now, the procedure is the substitute for it, and it works just as well under pressure.

  1. Is it one thing, or everything? One dead channel is a completely different problem from the whole system being silent. Establish which you've got before doing anything else — it decides which section below you need.
  2. What changed right before it broke? Someone plugged something in, a scene got recalled, a cable got moved or trodden on, a setting got bumped. Faults very often correlate with the last thing that changed — ask, even if the answer is "nothing that I know of".
  3. Check the cheap, obvious things before the exotic ones — mute, fader position, gain, a connector not fully seated, a power switch — in that order. It feels unglamorous. It's also where most faults actually are, and ruling them out in ten seconds each is much faster than fault-finding something that was never actually broken.
  4. Then follow the chain (§12.1) for whatever's left, using the specific walkthroughs below for the part of the system that's misbehaving.
  5. Narrate it, even just in your head. "Signal's fine at the stagebox, so it's not the mic or the cable — the fault's between there and the desk." Saying the logic out loud is what stops you re-testing the same thing three times.
Standard

Print or screenshot this section and the table in §12.9 onward, or bookmark them — the goal isn't to memorise everything below, it's to know it exists and where to find it fast when you actually need it.

12.11 Wireless & RF faults

The fuller diagnostic table lives in §5.9 — this is the beginner-friendly walk-through version, in the order to actually try things.

  1. Check the obvious first: is the pack switched on, is it on the right frequency/group-and-channel, is the battery actually fresh (not just "not flashing red yet")?
  2. Look at the RF meter on the receiver. No bars at all points at the transmitter, the antenna, or being out of range — not the audio path. Strong bars but bad audio points at a gain or audio-chain problem instead, not RF.
  3. Check antenna basics: connected, not damaged, not coiled up inside a rack, positioned with reasonable line of sight to the pack. See §5.3.
  4. Move the performer through the space the pack will actually be used in while watching the meter — a dropout at one specific spot points at a dead zone or reflection, not a broken pack.
  5. Swap the pack for a known-good spare to isolate whether it's the transmitter, receiver, or something environmental.
  6. If it worked earlier today and doesn't now, suspect the spectrum has changed — other RF users have arrived nearby. Rescan (§5.5) rather than assuming your own kit has failed.
Watch it

Never try to fault-find RF mid-performance beyond a fast pack swap. Diagnose properly afterwards — a wired backup mic (§5.9) is the correct answer in the moment, not troubleshooting live.

12.12 Console faults

The quick-reference table is in §7.7. As a beginner, work these in order rather than jumping around — most console "faults" turn out to be one of the first three.

  1. Mute and fader — is the channel actually unmuted and the fader actually up? Check the physical position, not just what you remember setting it to.
  2. Layer/bank — on a digital desk with multiple channel layers or banks, are you looking at (and adjusting) the layer the channel actually lives on?
  3. Routing — is the channel routed to the bus or main output that's actually live? A channel can look perfectly healthy and simply not be going anywhere useful.
  4. Solo — is something else soloed, silencing everything except one channel? Clear all solos and check again.
  5. Scene/recall safes — did a scene just get recalled that changed something without you noticing? Check recall history if the desk has one.
  6. Gain and processing — only once the above are all ruled out, look at gain, EQ, dynamics and inserts on that specific channel.

12.14 Digital audio & Dante network faults

  1. Check the physical layer first — network cable fully seated, link light on at both ends of the run. A dead link light means the fault is that cable or that port, not the audio settings.
  2. Open Dante Controller (or the equivalent) and check the device actually appears on the network. If it's missing entirely, it's a network/power problem, not a routing one.
  3. Check sample rate matches across every device (§2.9). A mismatched device simply won't connect — it'll show as unavailable rather than giving an obvious error.
  4. Check the subscription/routing is actually made in the grid — a device being visible on the network doesn't mean its channels are patched anywhere.
  5. Check clock status — you want to see a clear, single leader with everything else following it, not multiple devices disagreeing.
  6. Clicks, pops or dropouts with everything otherwise routed correctly usually mean a clock problem (§2.9) or, on a bigger network, congestion — try raising the device's latency setting a step (§8.2) before assuming a hardware fault.

12.15 Power & electrical faults — what's yours to check

This is the one category where the boundary matters as much as the diagnosis. As a non-electrician, there's a real, useful amount you can safely check — and a clear line past which the answer is always "get someone qualified", not "have a go".

Safe to check yourself
Is the socket/distro actually switched on. Is a fuse or breaker tripped, and if so, does it trip again immediately on reset (if it does, stop — that's a real fault, not a nuisance trip). Is a cable visibly damaged. Is a device actually plugged in properly. Is the right circuit/phase being used.
Not yours to open up or bypass
Anything inside a distro, a suspect mains lead you're tempted to "just tape up", a breaker that keeps re-tripping, any earth-related fault. This is exactly the boundary §13.2 and §3.8 describe — competence and the law both stop at the same point.
Watch it

A breaker or RCD that trips repeatedly is protecting someone from something real. Don't reset it more than once without investigating what caused it, and never defeat a protective device to "get through the show" — that's the exact scenario the Electricity at Work Regulations exist to prevent (§13.2).

12.17 Keeping a fault log

The fastest route from "following a script" to "just knowing" is deliberately recording what actually went wrong and what actually fixed it, every time, even for a minute. Doesn't need to be formal — a running note in your phone: date, symptom, what it turned out to be. Patterns show up after a surprisingly small number of entries — the same three or four faults account for most of what actually goes wrong, and once you've written them down a few times you'll start recognising them before you've even finished testing.

Also seen

Experienced engineers do this instinctively without calling it anything — it's just "oh, this always happens when X". Writing it down deliberately for your first year or two just gets you there faster than waiting for it to happen by accident.

12.18 Feedback — the immediate response

Prevention is §15.8. This is what to actually do when a mic starts ringing or howling live, because the instinctive reaction — reaching for EQ — is usually the wrong first move.

  1. Pull the fader first, not the EQ. The fastest way to stop feedback is to reduce the gain in the loop — bring the offending channel down (or all the way out) before you do anything else. You can't identify or fix anything sensibly while it's still howling.
  2. Work out which channel it actually is. On a busy stage with several open mics this isn't always obvious — mute channels one at a time if you have to, starting with whichever mic just moved or whoever just walked closer to a monitor.
  3. Once it's quiet, find the frequency — by ear, or with an RTA if the desk has one — and apply a narrow cut there, not a broad one that dulls the whole channel.
  4. Bring the fader back up slowly, listening for the same ring returning. If it comes back at a lower level than before, you've genuinely bought headroom; if it's still right on the edge, the room or the mic technique is the real problem, not the EQ.
Watch it

Feedback through an open IEM channel is a hearing-safety issue, not just an embarrassing noise — see the warning in §5.7. Pull the level immediately rather than trying to EQ your way out of it in-ear.

UK law and industry practice in plain terms: know your responsibility, the paperwork and when to ask someone qualified.

Go deeper

The legislation above sets the legal duties; it doesn't tell you how to actually run a safe event day to day. The Purple Guide — the HSE-endorsed, industry-written reference for health, safety and welfare at music and other events — is the document that translates all of this into practical guidance, and it's genuinely worth knowing exists even if you never read it cover to cover. See §18.8.

13.2 Electricity at Work Regulations 1989

The foundational duty: electrical systems must be constructed and maintained so as to prevent danger, and anyone working on or near them must be competent to do so. It doesn't specify exact test intervals or methods — it sets the outcome (safe equipment, competent people) and leaves the "how often" to risk-based guidance, which is why §13.8 on PAT testing looks the way it does.

For the temporary power distribution itself — as opposed to the individual appliances plugged into it — the industry code of practice that puts flesh on this regulation is BS 7909, covered alongside power distribution in §3.8.

13.3 LOLER, PUWER & Working at Height

Any motor, chain hoist, shackle, sling or spreader beam you use to lift anything is "lifting equipment" under LOLER, and needs a periodic thorough examination by a competent person, documented, before it's used.

Lifting accessories (slings, shackles, hooks) & anything lifting people
Thorough examination every 6 months.
Lifting machines used only for goods (most chain motors, hoists)
Thorough examination every 12 months, unless a written examination scheme specifies otherwise.

PUWER is the broader regulation covering all work equipment, not just lifting gear — it's why a company drill, a pallet truck or a table saw in the warehouse has to be suitable for the job, properly maintained, and only used by someone trained on it.

Standard

Never use a motor, chain, sling or shackle whose inspection tag is missing or out of date, and never improvise a lifting accessory from something not rated for the job (a length of rope instead of a proper sling, for instance). This one's non-negotiable — it's the regulation that exists because gravity doesn't negotiate either.

The Working at Height Regulations 2005 are the separate duty covering the person, not the equipment — anywhere a fall could cause injury, from a step ladder patching a stagebox to a MEWP flying a PA. The core requirement is straightforward: avoid work at height where reasonably practicable, use the right equipment (proper access rather than an improvised stack of cases), and make sure anyone working at height is trained and competent for the specific task and equipment involved.

Watch it

As a newer tech you're very unlikely to be flying PA or working from a MEWP yourself in your first year or two — that's a trained, often ticketed role. If you're ever asked to go up something you haven't been trained on, it's completely reasonable to say so rather than quietly having a go. Nobody experienced will think less of you for it; they'll think a lot less of you for not saying anything and getting hurt.

13.4 Manual handling

Flightcases, speaker stacks and cable drums are heavy, awkward, and the single most common source of injury in this job — far more so than electrical faults. The regulations require employers to assess and reduce risk where reasonably practicable, but the practical version is mostly common sense applied consistently:

  • Assess the load before you lift it — weight, shape, how far, how many people.
  • Use trolleys, ramps and team lifts rather than muscling something alone because asking for help feels slow.
  • Lift with your legs, keep the load close to your body, avoid twisting under load.
  • Know your own limits on a twelve-hour day — the tenth case feels heavier than the first, and that's exactly when backs go.

13.5 Noise at Work — protecting your own hearing

This is the regulation about your safety, not the audience's. Long-term exposure to loud sound damages hearing permanently and cumulatively — the risk you're managing here is a career-length one, not a single-show one.

80 dB(A) — lower exposure action value
Employer must assess risk and provide information, training and (on request) hearing protection.
85 dB(A) — upper exposure action value
Employer must provide hearing protection and mark out hearing protection zones.
87 dB(A) — exposure limit value
The absolute ceiling, accounting for any protection worn. Must never be exceeded.

These are averaged exposure values over a working day, not instantaneous levels — a loud band soundcheck plus a loud show plus loud monitors easily adds up to a daily average well past 85 dB(A), even if no single moment felt dangerous.

Standard

Wear hearing protection during system tuning, soundcheck and load-in tool use as a matter of routine — musicians' earplugs (flat attenuation, doesn't dull the tone) are cheap and worth having a set of in your bag permanently. Protecting your hearing at 22 is what lets you still do this job at 55. Mixing loud all afternoon also drifts your judgement, not just your hearing — §16.7.

Current source

The figures above are from the UK Control of Noise at Work Regulations. The official HSE noise guidance confirms the 80 dB(A) lower action value, 85 dB(A) upper action value and 87 dB(A) exposure limit. If your regular work is loud, ask the employer about risk assessment, protection and hearing checks rather than trying to judge exposure by ear.

13.6 CDM 2015 and events

CDM (Construction, Design and Management) 2015 applies to more than building sites — HSE guidance explicitly covers "the building, fitting out and taking down of temporary structures for TV, film and theatre productions and live events." A stage, a truss rig, a marquee and a temporary FOH structure are all "construction" in this legal sense.

In practice this means bigger events carry a Principal Designer and Principal Contractor role, a construction phase plan, and formal coordination of the build/de-rig — usually managed by the Production Manager, not by you directly. Your part in it is mostly the RAMS you're handed (§13.7) and following the site rules they set.

13.7 RAMS — reading and writing one

Risk Assessment & Method Statement. The risk assessment identifies what could go wrong and how likely/severe it is; the method statement describes how the work will actually be done to control that risk. Together they're the standard safety paperwork for any event task with meaningful risk — rigging, working at height, electrical builds.

As a reader
You'll be handed RAMS for tasks you're doing and asked to confirm you've read and understood them, sometimes with a signature. Actually read them — they're specific to that site and that task, not generic boilerplate, even when they look similar week to week.
As a (junior) writer
Early on you're unlikely to be writing RAMS solo, but you'll contribute to them — flagging a hazard, describing how you'll actually do a task. Be specific: "two-person lift, case weighs approximately 40 kg, route checked clear" is useful; "be careful" is not.

13.8 PAT testing in practice

Widely assumed to be a fixed annual legal requirement. It isn't. PAT (Portable Appliance Testing) is one common method of meeting the actual duty in the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 — that equipment is maintained in a safe condition — but the regulations themselves specify no fixed interval, and HSE is explicit that testing isn't a blanket annual legal requirement. The right frequency is risk-based: how the equipment is used, how harsh the environment is, how often it moves.

Also seen

In practice, most hire companies test far more often than any legal minimum requires, because kit that travels constantly, gets dropped, and plugs into unknown venue power genuinely does fail more often than a lamp in an office. Follow your company's PAT schedule as the actual policy — it's usually more conservative than the law strictly demands, which is the right way round for touring kit.

13.9 Long days — working in a way you can keep doing

Everything else in this part protects you from the kit. This one protects you from the schedule. Twelve- and fourteen-hour days, early starts after late finishes, irregular weeks and a culture that quietly rewards never complaining are ordinary in this industry, and they cause far more harm across a career than any single piece of equipment. §13.5 is about not losing your hearing by 55; this is about still wanting to do the job at 55.

Fatigue
It is a safety issue, not a character issue. Tired people misjudge lifts, mis-patch outputs and walk under loads. If you are too tired to do something safely — a lift, a ladder, a long drive — saying so is the professional response, not the weak one. Nobody sensible has ever been sacked for that; people have been badly hurt for not saying it.
The drive home
Usually the most dangerous part of the day. The genuinely serious risk in live events is not the show, it's a 1am motorway drive after a fourteen-hour day. Plan the journey home as part of the job: share driving, take the break, or say up front that you need accommodation rather than discovering at midnight that you can't safely drive. Microsleeps do not announce themselves.
Eat and drink
Do it on purpose. Covered as etiquette in §1.6, but it's a safety point too. Dehydration and skipped meals show up as poor decisions and short temper long before you notice them as hunger. Water in your bag (§19.17), and eat when catering is open rather than when you feel like it.
Irregular work
It carries its own load. Freelance and casual work adds uncertainty — not knowing next month's diary, saying yes to everything in case the phone stops ringing, no sick pay. That's a real and well-recognised pressure in this industry, not a personal failing, and it's worth naming rather than absorbing quietly.
Standard

Learn to say the useful version of no: "I can do the derig, but I'm not driving after it", or "I've been on since six — can someone else take the ladder?" It's specific, it offers a solution, and it's much easier to hear than a flat refusal. Crew chiefs would far rather rearrange a job than fill in an accident report.

Also seen

The UK live-events industry has its own support organisations, which is worth knowing before you need them rather than after. Music Support works with people in music and live events on mental health and addiction; Backup — the Technical Entertainment Charity gives financial help to technical professionals hit by illness, injury or hardship; and Stagehand is the welfare charity attached to the PSA (§17.4). Contact details and eligibility change, so look up their current pages rather than trusting a number printed anywhere — including here.

Watch it

Alcohol is unusually available in this line of work — bars, riders, wrap drinks, and a strong tradition of decompressing after a show. That's fine for plenty of people and genuinely isn't for others, particularly alongside irregular hours and stress. If it ever stops feeling like a choice, the organisations above exist precisely for that and talk to people in this industry every day.

13.10 Site induction, fire & emergency basics

Every site should give you a proper induction before you start working — even a five-minute one on a small job. If nobody offers one, ask; it's a completely normal question, not an awkward one.

What a decent induction covers
Where the fire exits and assembly point are, who the site's fire marshal/first aider is, what to do if the fire alarm goes (stop work, leave by the nearest exit, don't go back for kit), and any site-specific hazards (a pit, a raised stage edge, restricted areas).
Keep it clear as you work
Never stack cases, run cable or park a flightcase across a fire exit or in front of an extinguisher, even "just for five minutes" during a fast changeover.
Who's actually responsible
Fire safety at the venue is legally the "responsible person"'s job — usually the venue or the event organiser, not you — but everyone on site still needs to know where to go if the alarm sounds, and you're expected to follow their evacuation procedure without waiting to be told twice.
Go deeper

Fire safety in non-domestic premises in England and Wales sits under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005; Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own separate legislation. The detail varies by nation and by venue, so treat this as "know the site's actual procedure", not as a number to memorise from here.

13.11 PPE — what's actually expected of you

Personal protective equipment in this job is mostly unglamorous and mostly non-negotiable once a site asks for it. None of this is new if you've read this book cover to cover — it's gathered here as the one place to check what you actually need before you turn up.

Footwear
Steel-toe (safety) boots for load-in, build and de-rig on most sites — a dropped case or a rolled flightcase wheel is a genuinely common injury. Confirm with whoever's booking you whether a site requires them (§1.6).
Hi-vis
Standard for load-in and de-rig, especially anywhere vehicles are moving. Comes off once doors open and the show look matters (§1.6).
Hearing protection
Yours to bring, not usually issued — see §13.5 for why it matters and what to actually use.
Gloves
Worth having for cable and case handling — protects against splinters, pinch points and sharp rack ears, and gives you a better grip on a wet loading bay ramp. Not usually mandatory, but cheap insurance.
Standard

If a site tells you PPE is required for a task, that's not a suggestion. If you don't have something a site requires, say so before you start rather than working without it.

13.12 Working alone — early starts and late finishes

You'll sometimes be first in or last out — an early advance visit, a solo van run, a de-rig finishing long after everyone else has left. Working alone isn't against the rules, but it changes what "safe" looks like.

  • Tell someone where you are and roughly when you expect to finish — a message to a colleague or your production manager before you go into an empty venue is enough, not a formal check-in system.
  • Keep your phone charged and on you, not in a flightcase across the room, especially if you're working at height or lifting alone.
  • Don't take on a task alone that genuinely needs two people — a heavy lift, anything at height — just because nobody else is around. Wait, or ask, rather than improvise it solo.
  • Know how you'd actually get help if something went wrong — is the venue occupied by anyone else, is there a landline, does your phone have signal in that building.
PART 14

Capture, stream & hand over audio

Recording and broadcast basics for the jobs where somebody needs a multitrack, stream or archive.

14.1 Why you'll be asked to record

Multitrack recording used to be a specialist add-on; on a modern digital console it's often a tick-box, which means it gets asked for constantly — an archive for the band, a reference for mix notes, a virtual soundcheck for tomorrow, a podcast or stream running alongside a live event. You don't need to be a recording engineer to be the person who presses record correctly and hands over usable files.

14.2 Multitrack recording from the desk

Most digital consoles can record every channel to an internal or attached drive, independent of the live mix — the fader positions you're mixing with don't affect what gets recorded, because the recording happens pre-fader, at the gained-up signal.

What to check first
Drive formatted correctly for the console, enough free space for the set length at the console's sample rate/bit depth, and a test recording actually played back before doors — a red record light isn't proof of a good file.
Naming & organisation
Console projects/sessions named clearly by date and event, not left as the default. Someone will need to find this file in six months.
Gain staging for recording
The same §2.5 rules apply, but there's no fader to save you after the fact if the input gain was wrong — a clipped multitrack recording is permanently clipped, however good the live mix sounded.
Watch it

Confirm the recording actually started and is actually receiving audio — not just that the console says "recording". A drive that failed to mount, or a session that didn't arm properly, fails silently on plenty of desks. Check levels are moving on the recording meters a few minutes in, not just at the start.

14.3 Virtual soundcheck

Playing yesterday's (or last night's) multitrack recording back through the console today, instead of the band, to build or refine a mix without needing the musicians on stage. Standard practice on touring shows with a fixed lineup across multiple dates.

  • Route the recorded tracks back into the same channels they were recorded from, so all your processing and routing stays valid.
  • Mute the live inputs while doing this, obviously — you don't want stage mics live and bleeding into a playback session.
  • It's genuinely useful for training too — build a mix from a real multitrack at home on an offline editor (§8.1) to practise.

14.4 Formats, sample rate & bit depth for recording

Building on §2.9: for recording specifically, 48 kHz / 24-bit is the standard default for live multitrack — it matches the live/video world's sample rate and gives ample dynamic range without the storage and processing cost of 96 kHz, which is rarely worth it outside specialist work. Files are almost always exported as WAV — uncompressed, universally compatible, no quality loss. Avoid MP3 or other compressed formats for anything that might get remixed later; the compression throws away information permanently.

14.5 Broadcast handoffs

Covered from the department-relationship side in §9.3 — the technical version: a broadcast truck or streaming setup usually wants either an isolated analogue/digital split of your key channels, or a small submix (a "TX mix") built specifically for them, which is often different from your PA mix — drier, more controlled, sometimes mono-compatible.

Standard

Agree exactly what the broadcast team needs — which channels or a submix, what level, what format — at the advance stage, and test the actual feed with them before doors. "We'll sort it on the day" with a broadcast crew is a bad plan; their side often has far less flexibility to adapt live than yours does.

14.6 Streaming — what changes

A stream adds a compressed, internet-delivered version of the show, usually built by a separate video/streaming team, but you'll be their audio source. Two practical points: streaming platforms commonly normalise loudness to a target (broadly in the −14 to −16 LUFS integrated range for most major platforms, though the exact figure varies by platform and changes over time — check the current spec rather than assuming), so wildly over-driving your feed just gets turned down again by the platform rather than sounding louder; and stream delay means a presenter watching stream comments will always be seconds behind the room, which occasionally causes confusing crosstalk on comms if nobody accounts for it.

14.7 Setting up a multitrack recording — start to finish

The concepts are in §14.2; this is the actual sequence to follow on the day.

  1. Check storage first. Confirm the drive is formatted correctly for that console, and work out roughly how much space the set needs at your sample rate and bit depth (§14.4) — a full drive fails the recording silently on some desks rather than warning you.
  2. Name the session properly before you arm anything — date and event, not the console's default name. Nobody finds "Session 4" useful in six months.
  3. Arm the recording and do an actual test — a minute of real signal, not silence — then stop and play it back. Confirm you can hear it, on the right channels, at a sensible level.
  4. Check your gain staging one more time specifically for recording (§14.2) — there's no fader to rescue a clipped multitrack later.
  5. Start the real recording in good time before the actual show starts, not at the last second — you want margin to notice if something's gone wrong.
  6. Check in on it during the show — glance at the recording meters a few minutes in and periodically after, confirming levels are still moving.
  7. Stop, safely eject the drive, and back it up before you leave site if at all possible — see §14.10.

14.8 Building a broadcast submix — start to finish

The relationship and level of care needed is in §14.5; here's the actual build sequence for a simple TX/broadcast mix.

  1. Confirm what they actually want at the advance stage (§1.7) — a full isolated split of individual channels, or one submix bus.
  2. Build the submix on a spare bus or matrix, routed from the same channels as your PA mix but with its own fader and processing — never just tap your main mix, which moves every time you adjust the room sound.
  3. Dial it drier and more controlled than your PA mix — less reverb, tighter dynamics — since the broadcast audience isn't in the room and doesn't get the benefit of the room's own natural sound.
  4. Isolate the physical feed — an isolation transformer or a properly isolated digital feed (§10.5), never a bare parallel tap.
  5. Test the actual feed with the broadcast team before doors, not just in soundcheck with the band silent — confirm level, polarity and that they're getting what you think you're sending.
  6. Keep it running and stable through the show — big PA-mix moves (effects swells, big fader rides) don't need to follow through to the broadcast mix, and often shouldn't.

14.9 Simple stand-alone recordings

Not every recording request is a full multitrack off a big digital console — plenty are much smaller: a two-mic podcast recording, a single-camera interview needing clean audio, a small meeting room capture. Worth knowing this world exists as its own smaller category rather than assuming every recording ask needs the full §14.2 treatment.

Typical setup
A small audio interface (Focusrite Scarlett and similar are the common cheap standard) feeding a laptop running a DAW or dedicated recording software, or a standalone field recorder (Zoom, Tascam) for something fully portable.
What still applies
Every fundamental from earlier in the book — gain staging (§2.5), phantom power for a condenser mic (§2.6), a proper test before the real take. Small setups fail for exactly the same reasons big ones do.
What's different
Usually far less redundancy — one mic, one cable, no spare channel to fall back on if something's wrong. Check everything twice before a take you can't easily redo, especially an interview that won't happen again.

14.10 Backing up & handing over recordings

A recording nobody can find afterwards might as well not exist.

  • Copy it off the recording drive before you leave site wherever possible — drives fail, get lost, or get reused for the next job before anyone remembers to pull the files.
  • Keep two copies in two places until you know it's safely landed with whoever asked for it — a laptop and a cloud upload, or two separate drives.
  • Hand it over with basic context — event name, date, what's on each track if it's multitrack, anything that went wrong (a dropped channel, a late start). A silent file dump makes someone else redo work you've already done.

14.11 Rights, consent & who's actually allowed to record

Being technically capable of recording or streaming a show isn't the same as being allowed to. This sits above your pay grade to resolve, but it's worth being aware of so you flag it rather than assume someone else already has.

Performer consent
Artists and their management usually need to agree to being recorded, streamed or archived — sometimes it's in the contract rider, sometimes it isn't and needs asking. Don't assume "we're recording tonight" has automatically been cleared with the act.
Music licensing
Recording or streaming copyrighted music (a covers band, a DJ set) can need separate permission from the performance licence a venue already holds — PRS for Music and PPL cover public performance, but recording, streaming and later distribution are often a different licensing question entirely.
Audience
A stream or broadcast that shows the crowd is capturing identifiable people, which has its own privacy considerations — most events handle this with signage or terms at the door, arranged by the organiser, not by you.
Go deeper

None of this is something to solve yourself on the day — if you're ever asked to record or stream something and nobody's mentioned rights or consent at all, it's a reasonable question to raise at the advance stage (§1.7) rather than something to assume is already handled.

14.12 Backup recording — not relying on one capture

A multitrack recording off the main console is usually your best-quality capture, but it's also a single point of failure — one full drive, one crashed session, one setting left wrong, and a non-repeatable show is gone. Worth thinking about for anything that genuinely can't be redone.

  • A simple stereo backup costs little — a small field recorder (§14.9) fed from a spare matrix or aux output, running independently of the main console's recording path, catches the whole show even if the multitrack fails.
  • Keep it genuinely independent — a backup that shares the same drive, same power, or same software as the primary recording isn't really a backup, it's the same single point of failure twice.
  • Weigh the effort against what's actually at stake. A regular support slot probably doesn't need this; a one-off festival headline set, a once-only broadcast, or anything explicitly billed as "the archive recording" usually does.

14.13 Preparing files before handoff

§14.10 covers getting the recording safely off site and to the right person; this is about what actually makes those files usable once they land, rather than just present.

Trim the dead air
If you started recording well before doors and stopped well after (sensibly, per §14.7), the recipient doesn't want twenty minutes of silence at the head and tail of every file. Trim it, or at minimum tell them where the useful audio actually starts.
Leave the levels alone
Don't normalise, compress or otherwise process the handed-over files "to make them sound better" unless you've been specifically asked to mix them. Someone downstream may want the raw signal — processing it first just removes options you don't know they need.
Note what went wrong, with a timestamp
A dropped channel at 9:47, a clipped input on song four, a late start — write it down against the actual file, not from memory a week later when someone asks why track 12 is silent for two minutes.
Check the names travel with the files
Track/channel names set at the desk (§7.4) don't always survive an export to plain WAV files — confirm the exported filenames are still readable as channel names, not just "Track_001.wav", before you hand them over.
PART 15

Monitor mixes & IEMs

Mixing for the people making the sound. Different pressure, different priorities and a fast route to practical learning.

15.1 The monitor engineer's job

Introduced in §6.9 and §1.3 — the monitor engineer builds a separate mix (or several, one per performer) of what's happening on stage so performers can hear themselves and each other. It's arguably higher-pressure than FOH: a bad FOH mix is unfortunate, but a performer who can't hear their own pitch can actually fall apart on stage. Requests come fast, often mid-song, and need to happen immediately.

15.2 Wedges vs IEMs

Wedges (floor monitors)IEMs (in-ear monitors)
Stage volumeAdds to overall stage noise, heard by everyone nearbyPrivate to the performer — much quieter stage overall
Feedback riskReal — a mic pointed near its own wedge rings easilyNone — sealed in-ear, no acoustic path back to the mic
FeelPhysical, "in the chest" — many performers prefer itCan feel isolating; ambient mics help, §15.4
ConsistencyChanges with performer position on stageIdentical wherever they stand
SetupSimple — a speaker and an aux sendNeeds RF (or wired) transmission, fitted earpieces, more failure points

Most professional touring acts now run IEMs, often alongside a single wedge or a side-fill for the "feel". Corporate and theatre work leans wedges or none at all, since performers are usually speaking rather than singing to pitch.

15.3 Building IEM mixes

  • Start from a rough version of the FOH mix as a base, then adjust per performer — a drummer typically wants click and their own kit prominent; a singer wants their own voice loud and clear above almost everything.
  • Vocals in IEMs usually want more presence and less reverb than FOH — performers are singing to pitch against a mix in their ears, not enjoying the room's ambience.
  • Keep overall IEM levels sensible — performers wearing IEMs for a whole set are exposed to that level continuously, and the same hearing-damage physics from §13.5 applies to what's happening inside their ears, not just in the room.
  • Agree a simple, fast request system — verbal, hand signal, or a wireless mixing app on their own phone (Aviom, Behringer P16 and similar let performers ride their own mix, §15.5).

15.4 Ambient mics & hearing the room

Sealed IEMs cut out the room entirely, which some performers dislike — no crowd noise, no natural sense of the space. An ambient mic (a small mic at the drum kit or front of stage, blended low into the IEM mix) restores some of that sense without needing an open ear. A cheap, high-value addition many bands don't know to ask for until they've tried it.

15.5 Personal mixers

Systems like Aviom A-Net and Behringer P16/Powerplay give each performer their own small mixer, fed a multichannel signal over one cable (or wirelessly), letting them build their own IEM mix from a fixed set of sources rather than requesting changes from you. Genuinely popular with touring musicians because it removes the back-and-forth entirely — your job becomes making sure the right sources reach their personal mixer input list, not riding their fader positions for them.

15.6 Common monitor world faults

SymptomLikely cause

Performer says "I can't hear myself" mid-songUsually a level request, sometimes a channel actually dropped — check the meter, not just the fader

15.7 Building an IEM mix — start to finish

The principles are in §15.3; here's an actual build order for a performer's first IEM mix from nothing.

  1. Start with their own source at a comfortable, clear level — a singer's own vocal, a drummer's own kit. This is the anchor everything else gets balanced against.
  2. Add the click or backing track next, if there is one — loud enough to lock to without drowning out everything else. Getting this wrong is the single most common cause of a performer rushing or dragging the tempo.
  3. Bring in the rest of the band at a supporting level — enough to play together, not a full band mix. This isn't FOH; it doesn't need to sound "finished".
  4. Add anything they specifically asked for — another performer's vocal to harmonise against, a specific instrument they cue off.
  5. Check it standing where they'll actually stand, moving if the system allows it, since a mix judged only from the console can miss RF dead spots (§5.8).
  6. Confirm the level is sensible, not just "loud enough to hear over everything" — §15.3 on why that matters long-term.
  7. Save it and label it clearly if the console supports per-performer mix presets, so tomorrow's rebuild starts from today's result rather than from silence.

15.8 Ringing out a wedge — feedback control by ear

"Ringing out" a monitor means deliberately finding and taming its feedback-prone frequencies before a performer's mic is anywhere near it, so the wedge can run louder without howling later.

  1. With the mic that'll actually be used on that wedge, position both roughly as they will be for the show.
  2. Slowly bring the wedge's send level up while the mic is live, listening for the first ringing tone to emerge — not a full howl, the first hint of one building.
  3. Pull the send back down slightly the moment you hear it, then sweep a narrow EQ cut across the console's parametric EQ on that wedge output to find the exact frequency ringing.
  4. Cut that frequency by several dB, then bring the send level back up again — you should get further before the next ring appears.
  5. Repeat for the next frequency that emerges, but stop once you've caught 2–3 problem points — over-notching a wedge starts to audibly thin it out and makes it sound worse, not better, for genuinely more headroom.
Also seen

Some engineers do this with pink noise and an RTA (§8.3) rather than purely by ear — faster and more precise on a big rig with many wedges, but the by-ear version above is the one you can do anywhere with nothing but a console, and it's worth being genuinely good at it.

Also seen

[2026] A newer generation of AI-assisted feedback suppression products (AlphaLabs De-Feedback among them) claim to detect and notch ringing frequencies automatically, faster than a human can react. Where they're used, they're a safety net layered on top of good gain structure and sensible speaker/mic placement — not a replacement for either. Treat any claim that a product removes the need to understand feedback with healthy scepticism; ringing out a wedge by ear remains the skill that gets you hired.

15.9 Who's actually on the monitor desk

On a smaller job, one engineer runs FOH and monitors from a single console, splitting attention between both. On a bigger show, monitors get a dedicated engineer and a dedicated console side-of-stage, fed the same inputs as FOH via a split (§3.5) or a shared digital network (§8.2) so both consoles see every channel independently. As a newer tech you're more likely to start by assisting a monitor engineer — patching, cabling, running out to swap a pack — than running the monitor mix yourself, and that's a genuinely good way to learn the pressure of the role before you're the one under it.

15.10 Click tracks & drummer cues

Many modern acts play to a click (a metronome track in the drummer's, sometimes the whole band's, IEMs), often alongside backing tracks or programmed elements that need to stay locked to the live band.

Click source
Usually generated by playback software (§8.4) or a dedicated click/track player, fed as its own channel straight into the drummer's (and sometimes other performers') IEM mix — never through the PA, where the audience would hear it.
Why it matters to you
If a click drops out or falls out of time, the whole band notices immediately and the show visibly wobbles. Treat the click feed with the same care as a lead vocal — check it in line check, know where it comes from, and know how to get it back fast if it fails.
Common request
"More click" or "less click" from the drummer specifically — a fast, simple level request, not usually a fault. Learn to tell the difference between that and a genuine dropout (§12.12).

15.11 Building a wedge mix — start to finish

The principles are in §15.3 and the feedback-control groundwork in §15.8; here's an actual build order for a performer's wedge from nothing. It's a different process to §15.7's IEM build — feedback, not just balance, is the thing shaping every decision.

  1. Ring the wedge out first (§15.8), with the actual mic that'll be on it, before building any level into the mix — you need to know how much headroom you've actually got before you start.
  2. Start with their own source at a moderate level, well under where it started ringing — a singer's own vocal, an instrumentalist's own line. Leave real margin; you'll be adding more sources on top, not just this one.
  3. Add the click or backing track next, if there is one, at a level that locks without pushing the wedge closer to feedback than it needs to be.
  4. Bring in supporting sources sparingly — every source added to a wedge mix eats into the same shared feedback margin, unlike an IEM mix where headroom isn't really a constraint. Less goes in a wedge mix than an IEM mix for exactly this reason.
  5. Check it at performance position and orientation, not just from the desk — a wedge that's fine pointed at an empty stage can ring the moment the performer leans into the mic at their actual singing distance and angle.
  6. Recheck the ring-out points once the mix has level in it — the frequencies most likely to feed back can shift slightly once other sources are present and the room has people in it.
  7. Save it and label it clearly, same as §15.7 — tomorrow's rebuild starts from today's result.

15.12 Custom vs universal-fit earpieces

The IEM mix you build is only as good as the earpiece delivering it — worth knowing the difference exists, even though it's the performer's choice and cost, not yours.

Universal-fit
Generic earpieces with interchangeable foam or silicone tips. Cheap, replaceable on the day, but seal (and therefore isolation and low-end) varies a lot by ear and by tip size.
Custom-moulded
Made from an impression of the performer's own ear canal. Better seal, better isolation, more consistent bass response, and generally more comfortable for a long set — but expensive, slow to replace if lost or damaged, and useless to anyone else.
Watch it

Never hand a used universal foam/silicone tip to a different performer without a fresh tip fitted — sharing eartips between people is a genuine hygiene issue, not just an etiquette one.

PART 16

Make it sound good

EQ, dynamics and effects: the listening decisions behind the console controls.

16.1 Subtractive vs additive EQ

Two philosophies, both correct in different moments.

Subtractive
Cut what's wrong rather than boost what's missing. Find a harsh, boxy or muddy frequency and pull it down. Generally the safer, more transparent default — cuts rarely sound "processed" the way boosts can.
Additive
Boost a frequency to bring out a wanted character — air on a vocal, punch on a kick. Useful and often necessary, but easier to overdo, and every boost eats into your headroom (§2.5).
Standard

A reasonable default sequence: sweep a narrow boost across the spectrum to find problem frequencies, cut those, then use gentle, wide boosts if something still needs character on top. Cut narrow, boost wide is a decent rule of thumb.

Ear-training apps and plugins (Quiztones and similar) that play a boosted frequency and ask you to identify it are a genuinely fast way to build this skill — twenty minutes a day for a few weeks makes a real, measurable difference. See Part 18.

16.3 Gates & expanders in practice

A gate mutes a channel below a threshold; an expander turns it down more gently rather than cutting it off entirely — a softer version of the same idea. Live, the classic use is drum bleed: a snare mic picks up the whole kit, and a gate keyed to open only when the snare itself hits keeps the channel clean between hits.

Threshold
The level below which the gate closes. Set too high and it chops the start of quiet hits; too low and it doesn't help with bleed at all.
Attack
How fast it opens once triggered. Too slow and you lose the transient — the "click" of the hit.
Hold & release
How long it stays open and how it fades closed. Too short and it chatters or cuts a note off unnaturally; too long and the bleed it was meant to remove comes back through.
Watch it

An aggressively gated channel can sound fine solo'd and completely wrong in the full mix, because the gate is reacting to everything else happening on stage too. Always judge gate settings with the whole band playing, not solo'd.

16.4 Compressors in practice

A compressor turns down anything above a threshold, by a ratio, reducing the gap between the quietest and loudest moments of a signal — useful because live sources are unpredictable and a vocal that's controlled sits in a mix far more consistently than one that swings wildly between whisper and shout.

Threshold
The level above which compression kicks in.
Ratio
How hard it compresses above threshold. 2:1 is gentle; 8:1 and beyond starts to sound like limiting.
Attack
How fast it starts reducing gain once triggered. Fast attack tames transients hard (can dull the punch); slower attack lets the initial transient through before clamping down.
Release
How fast it lets go afterwards. Too fast can sound pumpy or breathy; too slow can leave it clamped down through the next quiet passage.
Makeup gain
Turns the whole signal back up after compression to compensate for the level lost — this is how compression can make something sound both more controlled and louder.

A workable starting point for a lead vocal live: moderate threshold so it's engaging on louder phrases, ratio around 3:1–4:1, medium attack, medium release, then adjust entirely by ear against the actual performance — printed numbers are a starting point, never a destination.

Also seen

Some engineers barely touch channel compression live and instead control dynamics mostly with the fader, riding it constantly — "the best compressor is your hand on the fader" is a genuinely common opinion. Both approaches produce good mixes in the right hands; neither is more "correct".

16.5 Reverb & effects sends

Live reverb is used far more sparingly than in a studio — too much and a mix loses definition and becomes hard to hear clearly in a room that likely already has its own natural reverberation. Typically routed as an aux send to a shared effects return rather than inserted per-channel, so several sources can share one reverb and sit in a common sense of space.

Send level
How much of each channel goes to the effect — usually modest for most sources, more for a lead vocal wanting some lift.
Pre vs post-fade send
Post-fade (the effect follows the channel's fader) is standard for reverb, so the effect fades with the source rather than being left hanging at a fixed level.
Decay time
Longer decay for ballad-paced or spacious material; shorter for busy, fast material where a long tail muddies things.

16.6 Building a mix from silence

A practical sequence for approaching a mix from nothing, useful whether it's a full soundcheck or a quick line check turned straight into a show:

  1. Drums up first (if present) — kick and snare roughly balanced, then the rest of the kit around them, then overheads to glue it together.
  2. Bass in, balanced against the kick so they sit together rather than fighting for the same low-end space.
  3. Other instruments, one at a time, each brought in against what's already there rather than judged solo.
  4. Lead vocal on top, set clearly above the band — it's usually the thing the audience most needs to hear and understand.
  5. Backing vocals and extras woven in around the lead without competing with it.
  6. Step back and listen from a few places in the room, not just at the desk — the mix that's right at FOH can be quite different ten metres to the side.

16.7 Why the mix changes with volume

Your ears are not equally sensitive across the spectrum, and — crucially — the shape of that sensitivity changes with level. At quiet levels we hear much less bass and slightly less extreme top; as things get louder the response flattens out. This is measured by the equal-loudness contours, and it has a very practical consequence: a mix built at one volume will not sound the same at another.

Built quiet, played loud
You compensated for ears that weren't hearing bass, so you added low end. Turn it up and it's boomy and thick.
Built loud, played quiet
The reverse. It sounds thin, gutless and oddly mid-heavy at background level — a common surprise on a corporate job where the "walk-in music" mix was set during a loud soundcheck.
Standard

Build the mix at roughly the level the show will actually run at, and check it at a couple of others before you commit. If the same material has to work at two very different levels — a conference that plays quiet beds and loud stings — expect to need a slightly different balance for each, rather than one mix that's a compromise at both.

Also seen

This is also why mixing loud all afternoon is a bad idea beyond the hearing-damage argument in §13.5: your ears fatigue, your judgement of top end drifts, and you tend to keep creeping the level up to chase the excitement you had an hour ago. Taking the level down for a few minutes resets your reference more reliably than trusting your ears at hour six.

16.8 Delay as an effect

Distinct from the reverb send in §16.5 — a delay repeats the signal as one or more discrete echoes rather than a wash of reflections, and it's routed and used the same way, as an aux send to a shared return.

Slapback
A single short repeat (tens of milliseconds), used to thicken a vocal or add a bit of vintage character without an obvious "echo" being audible.
Tempo-synced delay
A longer, clearly audible repeat set to a musical subdivision of the song's tempo (a quarter note, a dotted eighth) rather than a fixed time — sounds intentional and musical rather than like a mistake or a room problem.
Feedback (the delay parameter, not acoustic feedback)
How many times the signal repeats before dying away — confusingly named the same as the acoustic problem in §15.8, but a completely different thing here: it's a control on the delay effect itself.
Standard

Getting a tempo-synced delay right by ear means tapping the tempo in live if the console supports it, or setting a time in milliseconds calculated from the song's BPM — either works, but an un-synced delay sitting slightly off the beat is more distracting than no delay at all.

16.9 De-essing — controlling sibilance

Sharp "s", "sh" and "t" sounds sit in the same 5–10 kHz range that gives a vocal its clarity and air (§16.2), which is exactly why they're awkward to fix with a static EQ cut — pull that range down enough to tame the esses and the vocal loses its presence everywhere else too. A de-esser is a compressor that only engages in that narrow band, and only when a sibilant sound actually happens, leaving the rest of the vocal untouched.

What triggers it
Its own threshold, watching only the sibilant frequency band — not the whole signal like a normal compressor. When that band gets loud (an "s"), it ducks; the rest of the time it does nothing.
Split-band vs broadband
A split-band de-esser reduces only the offending frequency range when it triggers; a broadband one ducks the whole signal briefly instead, which is cruder but sometimes all an older console offers.
Why it's live-specific
A bright condenser vocal mic close-mic'd on a sibilant singer is the classic case — the closer and brighter the mic, the more esses bite, and it's rarely practical to ask a performer to change their diction mid-show.
Watch it

Over-de-essed vocals develop a noticeable lisp — the "s" sounds vanish rather than just soften. If it's audibly changing the words, back the amount off; a slightly sibilant vocal is far less distracting than one that sounds like it's had a stroke.

PART 17

Career path & next steps

Where people go from warehouse to audio, and what changes when you take on more responsibility or freelance.

17.1 Where people actually go from here

There's no single ladder, but common paths from where you are now: staying in-house and moving warehouse → crew → tech → operator over a year or two; going freelance and building a client list across several companies; specialising hard into one discipline (RF, systems/tuning, monitor world, install/DSP); or moving sideways into production management once you understand how a show actually gets built. None of these are better than the others — they suit different temperaments.

17.2 Freelancing basics

Day rate
Varies hugely by role, region and experience. Ask other freelancers what's typical for your level rather than guessing — undercutting the going rate makes you cheaper to book but harder for everyone (including you, later) to get a fair rate.
Invoicing
Simple, prompt, clear — company/job name, dates, rate, total, payment terms. Chase late payment politely but persistently; it's a genuinely common freelance headache.
Public Liability Insurance (PLI)
Covers you if your work causes injury or damage to someone else or their property. Many venues and production companies won't let you on site without it. Relatively cheap for the protection it gives — get it before you need it.
Equipment insurance
A separate policy from PLI — covers loss, theft or damage to kit you own (see §17.5). Only worth pricing up once you own something worth insuring, but venues and hire companies will occasionally ask whether your own gear is covered, so it's worth being able to answer.
Being your own admin
Diary management, tax (in the UK, registering as self-employed with HMRC if you're trading as a sole trader), and keeping basic records of income and expenses. Unglamorous, but getting caught out at tax time is avoidable with an hour a month.
Go deeper

This book won't make you an accountant, and it shouldn't try to. Talk to other freelancers in the industry about what they actually do for insurance and tax — the details change and local practice varies. See Part 18.

17.3 Building a network

This industry runs heavily on who's known to be reliable, more than on CVs. Practical habits that build a reputation faster than anything else: turn up early, do the unglamorous jobs without being asked twice, communicate clearly when something's gone wrong instead of hiding it, and stay in touch with people you've worked well with rather than only reaching out when you need work. Crew chiefs remember who made their day easier.

17.5 Kit of your own — what to buy first

You don't need much personal kit early on — most of what you use belongs to the company. A sensible first-purchase order: a decent multitool and torch, a set of musicians' earplugs (§13.5), a reliable pair of headphones, a small selection of adapters and a known-good short XLR for fault-finding (§12.2), and boots with steel toes if your company doesn't provide them. A measurement mic and your own console offline software are worth adding once you know which direction you're heading. Once you own the basics, §19.17 is the tickable version for actually getting them out of the door with you.

17.6 Asking for more responsibility, without overstepping

Most people don't get handed the next step up — they ask for it, at the right moment, in the right way. A few things that work better than waiting to be noticed:

  • Say what you want, specifically. "I'd like more time on the desk" is easier to forget than "next time we're short a line check hand, I'd like to shadow it."
  • Ask the person who actually decides, not just whoever's nearest — usually the crew chief or whoever books shifts, not a colleague at your own level.
  • Pick a quiet moment, not mid-build or two minutes before doors. A quick word at the end of a shift lands better than an ambush under pressure.
  • Prove it in small doses first. Ask to shadow a line check before you ask to run one; ask to run one before you ask to mix. Each small win makes the next ask an easy yes.
  • Accept "not yet" without sulking. A genuine "what would you want to see from me first?" gets you a clear target instead of a closed door.
Standard

This is exactly the position most warehouse staff are in when the industry opens up to them: reliable, known, and one honest conversation away from more stage time. The asking is very often the only step actually missing.

17.7 Keeping evidence of your work

§17.3 covers why reputation matters more than a CV here — this is about not losing track of it. A short, private record of what you actually did and who you did it for turns "I've done quite a bit of corporate work" into a specific, checkable list when someone asks.

What to note
Company, venue, date, your role, and one line on what you actually did — "ran line check solo for a 40-input corporate panel" is worth far more than "helped out".
Testimonials
A short "happy to work with again" message after a job you did well is worth asking for, especially from a crew chief or company you'd like to work with regularly. Most people are glad to give one if asked directly rather than hoping it happens on its own.
What not to record
Client-confidential details, other people's rates, or anything from a show you weren't supposed to discuss. A factual record of your own work never needs any of that.

19.13 Pocket reference

XLR pinout

Pin 1 = screen/earth, pin 2 = hot (+), pin 3 = cold (−). "One is ground, two is hot." (§2.3)

Signal levels

Mic level ≈ 1–10 mV. Line level ≈ 0.3–1.5 V (+4 dBu pro / −10 dBV consumer). (§2.2)

Gain staging target (digital desk)

Average around −18 dBFS, peaking to about −10 dBFS. Regularly touching red = gained too hot. (§2.5)

dB rules of thumb

  • +3 dB = double the power, barely audible
  • +6 dB = double the voltage, clearly audible
  • +10 dB ≈ twice as loud to a human ear
  • Double the distance = −6 dB (inverse square law)

Phantom power, fast

Condenser mic / active DI = needs it. Dynamic mic = safe either way. Ribbon mic = can be damaged by it — check before switching a bank on. Always mute before switching. (§2.6)

Frequency crib

  • 20–250 Hz — sub-bass/bass (boomy if too much)
  • 250 Hz–2 kHz — low mid/mid (boxy/honky if too much)
  • 2–8 kHz — presence (harsh/sibilant if too much)
  • 8–20 kHz — air (brittle/hissy if too much)

    A separate legal business structure some freelancers move to once turnover justifies it, distinct from trading as a sole trader. §17.2
    Limiter
    A compressor with a very high ratio, used to stop a signal exceeding a hard ceiling — protective on outputs, essential on IEMs. §7.5 · §5.7
    Line check
    Proving every individual input works correctly before a show. Part 11 · §19.5
    Load-in / load-out
    Getting the kit into the venue at the start, and back onto the truck at the end — steps 3 and 7 of the hire lifecycle. §1.2 · §19.3
    LOLER
    UK regulations governing the inspection of lifting equipment. §13.3
    Lone working
    Doing a task without anyone else present or nearby — changes what counts as a safe way to do it. §13.12
    LTC
    Linear timecode — an audio-rate sync signal used to lock playback to video. §9.3
    LUFS
    A loudness measurement standard used for broadcast and streaming normalisation. §14.6
    MADI
    A simple, fixed 56/64-channel digital audio link over one coax or fibre cable, with no network configuration. §8.2
    Manual handling
    The regulations and practical habits around lifting and moving things safely by hand. §13.4
    Matrix
    A mixer of mixes — takes existing buses and builds extra outputs from them, for delay stacks, fills, broadcast or the foyer. §7.3
    Metadata (field recording)
    The contextual information attached to an observation or recording: date, time, place, habitat, weather, equipment and identification confidence. Good metadata makes a conservation record useful. Outside work
    MEWP
    Mobile Elevating Work Platform — a "cherry picker" or scissor lift used to fly PA or work at height. A trained, ticketed role; not something to have a go at unsupervised. §13.1 · §13.3
    Mic level
    The very small voltage produced directly by a microphone, needing a preamp. §2.2
    Milan (AVB)
    An open network audio standard used natively by d&b, L-Acoustics and Meyer — a separate ecosystem from Dante, not a variant of it. §8.2
    MTC
    MIDI timecode — a data-based sync signal video systems can send to lock playback, distinct from the audio-rate LTC. §9.3
    Multicore
    A single cable bundling many audio channels together — also called a snake. §3.5 · §9.5
    Multipath
    The same RF signal arriving by two different paths and cancelling itself at one point in space — the usual cause of a wireless dropout at one specific spot on stage. True diversity defeats it. §5.3 · §5.9
    Network isolation
    Keeping a production audio network physically or logically separate from a venue's guest WiFi or public internet, so it can't be seen or interfered with from outside. §8.6
    Noise at Work (exposure action values)
    The UK regulations protecting your own hearing over a career — 80/85/87 dB(A). §13.5
    OSC (Open Sound Control)
    A lightweight open protocol for sending cues and control messages over IP between different systems — lighting, show control, consoles. Not an audio-transport protocol. §8.6 · §10.4 · §9.8
    PA
    Public Address — the loudspeaker system delivering sound to the audience. Part 6 · §11.1 · §12.13
    Pad
    A fixed attenuator for very hot sources, applied before the preamp. §7.1
    Palette (lighting)
    A saved, reusable Titan setting (colour, position or beam) that cues reference rather than copy. §9.10
    Pan
    A channel's position in the stereo image. §7.1
    Parametric EQ (PEQ)
    EQ with adjustable frequency, gain and bandwidth (Q) per band — surgical, and the main working tool on a channel. §7.5 · §15.8
    Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM)
    Surveying wildlife by leaving autonomous recorders in a habitat and analysing what was captured, rather than observing in person. Outside work · §18.15
    PAT testing
    Portable Appliance Testing — one common method of meeting electrical safety duties, with no fixed legal interval. §13.8
    Patch list
    Where each input physically lands — stagebox, socket, console channel. §1.5 · §11.2
    PFL
    Pre-Fade Listen — solo mode that ignores the channel's fader position. §7.6
    Phantom power
    The +48 V DC supply sent up a mic cable to power condenser mics and active DIs. §2.6 · §12.9
    Phase
    A frequency-dependent time relationship between two signals, distinct from polarity. §2.8 · §12.6
    PLASA (NELT)
    The UK entertainment technology trade body; National Event Lifting Training is its accessible entry point into rigging. §17.4 · §18.12
    PLI
    Public Liability Insurance — covers a freelancer if their work injures someone or damages property. §17.2
    PMR446
    The licence-exempt short-range walkie-talkie band around 446 MHz — a common uncoordinated RF neighbour on site. §5.9
    PMSE
    Programme Making and Special Events — Ofcom's category for the spectrum wireless mics and IEMs use. PMSE rules change; always check the current Ofcom guidance rather than assuming a band is still available. §5.4 · §18.5
    Polar pattern
    The shape of a microphone's sensitivity to sound arriving from different directions. §4.2
    Polarity
    Whether a waveform is the right way up — flipping it inverts the whole signal instantly. §2.8 · §11.3 · §12.10
    PPE
    Personal protective equipment — steel toes, hi-vis, hearing protection, gloves. What's required varies by site and task. §13.11
    Programmer (lighting)
    Titan's temporary workspace holding fixture changes until they're recorded into a cue or cleared. §9.10
    PRS for Music / PPL
    UK music licensing bodies — PRS covers the songwriter/composer side, PPL the recording/performer side. Recording or streaming copyrighted music can need permission beyond a venue's standard performance licence. §14.11
    PSA
    Production Services Association — the trade body built around the live-event crew role, with a competency card scheme and apprenticeship route. §17.4 · §18.12
    Purple Guide
    The HSE-endorsed, industry-written reference for health, safety and welfare at music and other events. §13.1 · §18.8
    PUWER
    UK regulations governing the safe provision and use of work equipment generally. §13.3
    QLab
    Mac cue-based playback and show-control software — the theatre and events standard for firing audio, video and lighting cues in sequence from a spacebar. Free to use; paid licences unlock more simultaneous output channels. §8.4 · §10.4 · §18.13
    QoS (Quality of Service)
    Switch-level priority tagging that lets urgent traffic — Dante's clock and audio packets — jump the queue ahead of everything else sharing the same network. §8.2
    Quarantine (equipment)
    Clearly separating faulty or suspect kit from working stock so it cannot return to a job by accident. Label the symptom and report it through the company process. People · §11.5
    RAMS
    Risk Assessment & Method Statement — the standard safety paperwork for a task with meaningful risk. §13.7 · §1.5
    RCD
    Residual Current Device — trips on an earth leakage imbalance to protect against electric shock, a different failure mode from an overcurrent breaker or fuse. §3.8 · §12.15
    RDM (Remote Device Management)
    A bidirectional extension of DMX512 that lets a compatible console discover, monitor and remotely address fixtures over the same cable. §9.7
    Redline
    Marking up the plan (stage plan, patch list, input list) with what was actually done on site, not just what was originally planned — handed over so the next crew or advance isn't working from stale paperwork. §1.5
    Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005
    The fire safety law for non-domestic premises in England and Wales — Scotland and Northern Ireland use separate legislation. §13.10
    Reverberation time (RT60)
    How long sound takes to decay by 60 dB after the source stops — the single most useful number for how live or dead a room is. §6.15
    RF
    Radio frequency — the world of wireless mics and IEM transmission. Part 5 · §12.11 · §18.5
    Rider
    An artist's or client's requested technical needs — aspirational rather than contractual. §1.5
    Ringing out
    Deliberately finding and cutting a monitor's feedback-prone frequencies before a mic is anywhere near it. §6.9 · §15.8
    Room mode (standing wave)
    A low-frequency resonance set by a room’s dimensions, making bass strong in some positions and absent in others with nothing wrong with the system. §6.15
    RTA
    Real Time Analyser — shows frequency content live. Useful for spotting a feedback or problem frequency fast; not a substitute for listening. §7.5 · §8.3 · §15.8
    Safety Advisory Group (SAG)
    The local authority, police, fire and other agencies who jointly review a bigger or higher-risk event and can set or tighten licence conditions such as noise limits. §6.10
    Sample rate
    How many times per second a digital audio signal is measured — 48 kHz is the live standard. §2.9 · §14.4 · §12.14
    Scene / snapshot
    A stored state of the console that can be recalled — essential in theatre and corporate, used sparingly in live music. §7.3 · §7.4
    Show file
    A saved snapshot of every setting on a digital console. §7.4
    SM
    Stage Manager — runs what happens on stage during a show, calls cues in theatre. §1.3
    Smaart
    Rational Acoustics' dual-channel FFT software — the industry standard for live system measurement and tuning. Paid, with widely respected training. §8.3 · §6.8 · §18.6
    Soundscape ecology
    The study of sound in an environment, including biological sounds, weather and human activity. It is a possible later bridge between audio skills and conservation, not a replacement for live-audio foundations. Outside work
    Speaker level
    The high-voltage signal between a power amp and a passive speaker. §2.2
    speakON
    The locking connector standard used for speaker-level connections. §3.3 · §12.13
    Spectrogram
    A visual plot of frequency against time, with amplitude shown by brightness or colour — the standard way to read a recording in both system measurement and bioacoustics. §8.3 · §18.15
    Squelch
    The threshold below which a wireless receiver mutes rather than passing noise. Set as low as reliably possible. §5.8 · §5.9
    Stage plan
    A top-down drawing of stage positions, wedges, DIs and power. §1.5
    Stagebox
    The numbered socket box at the stage end of a multicore or digital snake, where stage sources are plugged in. §3.5 · §11.2
    Stock label
    The company’s own identifier on an item, case or cable—often a barcode, asset number or label scheme. Record it exactly when logging a fault or matching an equipment-system export to physical stock. Equipment-system handover
    SWL / WLL
    Safe Working Load / Working Load Limit — the rated maximum for a piece of lifting equipment. Never exceeded, never guessed. §6.5 · §13.3
    System check
    Proving the PA itself works before any input is checked. §11.1 · §19.6
    T-coil
    The coil inside a hearing aid that picks up a hearing loop's magnetic field directly, bypassing room noise. §6.11
    Talkback
    A mic at the console routed to monitors, comms or the stage. §7.6
    Tech spec
    The client's or artist's contractual technical requirements. §1.5
    Tie line
    Permanent cabling installed in a venue between fixed points, so you can get a signal across the building without running your own cable. §9.5 · §1.3
    Titan (Avolites)
    Avolites' lighting control software — runs on their consoles and, via Titan PC Suite, on a normal PC. §9.8 · §9.10 · §17.4
    Titan PC Suite
    The free software package that puts the full Titan environment (plus the Simulator) on a Windows PC. §9.9 · §18.10
    Touch tour
    A pre-show session letting blind and partially-sighted patrons handle set pieces and props to build a mental picture before the performance. §6.13
    TRS / TS / TRRS (jack)
    Quarter-inch/mini jack connector types by contact count: TS carries one unbalanced signal, TRS either one balanced signal or two unbalanced, TRRS a stereo-plus-mic headset signal. The same connector can mean different things depending on the socket. §3.2
    Universe (DMX)
    One DMX line's worth of 512 control channels. §9.7 · §9.10
    Virtual soundcheck
    Building a mix from a recorded multitrack instead of a live band on stage. §14.3
    VocalEyes
    A UK charity providing audio description and other access services to arts venues. §6.13
    Wedge
    A floor monitor speaker angled up at a performer. §15.2 · §15.6
    Working at Height Regulations 2005
    The duty covering anywhere a fall could cause injury — ladders, MEWPs, rigging above head height. §13.1 · §13.3
    XLR
    The three-pin locking connector carrying almost all mic and line-level audio in live sound. §3.1 · §12.2 · §9.7
