PART 13

Work safe & meet the standard

UK law and industry practice in plain terms: know your responsibility, the paperwork and when to ask someone qualified.

13.2 Electricity at Work Regulations 1989

The foundational duty: electrical systems must be constructed and maintained so as to prevent danger, and anyone working on or near them must be competent to do so. It doesn't specify exact test intervals or methods — it sets the outcome (safe equipment, competent people) and leaves the "how often" to risk-based guidance, which is why §13.8 on PAT testing looks the way it does.

For the temporary power distribution itself — as opposed to the individual appliances plugged into it — the industry code of practice that puts flesh on this regulation is BS 7909, covered alongside power distribution in §3.8.

13.3 LOLER, PUWER & Working at Height

Any motor, chain hoist, shackle, sling or spreader beam you use to lift anything is "lifting equipment" under LOLER, and needs a periodic thorough examination by a competent person, documented, before it's used.

Lifting accessories (slings, shackles, hooks) & anything lifting people
Thorough examination every 6 months.
Lifting machines used only for goods (most chain motors, hoists)
Thorough examination every 12 months, unless a written examination scheme specifies otherwise.

PUWER is the broader regulation covering all work equipment, not just lifting gear — it's why a company drill, a pallet truck or a table saw in the warehouse has to be suitable for the job, properly maintained, and only used by someone trained on it.

Standard

Never use a motor, chain, sling or shackle whose inspection tag is missing or out of date, and never improvise a lifting accessory from something not rated for the job (a length of rope instead of a proper sling, for instance). This one's non-negotiable — it's the regulation that exists because gravity doesn't negotiate either.

The Working at Height Regulations 2005 are the separate duty covering the person, not the equipment — anywhere a fall could cause injury, from a step ladder patching a stagebox to a MEWP flying a PA. The core requirement is straightforward: avoid work at height where reasonably practicable, use the right equipment (proper access rather than an improvised stack of cases), and make sure anyone working at height is trained and competent for the specific task and equipment involved.

Watch it

As a newer tech you're very unlikely to be flying PA or working from a MEWP yourself in your first year or two — that's a trained, often ticketed role. If you're ever asked to go up something you haven't been trained on, it's completely reasonable to say so rather than quietly having a go. Nobody experienced will think less of you for it; they'll think a lot less of you for not saying anything and getting hurt.

13.4 Manual handling

Flightcases, speaker stacks and cable drums are heavy, awkward, and the single most common source of injury in this job — far more so than electrical faults. The regulations require employers to assess and reduce risk where reasonably practicable, but the practical version is mostly common sense applied consistently:

  • Assess the load before you lift it — weight, shape, how far, how many people.
  • Use trolleys, ramps and team lifts rather than muscling something alone because asking for help feels slow.
  • Lift with your legs, keep the load close to your body, avoid twisting under load.
  • Know your own limits on a twelve-hour day — the tenth case feels heavier than the first, and that's exactly when backs go.

13.5 Noise at Work — protecting your own hearing

This is the regulation about your safety, not the audience's. Long-term exposure to loud sound damages hearing permanently and cumulatively — the risk you're managing here is a career-length one, not a single-show one.

80 dB(A) — lower exposure action value
Employer must assess risk and provide information, training and (on request) hearing protection.
85 dB(A) — upper exposure action value
Employer must provide hearing protection and mark out hearing protection zones.
87 dB(A) — exposure limit value
The absolute ceiling, accounting for any protection worn. Must never be exceeded.

The regulations also set peak sound-pressure values for sudden loud bangs: 135 dB(C) lower action, 137 dB(C) upper action, 140 dB(C) limit. These daily figures are averaged exposure values over a working day, not instantaneous levels — a loud band soundcheck plus a loud show plus loud monitors easily adds up to a daily average well past 85 dB(A), even if no single moment felt dangerous.

Standard

Wear hearing protection during system tuning, soundcheck and load-in tool use as a matter of routine — musicians' earplugs (flat attenuation, doesn't dull the tone) are cheap and worth having a set of in your bag permanently. Protecting your hearing at 22 is what lets you still do this job at 55. Mixing loud all afternoon also drifts your judgement, not just your hearing — §16.7.

Current source

The figures above are from the UK Control of Noise at Work Regulations. The official HSE noise guidance confirms the 80 dB(A) lower action value, 85 dB(A) upper action value and 87 dB(A) exposure limit. If your regular work is loud, ask the employer about risk assessment, protection and hearing checks rather than trying to judge exposure by ear.

13.6 CDM 2015 and events

CDM (Construction, Design and Management) 2015 applies to more than building sites — HSE guidance explicitly covers "the building, fitting out and taking down of temporary structures for TV, film and theatre productions and live events." A stage, a truss rig, a marquee and a temporary FOH structure are all "construction" in this legal sense.

In practice this means bigger events carry a Principal Designer and Principal Contractor role, a construction phase plan, and formal coordination of the build/de-rig — usually managed by the Production Manager, not by you directly. Your part in it is mostly the RAMS you're handed (§13.7) and following the site rules they set.

13.7 RAMS — reading and writing one

Risk Assessment & Method Statement. The risk assessment identifies what could go wrong and how likely/severe it is; the method statement describes how the work will actually be done to control that risk. Together they're the standard safety paperwork for any event task with meaningful risk — rigging, working at height, electrical builds.

As a reader
You'll be handed RAMS for tasks you're doing and asked to confirm you've read and understood them, sometimes with a signature. Actually read them — they're specific to that site and that task, not generic boilerplate, even when they look similar week to week.
As a (junior) writer
Early on you're unlikely to be writing RAMS solo, but you'll contribute to them — flagging a hazard, describing how you'll actually do a task. Be specific: "two-person lift, case weighs approximately 40 kg, route checked clear" is useful; "be careful" is not.

13.8 PAT testing in practice

Widely assumed to be a fixed annual legal requirement. It isn't. PAT (Portable Appliance Testing) is one common method of meeting the actual duty in the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 — that equipment is maintained in a safe condition — but the regulations themselves specify no fixed interval, and HSE is explicit that testing isn't a blanket annual legal requirement. The right frequency is risk-based: how the equipment is used, how harsh the environment is, how often it moves.

Also seen

In practice, most hire companies test far more often than any legal minimum requires, because kit that travels constantly, gets dropped, and plugs into unknown venue power genuinely does fail more often than a lamp in an office. Follow your company's PAT schedule as the actual policy — it's usually more conservative than the law strictly demands, which is the right way round for touring kit.

13.9 Long days — working in a way you can keep doing

Everything else in this part protects you from the kit. This one protects you from the schedule. Twelve- and fourteen-hour days, early starts after late finishes, irregular weeks and a culture that quietly rewards never complaining are ordinary in this industry, and they cause far more harm across a career than any single piece of equipment. §13.5 is about not losing your hearing by 55; this is about still wanting to do the job at 55. §13.15 covers what the law actually entitles you to underneath all of this, if you want the detail.

Fatigue
It is a safety issue, not a character issue. Tired people misjudge lifts, mis-patch outputs and walk under loads. If you are too tired to do something safely — a lift, a ladder, a long drive — saying so is the professional response, not the weak one. Nobody sensible has ever been sacked for that; people have been badly hurt for not saying it.
The drive home
Usually the most dangerous part of the day. The genuinely serious risk in live events is not the show, it's a 1am motorway drive after a fourteen-hour day. Plan the journey home as part of the job: share driving, take the break, or say up front that you need accommodation rather than discovering at midnight that you can't safely drive. Microsleeps do not announce themselves.
Eat and drink
Do it on purpose. Covered as etiquette in §1.6, but it's a safety point too. Dehydration and skipped meals show up as poor decisions and short temper long before you notice them as hunger. Water in your bag (§19.17), and eat when catering is open rather than when you feel like it.
Irregular work
It carries its own load. Freelance and casual work adds uncertainty — not knowing next month's diary, saying yes to everything in case the phone stops ringing, no sick pay. That's a real and well-recognised pressure in this industry, not a personal failing, and it's worth naming rather than absorbing quietly.
Standard

Learn to say the useful version of no: "I can do the derig, but I'm not driving after it", or "I've been on since six — can someone else take the ladder?" It's specific, it offers a solution, and it's much easier to hear than a flat refusal. Crew chiefs would far rather rearrange a job than fill in an accident report.

Also seen

The UK live-events industry has its own support organisations, which is worth knowing before you need them rather than after. Music Support works with people in music and live events on mental health and addiction; Backup — the Technical Entertainment Charity gives financial help to technical professionals hit by illness, injury or hardship; and Stagehand is the welfare charity attached to the PSA (§17.4). Contact details and eligibility change, so look up their current pages rather than trusting a number printed anywhere — including here.

Watch it

Alcohol is unusually available in this line of work — bars, riders, wrap drinks, and a strong tradition of decompressing after a show. That's fine for plenty of people and genuinely isn't for others, particularly alongside irregular hours and stress. If it ever stops feeling like a choice, the organisations above exist precisely for that and talk to people in this industry every day.

13.10 Site induction, fire & emergency basics

Every site should give you a proper induction before you start working — even a five-minute one on a small job. If nobody offers one, ask; it's a completely normal question, not an awkward one.

What a decent induction covers
Where the fire exits and assembly point are, who the site's fire marshal/first aider is, what to do if the fire alarm goes (stop work, leave by the nearest exit, don't go back for kit), and any site-specific hazards (a pit, a raised stage edge, restricted areas).
Keep it clear as you work
Never stack cases, run cable or park a flightcase across a fire exit or in front of an extinguisher, even "just for five minutes" during a fast changeover.
Who's actually responsible
Fire safety at the venue is legally the "responsible person"'s job — usually the venue or the event organiser, not you — but everyone on site still needs to know where to go if the alarm sounds, and you're expected to follow their evacuation procedure without waiting to be told twice.
Go deeper

Fire safety in non-domestic premises in England and Wales sits under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005; Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own separate legislation. The detail varies by nation and by venue, so treat this as "know the site's actual procedure", not as a number to memorise from here.

13.11 PPE — what's actually expected of you

Personal protective equipment in this job is mostly unglamorous and mostly non-negotiable once a site asks for it. None of this is new if you've read this book cover to cover — it's gathered here as the one place to check what you actually need before you turn up.

Footwear
Steel-toe (safety) boots for load-in, build and de-rig on most sites — a dropped case or a rolled flightcase wheel is a genuinely common injury. Confirm with whoever's booking you whether a site requires them (§1.6).
Hi-vis
Standard for load-in and de-rig, especially anywhere vehicles are moving. Comes off once doors open and the show look matters (§1.6).
Hearing protection
Yours to bring, not usually issued — see §13.5 for why it matters and what to actually use.
Gloves
Worth having for cable and case handling — protects against splinters, pinch points and sharp rack ears, and gives you a better grip on a wet loading bay ramp. Not usually mandatory, but cheap insurance.
Standard

If a site tells you PPE is required for a task, that's not a suggestion. If you don't have something a site requires, say so before you start rather than working without it.

Your rights

Since 6 April 2022 the PPE at Work Regulations cover workers as well as employees — so if you're casual crew rather than genuinely self-employed, PPE the job requires (boots for a site that demands them, hearing protection in a loud zone) must be provided free by whoever engages you, not bought by you. Genuinely self-employed freelancers provide their own.

13.12 Working alone — early starts and late finishes

You'll sometimes be first in or last out — an early advance visit, a solo van run, a de-rig finishing long after everyone else has left. Working alone isn't against the rules, but it changes what "safe" looks like.

  • Tell someone where you are and roughly when you expect to finish — a message to a colleague or your production manager before you go into an empty venue is enough, not a formal check-in system.
  • Keep your phone charged and on you, not in a flightcase across the room, especially if you're working at height or lifting alone.
  • Don't take on a task alone that genuinely needs two people — a heavy lift, anything at height — just because nobody else is around. Wait, or ask, rather than improvise it solo.
  • Know how you'd actually get help if something went wrong — is the venue occupied by anyone else, is there a landline, does your phone have signal in that building.

13.13 RIDDOR — reporting accidents and near-misses

The Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations 2013 is the law behind why an accident or a near-miss on site gets written down and, sometimes, reported to the HSE. The duty to actually report sits with the employer or the venue's "responsible person," not with you — but you're often the one who saw it happen, so knowing it matters is your part of the job.

What's reportable
Broadly: deaths, specified serious injuries, any injury that puts someone off normal work for more than seven days, and "dangerous occurrences" — incidents that could easily have caused serious harm even if nobody was actually hurt (a dropped case from height, a partial rig failure, an electric shock). Deciding exactly what qualifies is not your call to make.
Your actual job
Make the area safe, get help if needed, and tell your supervisor or the venue's responsible person what happened — including near-misses, not just injuries. A "nothing happened in the end" near-miss (a case that nearly fell, a cable that nearly got someone) is exactly the kind of thing this system exists to catch before it happens again with worse luck.
Write it down
Time, what happened, what you saw, what was done immediately after. Your own factual note protects you as much as anyone else if it's ever followed up later.
Watch it

Never decide for yourself that something "wasn't serious enough to mention." Report it up and let the people whose job it is to judge reportability make that call — it costs you nothing, and staying quiet about a near-miss is how the same thing happens again to someone less lucky.

13.14 COSHH — hazardous substances on site

The Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002 cover anything that could harm you through breathing it in, touching it or swallowing it — which, on an event site, includes things you might not immediately think of as chemicals: haze and fog fluid, cleaning solvents, battery contents, and confetti or pyrotechnic residue.

The employer's duty
Assess the risk of any hazardous substance in use and control it — proper ventilation for haze, the right PPE, safe storage. Again, not a decision you're expected to make alone.
Your actual job
Before handling anything you don't recognise — a cleaning product, a fluid, a canister — check it has a safety data sheet and that you know what it is. Never mix cleaning products (some combinations release genuinely dangerous gases). Ventilate a haze/fog-filled space properly before extended time working in it, and treat spent pyro/confetti debris as something to clear up with gloves, not bare hands.
Also seen

Haze and fog machines are covered by COSHH like anything else, but the specific competence and risk-assessment requirements for who's allowed to fire pyro or confetti sit separately with the licensed operator (§9.12) — COSHH is about the substance itself, not who's authorised to deploy it.

13.15 Working Time Regulations — your legal hours entitlement

§13.9 covers handling long days as they come at you; this is what the law actually entitles you to underneath that, whether or not any single job feels like it.

The 48-hour week
The baseline limit is an average of 48 hours a week, averaged over 17 weeks — not a hard cap on any single week, which is exactly why a run of 14-hour build days is legal provided it evens out.
The opt-out
Almost every event and entertainment employer asks new starters to sign a voluntary opt-out from the 48-hour limit, precisely because show days routinely exceed it. Signing it is normal in this industry and not, on its own, something to worry about — but know that it must be voluntary and in writing (best kept separate from your main contract), and that you can cancel it — with 7 days' notice, or longer if your opt-out says so, up to a maximum of 3 months.
Daily & weekly rest
Separately from the 48-hour limit: you're entitled to 11 consecutive hours' rest in any 24-hour period, and 24 hours' uninterrupted rest in any 7 days (or 48 hours in any 14). A very early get-in after a very late derig is exactly the situation where this gets squeezed — worth knowing it's there, even on a job where asking for it isn't realistic.
Rest breaks
A 20-minute uninterrupted break if you work more than 6 hours in a day. In practice this usually means "eat something properly," not a formal announced break — but it's a legal entitlement, not a favour.
Also seen

None of this is about refusing a long day — the industry runs on them, and §13.9's advice on managing that stands. This section exists so you know the actual floor underneath the culture, not to suggest you should be citing regulations on a get-in.

Holiday
5.6 weeks' paid holiday a year (28 days for a five-day week), pro rata. Casual and irregular-hours crew build it up at 12.07% of hours worked; employers may instead pay it "rolled up" as an extra 12.07% on each payslip, which should be shown separately.
Sick pay
Since 6 April 2026, Statutory Sick Pay is paid from the first day off sick (no more three waiting days) and there's no minimum earnings to qualify. For 2026/27 it's £123.25 a week or 80% of your average weekly earnings, whichever is lower. It applies to employees — genuinely self-employed freelancers don't get SSP, which is one reason to keep some savings.
Zero-hours work
From April 2027 (Employment Rights Act 2025), zero- and low-hours workers get the right to be offered guaranteed hours that reflect what they regularly work, reasonable notice of shifts, and compensation when a shift is cancelled or cut short at late notice. Details are still being set — check ACAS nearer the time.
Under 18
Young workers have tighter limits: normally no more than 8 hours a day and 40 a week, longer rest and a 30-minute break after 4.5 hours — and no opt-out.

13.16 Martyn's Law and event security

The Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025, known as Martyn's Law, makes venues and events prepare for a terrorist attack. It's expected to come into force in spring 2027, regulated by the Security Industry Authority (SIA).

  • Standard tier — premises or events expecting 200–799 people at once: they must notify the SIA and have public protection procedures (evacuation, invacuation, lockdown, communication), so far as reasonably practicable.
  • Enhanced tier — 800 or more: they must also put protective measures in place, document them and name a senior responsible person.

It's the venue or organiser's duty, not yours — but you'll be part of it:

  • Expect security briefings in inductions, and know the evacuation, invacuation and lockdown signals for that venue.
  • Your PA and radio mics may be part of the communication plan — the show-stop or announcement mic must work, and you must know who can use it.
  • Don't leave cases, bags or kit unattended in public areas; report anything suspicious to security, not on social media.
Sources · checked Nov 2026

GOV.UK (Home Office / SIA), New tools to support preparation for Martyn's Law (Nov 2026); ProtectUK Martyn's Law overview; Home Office statutory guidance (2026). Commencement dates can move — check GOV.UK.

13.17 Temporary power at events — BS 7909 in brief

Temporary power for events is designed, installed and certified under BS 7909 (the current edition is BS 7909:2023+A1:2024), alongside the wiring regulations (BS 7671) and HSE guidance GS50. It's the power technician's or electrician's job — knowing the basics tells you what to expect and what not to touch.

  • Every temporary system is inspected and tested before it's switched on, and certified. On a multi-day event it's re-inspected, and the certificate reissued.
  • Socket circuits you plug into should have 30 mA RCD protection.
  • Generators create their own earth on site — that's why earth rods and bonding appear, and why you never disturb them.
  • Cables are fully unwound (coiled cable heats up), protected where people walk, and not daisy-chained from extension to extension.
Watch it

If the distro trips, tell the power tech — don't reset breakers on someone else's distribution, and never swap fuses or bypass an RCD. See §12.15.

Sources · checked Nov 2026

BS 7909:2023+A1:2024 (BSI); HSE GS50; event electrical safety guides (2026). Read the standard itself or ask the power tech for anything beyond these basics.
