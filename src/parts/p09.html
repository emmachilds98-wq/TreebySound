PART 9

Working with other teams

Lighting, video and staging share your space, routes, power and comms. Clear boundaries and good handovers make every department better.

9.1 Why this gets its own part

Everything so far has been your chain: mic to speaker. But you build that chain in a room where two or three other departments are building theirs at the same time, often through the same doorway, up the same truss, and off the same distro. Most of what goes wrong between departments isn't technical — it's someone not knowing what someone else needed, or two cable runs fighting for the same route. The technical bits (this part covers three real ones: electrical interference, timecode, and DMX) are the minority of the problem. The majority is just talking to people early.

Two more things sit at the end of this part, and neither is "working with other teams" content in the same sense as the rest of it — they're here because they're genuine, marketable skills on top of your audio work. §9.8–9.11 is a proper look at Avolites Titan, the lighting-control software, if you've got the licence to use it. §9.13–9.16 does the same for video — signal routing, vision mixing and LED walls — for whenever that side of the job pulls at you (§21.6 covers taking either further as a second discipline).

Standard

On any multi-department build, find out in the first hour: who's the LX/video/staging equivalent of your crew chief, where their cable is planning to run, and whether anyone's sharing your power. Five minutes of that conversation saves an afternoon of re-routing.

9.2 Lighting — noise, truss and real estate

You'll spend more time coordinating with LX than any other department, because you're fighting over the same physical space: the same truss to hang things from, the same cable tray or floor route, often the same power.

Dimmer noise
Old-style dimmers chop the AC waveform to control brightness, and that chopping radiates as buzz. It couples into anything running near it — which is the practical reason (alongside sharing a hum-prone supply, §3.8) that audio and lighting cable shouldn't be run in the same tray for long distances. LED fixtures with switch-mode drivers can do the same thing at a higher, whinier frequency.
Cable routing
Where you can't avoid a crossing, cross lighting and audio cable at right angles, not parallel — a 90° crossing picks up almost nothing, a long parallel run picks up plenty. Where you're both running the same direction for any distance, ask for separate trunking or at least separate sides of the tray.
Truss and rigging points
Delay speakers, hanging mics and comms often go on LX truss, not your own. That's a rigging conversation with whoever's calling the motors — know your box's weight and always confirm before you hang anything on someone else's point.
Comms
On bigger shows LX will have their own channel — know how to reach it. "Sound to LX" gets used constantly during a get-in, mostly to ask who's got the last free spot on the truss.
Watch it

If a wireless mic or IEM system starts buzzing or dropping the moment the lighting rig is powered up and tested, don't assume it's coincidence — it usually isn't. Report it early rather than fighting it all afternoon; a moved cable run or a different dimmer curve often fixes it in minutes once someone knows to look.

9.3 Video & AV

Video is the department you'll most often be feeding audio to, or taking it from.

Timecode
Where playback needs to stay locked to a video track — a corporate film, a themed show, a broadcast — video usually generates LTC (linear timecode, an audio-rate signal you can literally patch down an XLR) or sends MTC (MIDI timecode) to your playback system. Your job is normally just to receive and chase it, not generate it.
Embedded audio
Broadcast and streaming feeds often carry audio embedded inside the video signal (SDI) rather than as a separate cable. If a broadcast truck is involved, expect to be handing them a clean mix to embed, on their schedule, not yours.
Playback content with its own audio
VT, adverts and video content usually arrive with a soundtrack that needs to land on a specific console channel, at a level nobody's checked. Ask for it in advance and gain-stage it like anything else — video editors mix for headphones, not a PA.
Screens as a noise source
LED walls and their processing gear can be electrically noisy in the same way dimmers are. If you're running mic cable near a screen wall, treat it the same as a lighting run.
Standard

Get any video content with embedded audio onto your system and through a level check before doors, the same as you would a band. "It'll be fine, it's just a video" is how a screaming-loud advert happens live.

9.4 Staging, rigging & backline

The staging and rigging crew build the physical platform everything else sits on, and backline is the world of the instruments themselves — often the band's own tech, sometimes yours to provide and mic.

Backline
Guitar and bass amps, drum kits, keyboard rigs. If it's your job to mic it, that's Part 4 territory (§4.5 has placement); if it's the band's own tech's job, your role is patching their DIs and mic feeds into your system and agreeing stage volume with them — loud backline is one of the most common reasons a mix fights itself at FOH.
Risers & decking
Drum risers in particular need cable routed through them, not just to them — plan that before the deck goes down, because pulling a riser apart mid-build to feed a cable through is a bad afternoon.
Rigging calls
If any of your kit is flying (delay speakers, hung mics, truss-mounted anything), you'll be in the room for "hands in the air" — everyone confirms clear before a motor moves. Never wander off during a lift, and never assume someone else has checked your gear is clear.
Barrier & FOH position
Staging or event crew usually build your FOH riser and the barrier in front of the stage. If your desk position, sightline or cable route isn't what you were expecting, raise it as soon as you see the plan, not once it's built.

9.5 Shared infrastructure — routes, trays and colour

On anything bigger than a small corporate, several departments' cable ends up sharing the same physical path: a cable ramp across a doorway, a tray up a wall, a route through a riser. Getting this right is mostly etiquette plus a bit of planning.

  • Plan routes together at the start, not cable-by-cable as each department gets to it. Whoever's coordinating the build should know roughly where power, audio, lighting and video each want to go before the first cable hits the floor.
  • Colour-code where you can. Many companies use coloured tape or coloured cable for different disciplines — green for Dante/network, a specific tape colour for audio versus lighting DMX versus power. Learn your company's convention on day one; it saves enormous confusion at derig when three departments are coiling cable off the same tray.
  • Label at both ends. A cable that's clearly yours saves someone else's time as much as it saves your own.
  • Don't block a shared route with your own kit — a flightcase left across a tie-line ramp becomes everyone's problem, not just yours.
Also seen

On a small job this whole section is one conversation over a cup of tea before the get-in. It's only on multi-day, multi-department builds that formal cable route plans and colour conventions really earn their keep — but the habit is worth building early regardless of scale.

9.6 Power beyond your own rig

You'll usually only be responsible for your own audio distro, but it helps to recognise what's happening around it.

Generators
Off-grid events run on generators rather than mains. Generator power sags under sudden load — a big lighting cue hitting at the same moment as a bass-heavy song can occasionally cause an audible dip. It's not something you fix; it's something worth recognising so you don't chase a phantom console fault.
Load balancing
Three-phase power needs roughly equal load across all three legs, or one phase trips while the others sit comfortably under capacity. This is normally the power department's job to manage, but if you're asked which phase your rig is on, know the answer.
Separate audio supply
As covered in §3.8, the standard ask is a power feed dedicated to audio, ideally off its own phase, so lighting and motor loads aren't sharing your earth reference. Ask for this at the advance stage, not on the day — by the time you're on site the distro's usually already built.

9.7 DMX — the protocol you'll keep meeting

You don't need to operate lighting, but DMX turns up often enough at the edges of your world — shared connectors, shared trunking, the occasional "can you just patch this through your snake" — that it's worth knowing what it actually is.

What it is
DMX512 is a one-way digital control protocol carrying up to 512 channels of control data (dimmer levels, moving-light positions, colour, whatever) down one line, refreshed dozens of times a second. It's control data, not audio — there is nothing to listen to.
The cable
Electrically it's RS-485, a balanced differential signal, run on cable rated at 120 Ω. That's the number to remember: it's why DMX cable and mic cable, though both often on 3-pin XLR, aren't really interchangeable for anything but the shortest, least critical run (§3.1).
The connector confusion
The DMX spec calls for 5-pin XLR; a huge amount of fixture-level gear ships with 3-pin instead, because it's cheaper and 3-pin XLR is everywhere. You'll meet turnaround adaptors (5-pin to 3-pin) constantly. It's not your fault when it doesn't just work — it's a known mess in the industry.
Daisy chain & termination
DMX devices link in a chain, in to out, not a star like a Dante network. The last fixture in the chain needs a 120 Ω terminator (a resistor across the data pins) or the signal reflects back down the line and things flicker or misbehave, especially over longer runs.
Universes
One DMX line carries one "universe" of 512 channels. A big rig with hundreds of fixtures needs several universes, usually distributed over the lighting network (often Art-Net or sACN over standard Ethernet) rather than run as physical DMX the whole way.
Watch it

Never patch a DMX line down a spare pair in an audio multicore as a favour without checking with whoever owns the snake. Even where it technically works over a short run, the impedance mismatch (§3.1) can cause exactly the kind of intermittent flickering that's miserable to fault-find later, and it's now tangled up with your audio cable when someone needs to trace it.

Go deeper

You don't need to learn to programme a lighting desk, but understanding DMX addressing and universes well enough to have an intelligent conversation with an LX tech is a genuinely useful hour of reading. See Part 18.

9.8 Avolites Titan — what it actually is

If you've got a Titan licence, this is worth a proper section rather than a passing mention, because it's a real, marketable skill on top of your audio work, not just a curiosity.

Titan is Avolites' lighting control software — it's not audio kit, and it's worth being precise about that distinction even though this is an audio handbook: it's the operating software that runs on Avolites' own consoles (Tiger Touch, Arena, Sapphire, Quartz and others), and it also runs as PC software so you can build and rehearse a whole show without a physical console in front of you.

Titan PC Suite
Free software from Avolites that puts the full Titan environment on a normal Windows PC — patching, programming, the works.
Titan Simulator
Part of the same suite — shows an on-screen virtual version of a specific console's physical control surface, so what you learn on your laptop maps directly onto the real hardware later.
AvoKey
A USB licence dongle. This is almost certainly the "licence" you already have — it's what lets Titan PC Suite and the Simulator run at full capability rather than in a limited demo mode.

Why this is worth your time: on any job with LX, being the audio tech who can also patch a fixture, understand what "the desk's not seeing that dimmer" actually means, or sensibly troubleshoot a DMX chain (§9.7) makes you noticeably more useful than someone who only knows their own department. It won't make you a lighting designer, but it removes the "not my department" ceiling on a lot of small, useful jobs.

9.9 Getting started with Titan PC Suite

  1. Download Titan PC Suite from Avolites' own site — search "Avolites Titan PC Suite download" and get it from avolites.com directly rather than a third-party mirror.
  2. Plug in your AvoKey before or during install — the software checks for it to unlock full functionality.
  3. Pick a console to simulate. If your company uses a specific Avolites desk, match that model in the Simulator so what you learn transfers directly. If not, any mid-range console (Tiger Touch or similar) is a sensible default to learn the concepts on.
  4. Start with a small, fake rig — patch four or five generic fixtures rather than trying to recreate a whole show. The goal early on is fluency with the workflow, not a finished show file.
  5. Work through §9.10 below in order, actually doing each step in the software rather than just reading it. This stuff is genuinely hands-based knowledge — reading about patching without patching anything barely sticks.
Standard

Treat this exactly like the console offline editors in §8.1 — a few real evenings of deliberate practice on a fake show file will put you ahead of most people who only ever touch a lighting desk live, under pressure, with no time to explore.

9.10 Titan's core concepts

Five ideas that everything else in Titan is built from. Learn these properly and the rest of the software makes sense; skip them and every screen looks like an unrelated wall of buttons.

Patching & fixture personalities
Before Titan can control a fixture, it needs to know what that fixture is — how many DMX channels it uses and what each one does. That definition is called a fixture personality, and Titan ships with a library of them for real fixtures, organised by manufacturer, model and mode (many fixtures can run in different modes using different numbers of channels).
The Programmer
A temporary workspace, not a permanent one. When you select a fixture and change something — intensity, colour, position — that change lives in the Programmer until you either record it into a cue or press Clear to wipe it. Selecting a new fixture after making changes deselects the old one, but the changes you made stay in the Programmer until cleared. This trips up every beginner at least once: forgetting the Programmer is holding leftover values from an earlier fixture, and recording them into a cue by accident.
Palettes
Saved, reusable settings — colour palettes, position palettes, and beam/gobo palettes are the three main types. Critically, a cue doesn't store a copy of the palette's values — it stores a reference to the palette. Change the palette later and every cue using it updates automatically. This is exactly why touring lighting rigs can be rebuilt fast in a new venue: reset the position palettes for the new room, and every cue that uses them follows along.
Cues & cue lists
A cue is one recorded look — a snapshot of fixture states, which can include fade-in and fade-out times and even movement. A cue list is a sequence of cues triggered in order, usually from a "Go" button on a playback fader — the lighting equivalent of your show file's scene recall (§7.4), stepping through a show moment by moment.
Grand Master & playback faders
The Grand Master is a single fader controlling the overall intensity of everything the console is outputting — pull it down and the whole rig dims proportionally, regardless of what any individual cue says. It's often disabled by default on a new show specifically so a beginner doesn't get confused wondering why nothing's appearing on stage. Individual playback faders each run a cue list or a look, with their own Flash (momentarily add to the output) and Swop (solo this playback, suppress the others) buttons alongside them.

How to patch your first fixture

  1. Open the Patch menu.
  2. Choose the fixture's manufacturer and model from the personality library, and the correct mode for how it's set on the fixture itself.
  3. Set the DMX line and start address — or let Titan auto-assign a sensible one if you don't need a specific address.
  4. Repeat for each fixture, or patch several identical fixtures at once rather than one at a time.
Watch it

The single most common beginner mistake in Titan is exactly the audio equivalent of forgetting what's in a channel's processing (§7.4): leftover values sitting in the Programmer from a fixture you're no longer thinking about, which then get baked into the next cue you record. Get into the habit of pressing Clear before you start a new piece of programming, the same way you'd check for solo before trusting a mix (§7.7).

9.11 Where this actually helps you on an audio job

  • DMX fault-finding stops being a mystery — you'll recognise a patching problem, an addressing clash, or a termination issue rather than just reporting "the lights have stopped" (§9.7).
  • You become a useful extra pair of hands during an LX build or a quick fixture swap, which is exactly the kind of cross-department usefulness §9.1 talks about.
  • It's a genuine second specialism. Plenty of small companies and venues want one person who can cover both audio and basic lighting on quieter jobs — that combination is rarer and more valuable than being deep in audio alone.
Go deeper

Avolites' own manual (manual.avolites.com) is thorough, free, and kept current with each Titan version — it's the authoritative source once you're past these basics, including exact button references for whichever console you end up on. See Part 18.

9.12 Special effects — pyro, confetti & haze

Not your department, but its effects land on your gear and its cues sit in your running order, so it's worth knowing the boundaries.

Who fires it
Always a licensed pyrotechnician or a specifically trained operator — never you, and never anyone without the qualification, whatever the time pressure. Confetti and CO2 cannons carry their own competence and risk-assessment requirements too, even though they're not explosive.
Protecting your kit
Confetti and streamer residue gets into everything within its throw radius — open mic capsules, fader mechanisms, DI box connectors. Cover or relocate anything you can before a cannon fires nearby, and budget time afterwards to actually clean gear rather than packing it away dirty.
Cueing
Pyro and confetti cues are usually fired from a dedicated, separate firing system, not your console — but the cue to fire it often comes from the same show-control chain as your own cues (§8.4), so know where in the running order it happens even though you're not the one pressing the button.
Watch it

Never agree to fire, rig, or "just hold" a pyrotechnic device, however informally you're asked, and however confident the person asking sounds. It's licensed, regulated work with its own legal competence requirements — exactly the same boundary as §13.2 draws around electrical work.

9.13 Video signals & routing — the basics

Like Titan (§9.8), this is real hands-on video knowledge rather than just "what to expect from the video department" — a second marketable skill built on signal-flow thinking you already have from audio.

HDMI vs SDI
HDMI is the consumer/laptop connector — cheap, but the connector isn't locking and the reliable cable run is short (a few metres before signal integrity gets shaky). SDI (Serial Digital Interface) is the professional standard for a reason: a locking BNC connector, a single coax that runs hundreds of feet reliably, and — like Dante for audio — it's the one actually worth learning first if you're going to do this seriously.
NDI
Video carried over a standard network switch instead of dedicated cable, the video equivalent of Dante (§8.2) — same appeal (one network, many signals, no dedicated cable run per source), same caveat (it needs bandwidth and a network that's actually built for it, not just "plugged into the venue WiFi").
Genlock & sync
Multiple cameras or sources need to change frames at exactly the same instant, or switching between them produces a visible glitch — the video equivalent of word clock keeping digital audio devices sample-locked (§2.9). A genlock generator (or one source acting as the sync reference) is what keeps everything switching cleanly.
A video router / matrix
The video equivalent of a patch bay (§3.9's DI-box patching logic, scaled up) — any input can be routed to any output, so a camera feed can land on a screen, a recording device and a streaming encoder simultaneously without re-cabling.

9.14 Vision mixing & switching

A vision mixer (switcher) is the video department's console — it takes several sources in and decides what the audience actually sees, live.

PGM & PVW
Program (PGM) is what's live, on screen, right now. Preview (PVW) is what's queued up to go next. Everything on a switcher exists to move a source from preview into program cleanly.
Cut, mix & wipe
A cut is instant, no transition. A mix (dissolve) crossfades between two sources over a set time — the video equivalent of a crossfader. A wipe reveals the new source with a moving edge. Live events lean heavily on cuts and simple mixes; elaborate wipes read as dated.
Keying
Compositing one source over another — a chroma key (green screen) removes a specific colour to reveal what's behind it; a luma key uses brightness instead, common for overlaying titles or a presenter's slides.
What to actually learn on
Blackmagic's ATEM range is the accessible, widely-used starting point in events and streaming — inexpensive, well documented, and common enough that fluency on it is a genuinely transferable skill, the video equivalent of learning Dante Certification before a specific desk brand.
Go deeper

IMAG (image magnification) — a camera feed of the stage projected onto screens so a back-of-room or large-venue audience can actually see the performer — is the video job you'll meet most often at a live event. You're unlikely to be the camera operator, but knowing the basics of framing (headroom, not centring a face awkwardly, avoiding the top of a mic stand clipping a shot) means you can competently step in for a static, single-camera IMAG shot if you're ever asked to.

9.15 LED walls & image processing

The video screens now on almost every corporate and touring stage are their own small technical world, built on the same "source, process, output" logic as an audio system.

Pixel pitch
The distance between individual LEDs, in millimetres — a smaller number means a sharper image at close range but costs more. The right pitch depends entirely on how close the nearest viewer will actually be, exactly like speaker coverage depends on room size (§6.6).
The processor
The video equivalent of a lighting desk driving fixtures (§9.10) — it takes an input source and maps it correctly onto however the physical panels are actually arranged, including oddly-shaped or curved walls, and handles brightness calibration between panels so the wall doesn't look patchy.
Content gotchas
Content built at the wrong resolution or aspect ratio for the actual wall is a constant, avoidable problem — the video version of a mix built quiet and played loud (§16.7). Confirm the wall's real dimensions before content is finalised, not on the day.

9.16 Streaming & recording — tools worth knowing

§14.6 covers what changes about your audio job when a show streams. This is the software actually doing the streaming and recording on the video side.

OBS Studio
Free, open-source, and the default starting point for streaming and screen/webinar-style recording — genuinely capable enough that plenty of small-to-mid events run on it rather than anything paid.
vMix
Paid, more capable, more common on higher-end streaming and broadcast-adjacent events — a step up once a job's requirements outgrow OBS.
