Microphones & stage capture

What you will handle most often, how it behaves, and how to place and protect it.

4.1 The three types

TypeHow it worksCharacterPhantom?Live use

CondenserCharged diaphragm and backplate act as a capacitorDetailed, extended top end, more sensitiveYesOverheads, acoustic instruments, choirs, speech, anything needing detail

Also worth knowing: large-diaphragm condensers (fuller, more flattering, more prone to handling noise) vs small-diaphragm or "pencil" condensers (more accurate, better off-axis behaviour, the standard for overheads and acoustic sources live).

Watch it

Ribbons are genuinely delicate. Don't blow into them, don't wave them around, don't send phantom to one unless the manufacturer explicitly says it's fine, and never plug one in with phantom already live. In a hire warehouse they should be in dedicated cases and handled as fragile stock.

4.2 Polar patterns

The pattern describes which directions a mic hears from. It's the most practically important spec on the box, because it determines both how much bleed you get and how much level you can get before feedback.

PatternPicks upRejectsUse for
CardioidFront, heart-shapedDirectly behindThe live default. Vocals, most close mic'ing.
SupercardioidFront, narrowerSides strongly; small rear lobeLoud stages, tight rejection. Null is ~126° off axis.
HypercardioidFront, narrowestSides very strongly; bigger rear lobeMaximum isolation. Null ~110°.
OmniEverything, equallyNothingLavs, measurement mics, room mics. No proximity effect.
Figure-8Front and backSides completelyM/S recording, two people facing each other, clever rejection tricks.
ShotgunNarrow forward lobeSides (via interference tube)Distance pickup, broadcast, drama, corporate reinforcement of a stage.
Standard

The practical use of a polar pattern is aiming the null, not just the front. With a wedge on stage, you want the mic's dead spot pointing at the wedge. For a cardioid that's straight behind the mic — so wedges go directly in front of the singer. For a super/hypercardioid the null is off to the sides at the back, so wedges go in a pair at roughly 45°. Getting this right is worth more gain before feedback than any amount of EQ.

4.3 Proximity effect & other physical realities

Proximity effect
All directional mics boost low frequencies as the source gets closer. A singer eating the mic gets a big warm boom; backing off two inches thins it out. Vocalists use this deliberately. You compensate with the high-pass filter.
Off-axis colouration
Sound arriving from the side isn't just quieter, it's differently coloured — usually duller. Good mics have smooth off-axis response; cheap ones sound honky when the singer turns their head.
Plosives
"P" and "B" sounds fire a blast of air at the diaphragm, producing a low thump. Fixed with a pop shield, a foam windshield, slight off-axis positioning, and a high-pass filter.
Handling noise
Vibration through the body of the mic. Worse on condensers and on mics without internal shockmounting. Shockmounts and stand isolation help.
Wind
Outdoors, a foam windshield is not enough. You need a proper furry ("dead cat", "windjammer"). Wind noise is huge, low-frequency, and eats your headroom.
Standard

Engage the high-pass filter on almost every channel that isn't kick, bass or a full-range source. Somewhere around 80–100 Hz on vocals, 100–120 Hz on most other things. It removes rumble, handling noise, stage vibration and plosive energy that contributes nothing but eats headroom. This is genuinely one of the highest-value habits in live sound.

Watch it

Worn mics are the most expensive-per-gram and most abused items in the warehouse. Cables fail at the connector and at the capsule, sweat kills them, and they get yanked off. Check the termination matches the transmitter brand — a DPA with a Sennheiser 3.5 mm locking jack will not work on a Shure TA4F pack. Getting the wrong terminations on a job is a classic and totally avoidable prep failure.

Also seen

There's a whole school of thought that says minimal mic'ing beats close mic'ing — two overheads and a kick mic on a jazz kit, a single pair on a string section. In small rooms and acoustic contexts this often sounds far better than 12 close mics. Don't assume more inputs is better.

4.6 The 3:1 rule, and stereo techniques

The 3:1 rule: when using multiple mics on one source or in one space, the distance between mics should be at least three times the distance from each mic to its source. This makes the bleed at least ~10 dB down, which keeps comb filtering from being audible. It's why panel discussions with a mic each sound fine and a badly-spaced pair of lectern mics sounds hollow.

TechniqueSetupCharacter
XYTwo cardioids, capsules coincident, 90°Perfectly mono-compatible, narrow image. Safe choice.
ORTFTwo cardioids, 170 mm apart, 110°Natural, wide, good for ensembles and room capture.
Spaced pair (A/B)Two omnis, 0.5–3 m apartBig and spacious; mono compatibility suffers.
Mid/Side (M/S)Cardioid forward + figure-8 sideways, matrixedAdjustable width after the fact; perfect mono fold-down. Broadcast favourite.

4.7 Stands and accessories

Tall boom
The standard vocal/overhead stand. Heavy round base or tripod.
Short boom
Snare, toms, guitar cabs, kick. You need far more of these than people ever pack.
Straight stand
Simple, stable, no boom arm. Common for backing vocals and speech.
Desk / table stand
Top tables, panels, podium.
Round base vs tripod
Round base is more stable and tidier on stage (theatre and TV prefer them); tripod is lighter and cheaper, but the legs trip people and get in the way of a drum kit.
Clip / clamp
Every mic model has its own. They go missing constantly. Count them at prep.
Shockmount
Elastic cradle isolating the mic from stand vibration. For condensers.
Pop shield
Mesh or foam screen against plosives.
Windshield / dead cat
Foam for indoors, fur for outdoors.
Watch it

Two stand failures cause most on-stage incidents: a boom arm not tightened, which slowly droops through the show until the mic is at the singer's chest; and a tall boom with too much counterweight-free extension, which topples. Tighten every clutch properly, and set the base so the weight of the boom sits over a leg, not between them.

4.8 Handling & care

  • Don't blow into a mic to test it. It's crude, it tells you almost nothing, and on a condenser or ribbon it can genuinely cause damage. Speak into it, or scratch the grille gently.
  • Don't tap the grille of a condenser either — it's a big impulse straight into a sensitive diaphragm.
  • Condensation kills condensers. Bringing a cold mic from a van into a warm room causes moisture on the diaphragm — you get crackling and low output. Let it acclimatise; don't panic and bin it.
  • Store in their cases/pouches, not loose in a drawer. Foam-lined mic cases exist for a reason.
  • Silica gel in mic cases is worth doing for expensive condensers and worn mics.
  • Clean grilles. Vocal mics get genuinely disgusting. Most unscrew — wash the grille and the foam in warm soapy water, dry completely before reassembling. This is a proper warehouse task and it matters for both sound and hygiene.
  • Handle by the body, not the grille. Grip oils and dents both cause problems.
Standard

Clean and check every vocal mic on return, not at the next prep. It's five minutes when it's part of the check-in routine and it's a crisis when you discover it at 8am with a van to load.
