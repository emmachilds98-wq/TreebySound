PART 21

Beyond live audio

Real side-steps this experience opens up, and how to explore one gradually without leaving the job you've got.

21.1 Why this experience travels further than it feels like

From inside a warehouse or a Tuesday-night club show, the job can feel like one narrow lane. It isn't. Live audio and events work builds a specific, transferable set of habits: reading a signal chain under pressure, working calmly when something breaks in front of an audience, being trusted with expensive kit and other people's schedules, and functioning as a reliable stranger on a new site every few weeks. Almost every field in this part is short of exactly that combination, more than it's short of people who already know their specific software.

None of what follows means live audio isn't a real career on its own — Part 17 is that career, properly. This part is for a different question: not "how do I get better at this," but "what else does being good at this actually open up." Some of these are a short step sideways you could make next year; some are a slow multi-year pivot; a couple are things worth just knowing exist. §21.10 is the part that matters most if any of this is more than curiosity — how to actually test one of these without quitting anything.

21.2 Broadcast & outside broadcast (OB)

Sound for television and radio, live or recorded: studio-based broadcast, and outside broadcast (OB) — the trucks and crews that cover sport, awards shows and outside events. §10.5 already covers what changes technically when a broadcast split joins your show; this is about doing that job full-time rather than feeding one.

What the job actually is
Broadcast sound assistant/A2 through to sound supervisor — rigging and de-rigging radio mics and comms on presenters and contributors, running a broadcast desk, or building and striking an OB truck's audio chain on location.
Where the audio background helps
RF and radio mic handling (Part 5) transfers almost directly — broadcast uses the same wireless discipline, often at higher channel counts. Comms and split-feed experience from §10.5 is the exact skill a truck needs.
The usual way in
Less often straight into a broadcaster, more often via a facilities or OB hire company — the businesses that supply trucks, kit and freelance crew to the broadcasters. They hire from live/events backgrounds regularly, because the on-site instincts are the same.
Worth knowing about
ScreenSkills is the UK skills body for film, TV and games, and runs bursaries and short courses relevant to broadcast sound. The National Film and Television School (NFTS) runs postgraduate sound courses if you wanted to go deeper academically later.

21.3 AV systems integration & install

§10.6 covers what it's like operating an installed system — this is building and programming them for a living, for the systems integrators who fit out boardrooms, campuses, stadiums and control rooms.

What the job actually is
Installing and commissioning fixed AV — speakers, DSPs, control systems and video — then programming how it all behaves: what a single "Start Meeting" button on a touch panel actually does behind the scenes.
Where the audio background helps
DSP routing and gain-structure thinking from §10.6 and Dante/AV-over-IP from Part 8 are most of the technical core. What install companies usually can't get elsewhere is someone who's already calm fault-finding a live system in front of a client.
The usual way in
Many installers started as riggers or commissioning-day extra hands on a live crew's quieter weeks, then moved across once a company saw they could be trusted alone with a client's boardroom.
Worth knowing about
AVIXA's CTS (§17.4) is the entry-level credential the whole install industry recognises. Crestron, QSC Q-SYS and Extron — the three control/DSP platforms you'll meet most — each run their own free or low-cost certification tracks once you're pointed at a specific one; most installers end up fluent in whichever platform their employer standardises on.

21.4 Studio, post-production & music technology

Recording and mixing music, or sound for picture — dialogue editing, ADR, foley and sound design for film and TV. A quieter, more solitary discipline than live work, built around getting one thing exactly right rather than reacting to a room in real time.

Where the audio background helps
Signal flow, mic technique and critical listening (Part 2, Part 4) are the same skills pointed at a recording rather than a PA. What's genuinely different is the pace and the editing/DAW-based workflow, which live work doesn't teach.
The usual way in
Assistant engineer roles at a studio, or — more realistically for most people — building a portfolio on your own kit at home first (§14.3 already covers multitrack recording basics) and using that to get a foot in somewhere.
Worth knowing about
The Music Producers Guild (MPG) is the UK trade body for studio and production engineers. ScreenSkills (§21.2) also covers the post-production side specifically.
Also seen

This is usually the slowest of the paths here to earn a living from directly — studio and post work is competitive and often starts unpaid or low-paid. It's a genuinely good direction to build on the side for years before it needs to pay the bills, not one to jump into cold.

21.5 Game audio & esports production

Two different things that both sit near live audio. Game audio is sound design and implementation for video games — a studio-based, technical-audio discipline. Esports production is running the live event around competitive gaming — arena shows, broadcast overlay, comms for casters and players — and is functionally a live-events job with a gaming audience, a genuinely fast-growing part of the UK events calendar.

Game audio
Built around middleware that connects sound assets to game engines — Wwise (Audiokinetic) and FMOD (Firelight Technologies) are the two you'll meet. Both run their own free, structured online certification courses (Wwise 101/201/301; FMOD's own learning courses) that are a genuinely respected way in without a games-specific degree.
Esports production
Close to §10.3 and §10.5 combined — a broadcast feed, a live room, comms, and a fast, high-pressure build. Crews are very often drawn straight from live music and corporate AV backgrounds; there isn't a separate esports-specific training route worth chasing, the transferable event skills already covered in this book are the actual qualification.

21.6 A second discipline: lighting, video & LED

Part 9 already builds real Titan fluency for lighting, mostly so it helps your audio job. Taken further, it's also a genuine second discipline in its own right — and video/LED-wall work is one of the fastest-growing, most kit-hungry corners of live production right now.

Lighting
You already have a real head start from §9.8 onwards. Going further means building actual programming speed and a design eye, not just operational familiarity — best built by programming real shows, not just the simulator.
Video & LED
Vision mixing, camera operation, and — increasingly in demand — LED wall technicians who build, calibrate and run the video screens that now appear on almost every corporate and touring stage. Signal and networking knowledge from Part 8 transfers directly; video-specific skills (colour, resolution, processing) are the new part to learn.
A genuine advantage of a second discipline
Crew who can competently cover a second department are rarer and more bookable than deep specialists in one — §17.4 already flags this about Titan specifically; it's true more broadly.

21.7 Moving into production or technical management

§17.1 mentions this as one of the main routes out of a crew start — this is the detail. Production management is planning and running the event rather than operating one part of it: budgets, schedules, crew, suppliers and the client relationship, for a company or freelance.

Where the audio background helps
You already know what a realistic build schedule looks like, what a crew actually needs on site, and where things typically go wrong — all of which is most of what a technical production manager does, just from a desk instead of a stage.
The usual way in
Rarely a direct application — almost always an internal step up within a company that already trusts you, once you've shown you can see the whole job and not just your own patch of it (§17.6 covers asking for that kind of responsibility).
Worth knowing about
The Production Services Association (PSA) already covers the crew end (§17.4); its more senior membership tiers and events also connect you to the production-management side of the same industry.

21.8 Venue & festival technical management

Running the technical side of one place rather than following the work around — head of sound or technical manager at a venue, or technical/site coordinator for a festival. A natural place to land after some years of touring or freelance life, when a settled base starts to matter more than variety.

What changes
You become the person other visiting crews and touring engineers rely on for local knowledge — exactly the "advancing and supporting" role §10.3 describes touring companies valuing, but as your day-to-day rather than an occasional mode.
The usual way in
Building a relationship with one venue or festival over repeat bookings until they'd rather have you permanently than keep re-briefing someone new each time.

21.9 Training, technical sales & warehouse/ops leadership

Three different desk-based routes that all lean on the same thing: knowing the kit properly, from the inside, the way someone who's only ever sold or taught it usually doesn't.

Manufacturer application/training roles
Manufacturers and distributors employ application engineers and trainers to run the free courses you've already used (§17.4) and to support customers using their kit. They hire from users of the product, not from a separate teaching pipeline.
Technical sales
Pro-audio dealers, rental companies and manufacturers all need sales staff who can actually answer a technical question — genuinely different from, and better paid than, generic sales, precisely because real product knowledge is rare in that role.
Warehouse & operations leadership
The most direct route of all, since it's where you're already standing — warehouse manager, stock controller or operations lead at a hire company, running the systems (§0.2-adjacent record-keeping, prep scheduling, fleet maintenance) that everyone else's job depends on.

21.10 Acoustics consultancy & compliance

Two more technical, more office-based directions — worth knowing about even though both usually need further study.

Acoustics consultancy
Room design, noise assessment and building acoustics — a genuinely different career, usually reached via further study rather than a direct step. The Institute of Acoustics (IOA) is the UK professional body; its Diploma in Acoustics and Noise Control is a part-time, work-alongside route into the profession that doesn't require starting a full degree from scratch, and your practical hearing and measurement experience (§13.5, §6.8) is a genuine head start on it.
Health, safety & compliance
A senior step up from the IOSH course already covered in §17.4 — NEBOSH's General Certificate is the next recognised level up, and event/production companies increasingly employ a dedicated safety or compliance officer drawn from people who already understand how a site actually runs, not generic HR/safety staff.

21.11 How to try a direction without leaving your day job

Everything above is easier to explore than it looks, and none of it requires quitting anything first. A realistic, low-risk way to test whether one of these actually suits you:

  1. Name the one you're actually curious about. Not "something else eventually" — pick one section above and be specific, even privately.
  2. Say so, to the right people. §17.3 already covers how work arrives here — the same applies to a new direction. Mentioning you're interested in video, or install, or training, to people already working in it is usually all it takes to get the first small opportunity offered rather than having to chase one.
  3. Take one small, real dose of it before committing further. Shadow someone for a day, take the one free course that direction offers (most of the credentials in this part have a free or cheap entry-level option), or say yes to a single job in that world alongside your normal work.
  4. Give it a defined trial, not an open-ended maybe. "I'll say yes to anything in this direction for the next six months, then decide" is far easier to actually act on than an unbounded someday.
  5. Log it, the same way as everything else. §17.7's evidence habit applies here too — a small, specific record of what you tried and how it felt is what turns "I think I might like install work" into an actual decision.
  6. Treat it as reversible. None of this is a one-way door. Trying a direction and deciding live audio is what you actually wanted all along is not a wasted step — it's the same kind of useful information as the opposite result.
Standard

The people who successfully move sideways almost never do it in one leap. It's a small dose, repeated a few times over a year or two, next to the job that's already paying — not a decision made once and acted on cold.

21.12 Matching a direction to what actually suits you

A short, honest set of questions worth asking yourself before spending real time on any one direction — what you actually enjoy tends to matter more than what looks impressive from outside:
