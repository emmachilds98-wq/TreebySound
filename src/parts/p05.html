PART 5

Wireless & RF

Its own discipline, its own vocabulary, and the thing most likely to make you look bad in front of an audience.

5.1 Why RF gets its own part

Everything else in this book is a chain you can trace with your hands. RF is invisible, shared with everyone else in the building and the surrounding area, legally regulated, and fails in ways that are hard to diagnose while a presenter is mid-sentence. It's also the area where a bit of knowledge makes you disproportionately valuable — a lot of technicians never get past "put a battery in it and hope".

The good news: 90% of RF reliability comes from about six habits, and they're all in §5.9.

Regardless of brand, a system is: a transmitter (handheld, bodypack, or plug-on), a receiver, and antennas. Everything else is refinement.

Handheld
Mic and transmitter in one. Capsules are often interchangeable — a Shure handheld body can take an SM58, Beta 58, KSM9 head. Check what capsule is fitted at prep; clients specify it.
Bodypack
Belt pack feeding a lav, headset, or instrument cable. Connector type is brand-specific and a classic prep trap.
Plug-on
Transmitter that clips onto the back of a normal XLR mic. Turns any wired mic wireless. Great for roving reporter mics and awkward positions; many supply phantom.

5.3 Antennas

HelicalDirectional, circular polarisationLess sensitive to transmitter orientation. Good where performers move and tumble.
Active antennaAnyHas an amplifier built in to overcome cable loss on long runs.

Diversity

Almost all professional receivers are true diversity: two antennas, two receiver sections, and the unit continuously picks whichever is getting the better signal. This defeats "dropouts" caused by multipath — the same signal arriving via two paths and cancelling itself at one point in space. Two antennas, spaced apart, are very unlikely to both be in a null at once.

Standard

Space diversity antennas roughly a wavelength apart (about 0.5 m at UHF) — or more — and angle them outwards at about 45° from each other. Get them above head height and with line of sight to the performers. Bodies are mostly water and water absorbs UHF; an antenna behind a crowd is an antenna in a swamp.

Watch it

Coax loses signal, and thin coax loses a lot. A long run of cheap RG-58 to a paddle can lose more than the paddle gains, making things worse. Use proper low-loss coax for long runs, keep runs as short as you can, and use an active antenna or in-line amplifier if you genuinely need distance.

Antenna distribution

Running more than about four receivers? Don't give each one its own pair of whips — they interfere with each other and it's a forest of aerials. Use an antenna distro: one pair of good antennas feeds a splitter which feeds all the receivers, and it usually passes DC to power active antennas and daisy-chains power to the receivers too. Cleaner, more reliable, less cable.

5.4 UK spectrum — the legal bit

Watch it

Spectrum rules genuinely change — bands get sold off, boundaries shift, and what was legal five years ago may not be now. Check the current Ofcom PMSE position before you rely on anything in this section, and check the rules for the country you're in if you travel. Kit bought abroad may be illegal to transmit on here. Ofcom periodically runs a Sector Spectrum Review consultation covering PMSE alongside other spectrum users — these consultations are exactly the kind of thing that quietly reshapes which bands wireless mics and IEMs can legally use a few years out, so it's worth a search for the current one every so often rather than assuming today's bands are permanent.

5.5 Coordination & intermodulation

You can't just pick frequencies at random. Two transmitters don't only occupy their own frequencies — they mix together in the front end of receivers and produce intermodulation products at predictable mathematical relationships (the classic being 2×f1 − f2). Those products can land squarely on a third channel you're using, and that channel will be noisy or drop out even though nothing is obviously "on" its frequency.

This is why manufacturers supply coordination groups — pre-calculated sets of frequencies that are intermod-clean with each other. Use them.

  1. Scan the spectrum on site to find what's already in use — TV transmissions, other companies' kit, the venue's own systems.
  2. Coordinate — pick a clean group, either using the receiver's built-in group/channel scheme or software.
  3. Deploy the frequencies to the transmitters, usually by IR sync from the receiver.
  4. Document what's on what. Write it down. You will need it.
Standard

Always scan on site, on the day, with the venue's own systems and the other departments' kit switched on. A scan done in an empty hall at 8am is a different spectrum from the one you'll have at 8pm. On multi-company events there is usually a designated RF coordinator — find them and get your frequencies from them rather than grabbing your own.

Go deeper

Shure Wireless Workbench and Sennheiser WSM are both free and both do proper coordination and monitoring. Learning one of them is a big, visible step up in capability. Links in §18.5.

5.6 Batteries

The most common cause of a wireless failure is a flat battery, and it's completely preventable.

  • Fresh batteries for every show, not every day, not "it looked fine". Standard
  • Good alkalines (Duracell Procell, Energizer Industrial) if you're on disposables. Cheap alkalines have wildly variable capacity and dump their voltage suddenly.
  • Never use zinc-carbon and be careful with generic rechargeable NiMH in analogue systems — their lower nominal voltage (1.2 V vs 1.5 V) means the receiver's battery meter reads wrong and you get much less warning.
  • Proper rechargeable systems (Shure SB900 series, Sennheiser BA series) report accurate remaining time in hours and minutes and are far better. If your company has them, use them and learn the charging discipline.
  • Battery contacts get corroded and bent. Clean them; check the pack door closes firmly. A pack that cuts out when the performer moves is often a mechanical contact problem, not RF.
Standard

Build a battery routine: new cells go in at a fixed point (e.g. at the top of soundcheck), used-but-good cells go in a separate labelled box for rehearsals and testing, and dead ones go straight in the recycling tub. Never let used and fresh mix in the same bag — that's how a "fresh" battery dies in Act Two.

5.7 In-ear monitors (IEMs)

Same RF principles, opposite direction: the transmitter is in the rack and the receiver is on the performer.

Common systems
Shure PSM 300 / PSM 900 / PSM 1000, Sennheiser EW IEM G4 and 2000 series, Lectrosonics.
Mono vs stereo
Most systems can run true stereo, or "MixMode" where one ear is the band and the other is vocals, blended with the pack's balance control.
Combiner
Multiple IEM transmitters into one antenna. Essential above two or three channels — otherwise the transmitters desensitise each other.
Moulds vs universals
Custom-moulded earpieces isolate far better and sound better. Generic tips are what you'll hire out. Hygiene matters — new tips per user.
Watch it

Keep IEM transmit antennas physically separated from mic receive antennas — different ends of the stage if you can, and at minimum a couple of metres apart. A transmitter next to a receiver's antenna will swamp its front end. This is the single most common cause of "the radio mics were fine until we added in-ears".

Watch it

IEMs sit directly in someone's ear canal. A feedback squeal, a dropped mic, or a mis-patch at full level can cause real hearing injury. Build the habit of bringing IEM levels up from zero, always limiting the IEM outputs, and never soloing something into a live IEM mix.

5.8 Setting up and proving a wireless system

  1. Position antennas first — line of sight, above head height, away from metal, away from LED walls and lighting racks (both are RF-noisy).
  2. Power up receivers only, all transmitters off, and scan. This shows you the noise floor and anything already occupying the band.
  3. Assign frequencies from a clean coordinated group.
  4. Sync each transmitter to its receiver (usually IR). Label the transmitter with the channel name — gaffa and a Sharpie, or proper channel tape.
  5. Set transmitter gain so that the loudest expected input peaks near but not into the transmitter's limiter. This is set on the pack, and getting it wrong here cannot be fixed at the desk — too low is noisy, too high is distorted.
  6. Check RF and AF meters on the receiver. Strong steady RF, healthy audio.
  7. Walk test. Walk the entire performance area — stage, wings, backstage route, into the crowd if they go there — talking into the mic while someone watches the meters and listens. Find the dead spots before the show does.
  8. Set squelch as low as reliably possible. Too high and you get dropouts on weak signal; too low and you get noise bursts when the transmitter is off.
  9. Document the frequencies, the pack assignments and the gain settings.
Standard

Do the walk test with the show conditions in place as far as possible — audience is the thing you can't replicate, so bias your margin generously. A system that's marginal in an empty room will fail in a full one.

Carry spares as a matter of course: a spare pack, a spare capsule, a spare lav, and a wired handheld with a long cable. The wired mic is the ultimate fallback — when RF has genuinely failed mid-show, a 20 m XLR and an SM58 saves the day and nobody in the audience knows anything happened.

5.10 RF exposure & EMF safety

Genuinely low on the list of things to worry about day to day, but worth actually knowing about rather than never hearing of it until someone asks you a question you can't answer. In the UK, the Control of Electromagnetic Fields at Work Regulations 2016 require employers to assess workplace exposure to electromagnetic fields — including RF — against exposure limits based on international (ICNIRP) guidance, and to control it where a real risk exists.

Standard

At the power levels and distances you'll actually work with — a handheld transmitter a performer is holding, a bodypack against their skin, a wedge-mounted receive antenna — this is not a realistic hazard. The situation actually worth knowing about is higher-power multi-channel IEM transmit combiners and antenna racks, where several transmitters' output is combined into one feed at meaningfully higher total power. Manufacturers of that class of kit publish a minimum safe working distance from the transmit antenna for exactly this reason — check the manual for anything you're not personally familiar with, and don't lean against or work for extended periods pressed right up against a live high-power transmit antenna.

Also seen

You are far more likely to be asked about this by a venue's own health and safety officer doing due diligence than to encounter an actual problem. Being able to say "our transmit power and distances are well within manufacturer guidance" is the correct answer, not a shrug.

5.11 Shure and Sennheiser systems — step by step

Most premium hire stock is Shure (Axient Digital, ULX-D, SLX-D) or Sennheiser (Digital 6000, EW-DX). The steps are the same shape on every system.

  1. Antennas on, receivers powered, outputs into the desk or stage box. With several receivers, use an antenna distribution system and remote paddles, not a forest of whips.
  2. Turn on everything that will be on during the show — LED walls, video, other radio — then scan.
  3. Coordinate. Shure: Wireless Workbench (free) — scan, add your devices' frequencies from the inventory, Analyze, Calculate, then Assign and Deploy to the networked receivers. Sennheiser: Wireless Systems Manager or Sennheiser Control Cockpit do the same job. On a single small system, the receiver's own group scan is fine.
  4. Stay inside your licence — Channel 38, Channel 70, or the frequencies on your site licence (§5.12).
  5. Batteries in (fresh AAs or the system's rechargeable packs, e.g. Shure SB900-series), transmitter on.
  6. Sync each transmitter to its receiver channel (IR sync — hold the pack's window to the receiver's sync window).
  7. Set transmitter gain/sensitivity so the loudest talker or singer doesn't clip, then label the pack and receiver channel with the person's name.
  8. Walk test every mic (§11.6) and keep backups: spare mics already coordinated and synced.
Also seen

Sennheiser Spectera and other new "wideband" (WMAS) systems carry many mics and IEMs in one wide channel. They're coordinated differently — follow the system owner's setup rather than the steps above.

Whoever owns the radio kit usually holds the licence — the hire company or the production. Your job is to know which licence the job is using and keep every transmitter (mics and IEMs) inside it. Using frequencies you aren't licensed for is a criminal offence under the Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006, and on mobile-network frequencies it interferes with phones.

Watch it

Cheap imported radio mics often transmit on frequencies that are illegal in the UK. If a client or band brings their own, check the frequency range before it goes on.
