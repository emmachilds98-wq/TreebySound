PART 0

How to use this thing

Two minutes here saves you a lot of scrolling later.

0.1 The four markers

Live audio has a lot of "well, it depends". So instead of pretending there's one answer, everything in here is tagged:

Standard

The way it's normally done, and what you should default to when nobody's told you otherwise. If you do this on a job, nobody will blink.

Also seen

A different method that's common enough that you will meet someone doing it. Not wrong — often just a different house style, a different era, or a different type of gig. Know it so you don't think they've made a mistake.

Watch it

The thing that breaks kit, ruins a show, or hurts someone. These are the ones worth actually memorising.

Go deeper

Where to go and learn this properly, because it's a whole subject on its own and one paragraph won't cut it. Every one of these has a matching entry in Part 18.

Finding things

There's no one correct way to find something in here — if one route doesn't work, another will.

  • Search — press / anywhere (or tap Library in the navigation bar) to open the search box; it filters the contents list live as you type, and Esc clears it. This searches every part of the book and every one of the five screens at once — including the ones you're not currently looking at — so use it rather than your browser's own Ctrl/⌘+F, which only sees what's on screen.
  • Browse — All parts lays out the whole library as cards, grouped by the question you're answering, with what each part is actually for. It's the one to use when you don't yet know what you're looking for.
  • Section numbers — every heading has one. They're stable, so notes to yourself like "look at 12.3 before Friday" will still work in a year.
  • Support — the fastest route in when you're mid-job and know roughly what kind of thing you need rather than where it lives: making it safe, fault-finding — including a fault finder you can talk to, which also walks you through setups and looks things up — the calculators, the pocket reference and the glossary are all one tap away there. On a computer the On a job now button, bottom-right, opens the same shortcuts as a menu without leaving the page you're reading.
  • Checklists — Part 19 has printable ones. Ticks save in your browser, so a half-done prep list is still half-done tomorrow.
  • How-to guides — every start-to-finish, step-by-step procedure in the book, grouped by area at §19.12 and as one flat alphabetical list at §19.16.
  • Glossary — every piece of jargon, alphabetically at §20.1 or grouped by area at §20.2. Each entry links straight back to where it's properly explained.
  • Pocket reference — a condensed, one-page cheat sheet of the numbers and steps you'll want mid-job, at §19.13, with its own print button.
  • Print — the Print button lays it out for paper properly. A printed line-check sheet in your back pocket beats a phone in the rain.
  • Works with no signal — this whole book is one self-contained file with nothing pulled in from the internet, so once it's saved to your phone, laptop or a USB stick it works exactly the same in a basement venue with no signal as it does at home. Keep a copy on your phone specifically — that's the one device you'll always have with you. To pin it like an app: open the file in your phone's browser, then Share → Add to Home Screen (iPhone/Safari) or the ⋮ menu → Add to Home screen (Android/Chrome).
  • Back up your notes — the private notes, ticked checklists, progress and job workspace you build up are saved only in this browser. Now and then, open Appearance and use Export backup to save them to a file; Restore backup reads one back on a new phone or after clearing your browser. Nothing is ever sent anywhere.

0.3 What this book assumes

It assumes UK working practice, because that's where the regs and the radio frequencies come from. It assumes you're working for a hire company or in-house AV team doing a mix of corporate, live music, theatre and events — the broad "technical event" world rather than one narrow specialism. And it assumes you'd rather understand why than memorise a sequence, because the sequence changes every time and the why doesn't.

Where a section is thin because the answer depends entirely on your specific kit, it says so and points you at the manual. That's not a cop-out — reading the actual manual for the actual box is the single highest-value habit in this job and almost nobody does it.

0.4 Where you're actually starting from

Six months of logging kit in and out, testing it, seeing what everything is, and being shown a setup once or twice for fun rather than for real. That's a specific and genuinely useful starting point, and it's worth being honest about what it does and doesn't give you, because this book is written to build from exactly here.

What it gives you: you already know what the kit is — what a stagebox looks like, roughly what lives in which flightcase, what a DI box does even if you've never wired one into a show yourself. That's the vocabulary most beginners spend their first month just acquiring. You skipped that month.

What it doesn't give you yet: the muscle memory of doing it under a bit of pressure, with someone waiting, without someone more experienced quietly fixing your mistakes before you notice them. That only comes from reps — and you haven't had many yet. That's completely normal at six months, whatever it might feel like watching someone who's done it for years make it look easy.

Standard

Read this book in the order it's written the first time through, even the bits that feel basic — the basics are exactly where "I sort of know this" quietly turns into a real gap under pressure. After that, use it as a reference: search for what you need, when you need it.

Go deeper

If "in the order it's written" is too blunt an instruction, the learning pathway breaks it into five stages and tells you exactly which sections to read at each one, what to practise on a real job before moving on, and which free training is worth doing at that point rather than later.

Two parts are worth flagging specifically for where you are right now. Part 12 (Fault-finding) is written to be usable even before you have deep intuition for this stuff — it gives you an actual procedure to follow, not just "get a feel for it", because a script beats a feeling every time when someone's looking at you expectantly. And §9.8 onwards covers Avolites Titan in real depth, because you've already got a head start there that's worth building on properly rather than leaving half-used.

Also seen

Nobody who's good at this job was born knowing it. Every engineer whose fault-finding looks like a magic trick got there by fixing the same handful of problems dozens of times each until the pattern-matching became automatic. You're not behind — you're at the very start of building exactly that.
