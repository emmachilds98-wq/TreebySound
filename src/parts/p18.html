PART 18

Free learning & trusted sources

A curated, current starting point for learning the skills that match the work in front of you.

18.1 How to use this part

Each entry below corresponds to one or more Go deeper notes earlier in the book. None of this is required reading to do the job competently — it's where to go once you want to go from competent to genuinely good in a specific area.

18.2 Console & offline editors

Referenced from §8.1. Search the manufacturer's own site for the current download — these tools update often enough that a fixed link goes stale, but they're consistently free and easy to find directly from Behringer/Midas, Allen & Heath, Yamaha, DiGiCo and Soundcraft's own pro-audio pages.

  • Search "[console model] offline editor" or "[console model] editor download" — every major manufacturer hosts theirs prominently.Free, and the single highest-value download for learning a specific desk.

18.3 Dante Certification

Referenced from §8.2. Audinate (the company behind Dante) run a free online certification programme.

  • Dante Certification ProgramFree, self-paced Levels 1–3 from Audinate. The current third edition is the right place to start; the certification is valid for three years.

18.4 Gain staging & signal flow

Referenced from §2.5.

  • Search "gain staging live sound" alongside Rational Acoustics and ProSoundWeb.Both publish well-regarded, freely available articles on practical gain structure — worth reading a couple of different takes rather than just one.

18.5 Wireless & RF

Complements Part 5. Shure and Sennheiser both publish detailed, freely available RF coordination and antenna guides aimed at working engineers rather than marketing — search "[Shure or Sennheiser] wireless systems guide". Ofcom's own PMSE licence pages are the authoritative, current source for what's actually licensed in the UK — spectrum allocation does change over time, so check there rather than trusting anything printed, including this book, as permanently current.

18.6 System tuning & measurement — courses

Referenced from §6.8. Rational Acoustics (makers of Smaart) run widely respected training, from free introductory webinars through paid in-depth courses. Open Sound Meter and Room EQ Wizard (§8.3) both have active user communities and documentation worth working through even if you can't access paid Smaart training yet.

18.7 Essential reading

Referenced from §6.8.

  • Sound Systems: Design and Optimization — Bob McCarthy.The standard text on system design and tuning. Dense, but the closest thing this field has to a definitive reference.
  • Yamaha Sound Reinforcement Handbook — Gary Davis & Ralph Jones.An older but still-solid, broad introduction to live sound fundamentals.
  • Live Sound Reinforcement — Scott Hunter Stark.Practical, hands-on, less theory-heavy than McCarthy — a good companion read.

18.8 Safety & regulation

Complements Part 13. HSE's own entertainment industry pages cover CDM 2015, noise, and lifting operations with sector-specific guidance rather than generic construction-site material. PLASA is the UK trade body for entertainment technology and a good source of current, industry-specific safety guidance.

  • The Purple GuideThe HSE-endorsed, industry-written reference for health, safety and welfare at music and other events (§13.1). The single best "how does this actually work in practice" companion to the legislation in Part 13 — free to browse online.
  • Search "BS 7909 guidance" for freely available summaries, or the official BSI standard page.The code of practice for temporary electrical systems at events (§3.8). The standard itself isn't free, but plenty of electricians and hire companies publish accurate plain-English summaries of what it requires.

18.9 Communities & forums

ProSoundWeb, Gearspace (the live sound and pro audio forums specifically) and the r/livesound community are all active places where working engineers answer specific, practical questions. Most regions also have local Facebook or WhatsApp groups for freelance crew — ask around at your next job, they're usually where last-minute work and gear questions actually circulate.

18.10 Avolites Titan & lighting control

Referenced throughout §9.8–9.11.

  • avolites.com — search there for the current Titan PC Suite download.The official installer, free from Avolites — needs your AvoKey plugged in to unlock full functionality (§9.8).
  • manual.avolites.com — the official Titan manual.Free, thorough, and kept current with each Titan software version. The authoritative reference once you're past the basics in §9.10, including exact button references for whichever specific console you end up learning on.
  • Search "Avolites Titan forum" or check Avolites' own YouTube channel.An active user community and a steady stream of official walkthrough videos — useful once you've got the core concepts and want to see real workflows.

18.11 Assistive listening & hearing loops

Referenced from §6.11.

  • ampetronic.com — search their site for technical guides.Free installer-level guides on hearing loop theory, driver sizing and spill (loop signal leaking into an adjacent room) — from one of the leading loop manufacturers.
  • contacta.co.uk — search their site for the induction loop guide.A second manufacturer's take on the same material — useful for cross-checking, since the two rarely disagree on the fundamentals.
  • Search "IEC 60118-4".The technical standard itself (§6.11) — not free, but summaries of what it requires are widely available from loop manufacturers and testing companies.

18.12 Professional & training bodies

Referenced from §17.4.

  • Production Services AssociationCompetency card scheme and the Live Event Technician Apprenticeship — the trade body built around the crew role itself rather than one discipline.
  • PLASA — National Event Lifting TrainingThe accessible entry point into rigging, worth having even if you never rig full-time.
  • ABTT trainingThe theatre world's recognised training body — chase this specifically if §10.4 pulls you.
  • AVIXA — CTS certificationThe standard credential in corporate and permanent-install AV (§10.2, §10.6) — the install-world equivalent of Dante Certification.
  • BectuThe UK entertainment and broadcast union — worth joining once you're freelancing regularly (§17.4).

18.13 Theatre cueing & show control

Referenced from §10.4. Complements OSC in §8.6.

  • qlab.app — official site and documentation.QLab is the dominant show-control/cueing software in UK and US theatre — free to use with no cue-count or feature limit, with paid licences unlocking more simultaneous audio/video output channels for bigger rigs. The documentation itself is a genuinely good introduction to cue-based thinking even before you touch it in anger.
  • ABTT trainingThe theatre world's recognised training body, covering cueing and stage management workflow alongside sound-specific content (§17.4).
  • Search "Deputy Stage Manager calling a show".Understanding how a DSM actually calls cues — the rhythm of "standby… go" — makes you a noticeably better theatre sound tech, because you start anticipating rather than just reacting.

18.14 Free development, without taking time off

Referenced from TreebySound Learn. These are deliberately short, free routes that fit around work. Do the one that matches the next likely opportunity at work; completing everything is not the goal.

  • Shure AcademyFree, self-paced courses in audio fundamentals, wireless, conferencing and AV networking. Use it to prepare for equipment or work types you will actually encounter.
  • Wireless WorkbenchShure's official page (download, release notes and user guide) for Shure's free RF coordination and monitoring software. Learn the workflow before touching a real RF plan, then practise alongside an authorised engineer.
  • Avolites Titan PC SuiteOfficial software and documentation. Licensing and feature access change by version, so check the current requirements and do not open a live show file without permission.
  • iRecord / iNaturalistKeep conservation practice alive in small weekly pieces, but at your level treat records as data that feeds the national datasets, not just a private log — full guidance and the bioacoustics crossover are in §18.15.

18.15 Conservation & bioacoustics — keeping your other career alive

Referenced from Outside work. This part of the book assumes what the rest doesn't: that you're already a conservation graduate with field-survey experience, not a beginner. So this is pitched at keeping a real portfolio active and building the one specialism where your new audio skills are a genuine advantage — bioacoustics — rather than at getting started from scratch. External sites and scheme details change; treat the official bodies below as the current source and search from their own pages.

Go deeper

The through-line here: passive acoustic monitoring is a fast-growing survey method, and the audio job you're now doing builds exactly the skills it needs — clean recording, gain structure, RF and battery discipline in the field, reliable metadata, and reading a spectrogram. You'd enter it already fluent in the half most ecologists find hardest.

Bioacoustics & passive acoustic monitoring (PAM)

  • AudioMoth / Open Acoustic DevicesThe low-cost, open-source acoustic recorder that made large-scale PAM accessible — full-spectrum versions cover bats as well as birds and other wildlife. The obvious hardware for a self-funded personal study.
  • BirdNET & Raven Pro (Cornell Lab)BirdNET is the machine-learning classifier for identifying species from recordings; Raven Pro is the standard spectrogram-analysis tool. Cornell publishes free best-practice guides for using them together for PAM.
  • Wildlife Acoustics (Song Meter, Kaleidoscope Pro)The professional end — Song Meter recorders and Kaleidoscope Pro for batch analysis and bat call identification. Worth knowing as the kit real survey contracts specify, alongside AnalookW for bats.
  • Arbimon / Rainforest ConnectionA cloud platform for storing and analysing large acoustic datasets, with a strong tropical-soundscape focus — a natural fit for your jungle-survey background and a way to stay involved in that world remotely.

Records, schemes & structured survey

  • iRecord → NBN AtlasSubmit verifiable records that flow into the National Biodiversity Network and the Biological Records Centre — research-grade, used in real assessments. At your level, consider verifying for a recording scheme or acting as a county recorder for a group you know well.
  • BTO surveysStandardised schemes — Breeding Bird Survey, WeBS, and the acoustic-friendly ones — that keep your methodology sharp and look far stronger on a CV than casual sightings. Bird ringing is a separate licensed training pathway if it appeals.
  • National Bat Monitoring ProgrammeBat Conservation Trust's structured monitoring — a strong match for the AudioMoth/detector skills above. Note that handling and some survey types are licensable in the UK.
  • ZooniverseReal research projects you can contribute to from anywhere — classifying camera-trap images and acoustic recordings, including tropical work. A low-friction way to stay hands-on with field science between your own deployments.

Professional standing & keeping skills current

  • CIEEM membershipThe UK professional body for ecologists. Join at graduate/associate grade and work towards Chartered Ecologist (CEcol) — it keeps a degree from going stale and is the recognised signal that you're still active and progressing in the field.
  • British Ecological SocietyMembership, journals and a large community — cheap, and the simplest way to stay in the literature and near the jobs board.
  • QGIS and RThe free, industry-standard tools for spatial and statistical ecology. Employers expect them; your own small acoustic dataset is the ideal excuse to keep them warm rather than letting them fade after graduation.
Watch it

Everything here sits under the same rules your degree drummed in: respect welfare, land access and data-sharing permissions, and remember that some UK bat and protected-bird work is licensable — a personal recorder left in the wrong place, or approaching a protected species, is a legal issue, not just an etiquette one. For any overseas or protected-species work, permits and local permissions come first.
