PART 1

How a job runs & who owns what

The working day, the people around you, paperwork and the habits that make you dependable.

1.1 What the role actually is

"Audio technician" in the event world is really four jobs wearing one coat, and which one you're doing changes by the day:

Warehouse
Prep, test, pack, unpack, fault-find, maintain, keep the stock honest. You're doing this bit already. It's the foundation — a well-prepped job barely goes wrong on site.
Crew
Load in, push boxes, run cable, build stacks, take direction. Physical, fast, and where you learn the most by watching.
Tech
Actually setting things up and making them work — patching, powering, testing, line-checking, fixing. This is the step you're moving into.
Operator
Mixing the show, or running playback, or looking after monitors. The visible job, and the one everyone thinks is the whole job. It isn't.

Most people move roughly along that line, but not cleanly — you'll be crewing on Monday, teching on Wednesday, and back in the warehouse Friday. That's normal and it's a good thing. The best techs are the ones who've done every part of the chain, because when something breaks on site they know what happened to it in the van.

1.2 The lifecycle of a hire

Everything you touch is somewhere on this loop. Knowing where you are on it tells you what your job is right now.

  1. Enquiry & spec — client asks for something, someone works out what kit answers it. Produces a kit list.
  2. Prep — kit pulled from shelves, tested, assembled into racks and cases, consumables added. Produces a packed job.
  3. Load out — onto the van/truck, in a sensible order (last on, first off).
  4. Load in & build — on site, unpacked, positioned, rigged, cabled, powered.
  5. Test — system check, line check, RF walk. Part 11 is entirely this step, because it's the one that decides whether the show is calm or awful.
  6. Show — operate, monitor, stay alert, fix quietly.
  7. Derig — down, coiled, cased, counted, loaded.
  8. Return & check-in — back on the shelf, but only after it's been checked. Damaged or suspect kit gets flagged, not shelved.
Watch it

Step 8 is where companies quietly lose money and reputation. A faulty mic that goes back on the shelf untested becomes someone's emergency in three weeks' time. Never shelve something you're not sure about — see §11.5 on quarantining.

Doors is a hard line. Once the audience is in, no drilling, no shouting across the room, no wandering on stage in a hi-vis, no testing a wedge at volume. If something is broken at doors, you fix it silently or you fix it at the interval.

1.5 The paperwork, and why it matters

Every document below exists because someone once got it wrong without one. Learn to read all of them; learn to write the first three.

Input list
Numbered list of every source: 1 Kick In, 2 Kick Out, 3 Snare Top… It includes the mic type and whether it needs phantom or a DI. This is the single most useful bit of paper in live sound.
Patch list
Where each input physically lands — which stagebox, which socket, which console channel. Sometimes merged with the input list.
Stage plan
A top-down drawing of who stands where, where the wedges point, where the DIs and power are. Lets you build the stage before anyone arrives. Example below.
Kit list
What's going on the job. Your warehouse world. Check it against what's physically in the cases, not against what the system says.
Tech spec / rider
The artist's or client's requirements. Riders are aspirational; specs are contractual. Read them and flag anything you can't supply early.
Schedule
Times for load-in, soundcheck, doors, curfew. Curfew is usually a legal condition, not a suggestion.
RAMS
Risk Assessment & Method Statement. The safety paperwork for the job. See §13.7.
Line check sheet
Your own tick list for proving inputs. See Part 19.
Standard

Get a copy of the input list and stage plan on your phone before you leave the warehouse. Half of prep mistakes are "we didn't know they needed four DIs" and that's a five-second check.

1.6 Site etiquette — the unwritten stuff

This gets you rebooked more reliably than technical skill does, especially early on.

  • Don't touch what isn't yours. Never repatch, unplug, move or "tidy" someone else's kit without asking. Especially not a console. Especially not during a show.
  • Ask before you assume. "Do you want this here?" costs four seconds. Moving a stack twice costs twenty minutes.
  • Say when you don't know. Nobody minds "I've not done this before, show me once". Everyone minds finding out at line check that you guessed.
  • Wear black. Blacks on stage from soundcheck onwards, always in theatre. Steel toes on site.
  • Radio discipline. Listen before you talk, say who you are and who you want, keep it short, say "copy" not a monologue. Don't editorialise on the ring — everyone hears it, including the client.
  • Keep your area tidy as you go. Cable off walkways, cases stacked, tape off the floor. Tidy sites are safe sites and it's noticed.
  • Don't disappear. If you're going for a break or to the van, tell someone.
  • Eat and drink. Twelve-hour days will find you out. Water, food when catering's open, and don't let the first thing you consume all day be at wrap. The safety side of long days is §13.9.
  • Look busy is not the same as be busy. But when there's genuinely nothing to do, ask if anyone needs a hand rather than sitting on a case with your phone out.
Also seen

House style varies a lot on formality. A West End theatre and a festival stage have very different tolerances for chat, phones and clothing. Read the room in the first hour — watch what the experienced crew do and match it.

1.7 Advancing a show

"Advancing" is the work that happens before anyone loads a van — a call or email exchange with the client, venue or artist to find out what you're actually walking into. Early on, you probably won't run this call yourself, but you'll be handed its output (the input list, stage plan and schedule in §1.5) and you'll increasingly be the one someone asks "did anyone check X?" — so it's worth knowing what a good advance actually covers.

  1. Confirm the basics first — date, times, venue address, load-in access (loading bay, stairs, lift size, parking restrictions).
  2. Get the input list and stage plan, even a rough one — see §1.5 for what good versions of these look like.
  3. Ask what power is available and where it is relative to FOH and the stage — a venue that says "there's plenty of power" has usually not actually checked.
  4. Ask what's already in the room — house PA, house console, existing rigging points — so you know what you're bringing versus what's provided. Assuming a "fully equipped venue" has what you need, without checking, is a common and expensive mistake.
  5. Confirm who else is on site — other departments, their call times, whether you're sharing power or cable routes with them (Part 9).
  6. Check any venue-specific restrictions — noise limits (§6.10), curfews, no-fly zones for rigging, restricted load-in hours.
  7. Flag anything you can't supply, early — a rider requesting kit that's not in stock, a schedule that doesn't leave enough time to build safely. Raising this a week out is a conversation; raising it on the day is a crisis.
Standard

A good advance answers questions before they become problems on site. If you're ever handed an advance that's clearly thin — missing power info, no stage plan, vague on access — it's worth flagging to whoever's running the job rather than just quietly hoping it works out.

1.8 A day in the life — a worked example

Everything above as separate topics can feel disconnected from what a real day actually looks like. Here's one, stitched together, with links back to where each part is properly explained.

06:30
Arrive at the warehouse. Check the job sheet against what's been pulled overnight — kit list versus what's actually in the cases (§1.2). Spot a DI missing from the count; grab a spare rather than assuming it'll turn up.
07:15
Load the van in reverse order of need — last on, first off (§19.3). Paperwork — RAMS, input list, contact numbers — goes in your bag, not just on a shared drive.
08:30
Arrive on site. First two questions: who's running audio today, and where's the power coming from (§1.3). Meet the LX crew chief while you're at it — you'll be sharing a cable route with them later (§9.5).
09:00–11:30
Build: PA positioned and rigged, stage cabled, console patched to match the patch list. A DI you're handed crackles when you wiggle the cable — swap it and quarantine the faulty one rather than fighting it mid-build (§11.5).
12:00
Line check, patch order, one input at a time, ticking off as you go (§11.3). Channel 14 is silent — gain's at zero, not actually faulty. Channel 22 stays quiet after that's fixed — swap the cable, that clears it (§12.9).
13:30
Wireless proving — walk every pack through where the performers will actually stand, watching the RF meter, not just checking it makes a sound at all (§5.8).
15:00
Soundcheck. Monitor requests come fast — a wedge with nothing in it turns out to be a pre-fade aux with the fader down, not a dead speaker (§15.6).
17:45
Doors. Everything on stage stops being adjusted out loud from this point (§1.4). A last-minute buzz turns out to be a cable now running parallel to a lighting run that wasn't there this morning — move it, problem gone (§9.2).
20:00–22:30
Show. Watch, listen, fix quietly, take notes for tomorrow.
23:00
Derig — coiled properly even though it's late, everything counted before it leaves the building, the faulty DI from this morning handed in properly flagged, not just shelved (§19.7).

Nothing in that day was exotic. It was the fundamentals, applied in order, under a bit of time pressure — which is really what most of this book is training you for.

1.9 The different worlds of event audio

"Events" is one job title covering genuinely different working cultures. The core skills — signal flow, patching, line check — carry across all of them; what changes is the pace, the paperwork, and how much room there is to improvise. Knowing which world a job sits in tells you what to expect before you arrive.

Corporate & conference
AGMs, product launches, awards dinners, panel discussions. Tight running order, radio mics on delegates who've never worn one, playback cues to the second, high client visibility. Formal dress, minimal visible cable, very low tolerance for anything audible going wrong mid-speech.
Live music & touring
Bands, one-off gigs, support-band changeovers. Faster, noisier build; more paperwork upfront (rider, stage plan, input list) but less formality on site than corporate work.
Theatre
A fixed, repeatable run rather than a one-off show. Cue-driven, careful radio mic changeovers between cast members, and stricter backstage discipline than a touring gig — see the "wear black" habit in §1.6.
Outdoor & festival
Weather, generators, longer cable runs, a licensing-condition noise limit that's actively enforced on-site (§6.10), and more genuine improvisation than any of the above — less shelter for kit, people or plans.
Private parties & weddings
Often your first paid solo booking. No producer, no crew hierarchy — you're frequently the only production person on site. See §10.9 for what's different.
Standard

Ask which of these a job sits in before you leave the warehouse — it changes what to wear, how much paperwork to expect, and how much slack there is if the build runs late.

1.10 The part nobody puts in a manual

Every technical manual, including this one, can make it sound like the job is only ever a sequence of correct actions. It isn't. Some of it is standing next to someone who's done this for fifteen years, understanding about a third of what they just said on the radio, and trying not to let it show. That feeling is completely normal — it happens to nearly everyone at some point, however experienced they look now — and it's worth a page of its own rather than pretending it away.

Standard

Everyone currently confident on a stagebox once didn't know what a stagebox was. The gap you can see between yourself and an experienced tech is real, but it's a gap of reps, not of ability — and it closes faster than it feels like it will.

Why this job makes it louder than most
The mistakes are audible, the audience is real, and jargon flies past on the radio fast enough that you can't always ask what something meant in the moment. That combination makes ordinary new-job nerves feel bigger here than in a quieter job where a slow week goes unnoticed.
The comparison that isn't fair
Measuring your month three against someone else's year ten is a rigged comparison — they've had the ten years of reps you haven't had yet. A fairer one is your month three against your own month one.
In the moment
Narrow the job down to the next physical action, not the evaluation. "Coil this cable" is manageable; "am I good enough to be here" isn't a question you can usefully answer mid-show. §12.7's approach to asking for help works the same way — one small, specific action beats one large, vague worry.
After a mistake
What almost always actually happens: someone notices, it gets quietly fixed, and within a day nobody but you remembers it. What it feels like happens: everyone clocked it and is silently judging you. The second one is very rarely true. Own it briefly, fix what's yours to fix, and let the moment end — dwelling on it on site costs you the next task's attention, which is the thing actually in your control.
Write it down instead of carrying it
The learning log exists partly for this. A specific, three-minute note turns a vague bad feeling about a shift into a concrete, fixable thing — and concrete things are much easier to stop worrying about than vague ones.
Watch it

Normal nerves settle with reps. If a job is leaving you dreading it, not sleeping, or feeling genuinely low well beyond the shift itself, that's no longer just new-job nerves, and it's worth talking to someone about — a GP, or the British Association for Performing Arts Medicine (BAPAM), a UK charity supporting the health of people working in live events and performance, crew included, not just performers. Pushing through in silence isn't the professional option; asking for help is — the same standard §17.6 already sets for asking for more responsibility applies just as well to asking for support.
