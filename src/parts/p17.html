PART 17

Career path & next steps

Where people go from warehouse to audio, and what changes when you take on more responsibility or freelance.

17.1 Where people actually go from here

There's no single ladder, but common paths from where you are now: staying in-house and moving warehouse → crew → tech → operator over a year or two; going freelance and building a client list across several companies; specialising hard into one discipline (RF, systems/tuning, monitor world, install/DSP); or moving sideways into production management once you understand how a show actually gets built. None of these are better than the others — they suit different temperaments.

All of that is still inside live audio. If you're curious what the same experience opens up further afield — broadcast, AV install, studio work, a second discipline in lighting or video, production management, venue leadership and more — Part 21 maps them out, including how to test one gradually without leaving the job you've got.

17.2 Freelancing basics

Day rate
Varies hugely by role, region and experience. Ask other freelancers what's typical for your level rather than guessing — undercutting the going rate makes you cheaper to book but harder for everyone (including you, later) to get a fair rate.
Invoicing
Simple, prompt, clear — company/job name, dates, rate, total, payment terms. Chase late payment politely but persistently; it's a genuinely common freelance headache.
Public Liability Insurance (PLI)
Covers you if your work causes injury or damage to someone else or their property. Many venues and production companies won't let you on site without it. Relatively cheap for the protection it gives — get it before you need it.
Equipment insurance
A separate policy from PLI — covers loss, theft or damage to kit you own (see §17.5). Only worth pricing up once you own something worth insuring, but venues and hire companies will occasionally ask whether your own gear is covered, so it's worth being able to answer.
Being your own admin
Diary management, tax (in the UK, registering as self-employed with HMRC if you're trading as a sole trader), and keeping basic records of income and expenses. Unglamorous, but getting caught out at tax time is avoidable with an hour a month.
Go deeper

This book won't make you an accountant, and it shouldn't try to. Talk to other freelancers in the industry about what they actually do for insurance and tax — the details change and local practice varies. See Part 18.

Also seen

A job that crosses into the EU brings its own paperwork — carnets, work permits, visas — on top of all this. See §17.8.

17.3 Building a network

This industry runs heavily on who's known to be reliable, more than on CVs. Practical habits that build a reputation faster than anything else: turn up early, do the unglamorous jobs without being asked twice, communicate clearly when something's gone wrong instead of hiding it, and stay in touch with people you've worked well with rather than only reaching out when you need work. Crew chiefs remember who made their day easier.

17.4 Training & certifications worth having

Dante Certification (Levels 1–2)
Free, online, genuinely respected. See §8.2.
IOSH Working Safely
A short, widely-recognised general safety course. Useful baseline credential for site work generally.
Manufacturer-specific training
Console and PA manufacturers (Allen & Heath, DiGiCo, d&b, L-Acoustics and others) run free or low-cost training on their own systems — worth doing on whatever your company uses most.
PLASA membership & rigging pathway
PLASA is the UK entertainment technology trade body. Its National Event Lifting Training (NELT) is the accessible entry point into rigging — a day-course-length qualification worth having even if you never rig full-time, since audio kit is flown constantly and understanding the basics of what's holding it up is genuinely useful. The National Rigging Certificate (NRC) is the more senior, specialist qualification for once you're actually moving in that direction.
PSA competency card & Live Event Technician Apprenticeship
The Production Services Association is the trade body most directly built around the live-event crew role itself, rather than a specific discipline. Its competency card scheme functions as the events industry's rough equivalent of a construction-site CSCS card, and its Live Event Technician Apprenticeship is a genuine, funded route into the trade if you're early enough in your career to consider it. Worth knowing about even if you came in a different way, since more of the industry checks for it every year.
Bectu
The UK entertainment and broadcast trade union (operating as the Bectu sector of Prospect). Worth joining once you're freelancing regularly — collective advice on rates, contracts and disputes is genuinely useful when you're negotiating alone.
First Aid at Work
Not audio-specific, but genuinely useful on site, and companies often prefer crew who hold it.
Avolites Titan fluency
If you've already got a Titan licence (§9.8), building real fluency with it on the side is one of the better uses of a spare evening on this whole list — a second, cross-department skill is rarer and more hireable than depth in audio alone.
ABTT training (if theatre pulls you)
The Association of British Theatre Technicians runs the training most theatre venues and touring theatre companies actually recognise — worth chasing specifically if §10.4's world appeals more than corporate or touring music.
AVIXA CTS (if install/corporate AV pulls you)
The Certified Technology Specialist is the standard credential in corporate and permanent-install AV — the world of §10.2 and §10.6 rather than touring. It's the AVIXA/install-world equivalent of Dante Certification above: not essential, but a recognised signal that you're serious about that specific direction.

17.5 Kit of your own — what to buy first

You don't need much personal kit early on — most of what you use belongs to the company. A sensible first-purchase order: a decent multitool and torch, a set of musicians' earplugs (§13.5), a reliable pair of headphones, a small selection of adapters and a known-good short XLR for fault-finding (§12.2), and boots with steel toes if your company doesn't provide them. A measurement mic and your own console offline software are worth adding once you know which direction you're heading. Once you own the basics, §19.17 is the tickable version for actually getting them out of the door with you.

17.6 Asking for more responsibility, without overstepping

Most people don't get handed the next step up — they ask for it, at the right moment, in the right way. A few things that work better than waiting to be noticed:

  • Say what you want, specifically. "I'd like more time on the desk" is easier to forget than "next time we're short a line check hand, I'd like to shadow it."
  • Ask the person who actually decides, not just whoever's nearest — usually the crew chief or whoever books shifts, not a colleague at your own level.
  • Pick a quiet moment, not mid-build or two minutes before doors. A quick word at the end of a shift lands better than an ambush under pressure.
  • Prove it in small doses first. Ask to shadow a line check before you ask to run one; ask to run one before you ask to mix. Each small win makes the next ask an easy yes.
  • Accept "not yet" without sulking. A genuine "what would you want to see from me first?" gets you a clear target instead of a closed door.
Standard

This is exactly the position most warehouse staff are in when the industry opens up to them: reliable, known, and one honest conversation away from more stage time. The asking is very often the only step actually missing.

17.7 Keeping evidence of your work

§17.3 covers why reputation matters more than a CV here — this is about not losing track of it. A short, private record of what you actually did and who you did it for turns "I've done quite a bit of corporate work" into a specific, checkable list when someone asks.

What to note
Company, venue, date, your role, and one line on what you actually did — "ran line check solo for a 40-input corporate panel" is worth far more than "helped out".
Testimonials
A short "happy to work with again" message after a job you did well is worth asking for, especially from a crew chief or company you'd like to work with regularly. Most people are glad to give one if asked directly rather than hoping it happens on its own.
What not to record
Client-confidential details, other people's rates, or anything from a show you weren't supposed to discuss. A factual record of your own work never needs any of that.
Also seen

This is the freelance version of the learning log described earlier in this book — same habit, later purpose. Keep doing it once you're being paid, not just while you're learning.

17.8 Touring outside the UK

Since Brexit, taking a job into the EU means more than packing a passport. None of this is something you'll arrange yourself as a junior on someone else's tour — it's the tour manager's or production company's job — but not recognising the words when they come up makes you look like you don't understand the gig, so it's worth knowing what they mean.

ATA Carnet
An international customs document that lets professional equipment cross a border temporarily without paying import duty, on the promise it leaves again within the carnet's validity. Every case on the truck typically needs to be listed against it, matched at each border — which is why an accurate kit list matters even more on an international run.
Work permits & visas
UK crew working in the EU (and vice versa) may need a specific work permit or visa depending on the country and how long the job runs — this varies country by country and changes over time, so it's genuinely not something to assume from a previous job.
The 90/180-day rule
UK passport holders can generally spend up to 90 days in any rolling 180-day period across the Schengen area without a visa — a touring crew member working multiple EU dates across a year needs to actually track this, not estimate it.
Cabotage
EU rules restricting how much a UK-registered truck can move goods between EU countries on one trip — relevant to how a touring truck's route gets planned, not something you'll plan yourself, but a word worth recognising when a tour manager mentions routing headaches.
Watch it

All of this changes with government policy and isn't something to work from memory of a previous tour. If an international job is genuinely yours to help plan rather than just crew, get current, specific advice — a carnet agent or a production company with recent experience of the actual route — rather than relying on what applied last time or what worked for someone else's tour.
