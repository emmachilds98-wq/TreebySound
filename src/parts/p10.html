PART 10

Types of event & what changes

Corporate, touring, theatre and broadcast use the same fundamentals but reward different priorities and habits.

10.1 Why context changes everything

Everything in Parts 2–9 is true everywhere — signal flow doesn't care what kind of gig you're on. But what good looks like, how formal the site is, how much you're expected to improvise versus follow a document exactly, and even how you dress and speak on the radio, shifts a lot between contexts. Turning up to a black-tie AGM with festival-stage energy reads as unprofessional; turning up to a punk show in a suit reads as clueless. This part is a quick map of the main worlds so you know what to expect walking in.

10.2 Corporate AV

Conferences, AGMs, product launches, award dinners. High client visibility, low tolerance for anything visibly going wrong, and the audience is usually not there for the sound — it just needs to be invisible and correct.

Priorities
Speech intelligibility above everything. A slightly boring but flawless mix beats an exciting one with a single dropout.
Typical sources
Lecterns, handheld/lav radio mics on presenters, laptop playback for VT and slides, panel discussions on multiple mics at once.
What's different
Tight rehearsal time, strict running order, a producer or stage manager calling cues, and often a client watching you directly. Presentation and calm matter as much as technical skill.
Common pitfalls
Presenters who wander off-axis from a lav mic, laptop audio nobody checked, and multiple panellists on live mics causing feedback or comb filtering if gains aren't managed.
Standard

Get every presenter mic'd and sound-checked before doors, including a moment of them actually talking at a normal volume — not just "check check". Nerves make people speak differently on the day than in rehearsal.

Go deeper

A growing share of corporate work has a remote-participant element built in from the start — see §10.8 for hybrid meetings and video conferencing specifically.

10.3 Live music & touring

Bands and club shows. The audience is there specifically for the sound and the performance — expectations for impact and quality are high, and so is the pace. Festivals share everything here but add enough of their own (multiple stages, multiple contractors, hard licensing curfews) to get their own section — see §10.10.

Priorities
Energy, impact, and a mix that serves the music. Perfect isn't always the goal — the right amount of excitement is.
Typical sources
Full band inputs — drums, bass, guitars, backline, multiple vocals — often changing completely between acts on a multi-band bill.
What's different
Fast changeovers (sometimes minutes between acts), visiting engineers who may want to run their own desk or bring their own show file, and a much more physical, high-tempo build.
Common pitfalls
Under-running changeover time, not having a solid house patch that visiting engineers can drop into quickly, and stage volume from backline overwhelming the FOH mix.
Also seen

On touring shows with their own engineer and system tech, your job shifts to advancing, translating local knowledge (venue quirks, power, load-in access) and supporting their show rather than mixing it yourself. Being genuinely useful in that support role gets you rebooked by touring companies just as much as mixing skill does.

10.4 Theatre & musicals

The most heavily documented, cue-based world you'll work in. Everything happens on a call, from a defined script, at a defined moment — audio design here is closer to a technical performance in itself than a live mix.

Priorities
Precision and repeatability. The show must sound the same on night 40 as night 1.
Typical sources
Radio mics on performers (often a lot of them, with fast changeovers backstage), playback/sound effects cues, orchestra or band in a pit.
What's different
Everything is cued — the Stage Manager calls "SQ42, go" and a sequence fires. A show file with every scene, every cue, painstakingly built and rehearsed. Radio mic changeovers between actors happen in the wings in seconds, silently, in the dark.
Common pitfalls
Missing a cue, a radio mic pack not swapped in time for the next scene, wig/costume interference with a lav mic, and — as covered in §1.4 — anything audible once doors are open and the house is dark.
Go deeper

If you want to move into theatre sound specifically, learning a cueing system like QLab or a theatre-standard console workflow, and understanding how a Deputy Stage Manager calls a show, is time very well spent. See §18.13.

10.5 Broadcast & streaming

The audience isn't in the room — they're watching a screen, and the room's acoustics are irrelevant to what they hear. Different rules apply.

Priorities
A clean, consistent, correctly-leveled feed for the broadcast mix — not necessarily what sounds best in the room. Broadcast engineers often want a drier, more controlled sound than a PA mix.
Typical sources
The same inputs as the live show, split or fed in parallel to a broadcast desk via a splitter or a Dante/AES67 feed, plus your own comms tying into theirs.
What's different
Loudness standards (broadcasters commonly work to defined LUFS targets rather than dBFS peak), strict timing, and a whole extra layer of people — a broadcast sound supervisor — who need a split of your signal, usually via an isolated multicore split or digitally via §9.3.
Common pitfalls
Ground/earth issues introduced by an improperly isolated broadcast split, level mismatches between what sounds right in-room and what the broadcast desk wants, and forgetting the broadcast feed needs to keep working even if the PA doesn't.
Also seen

A proper broadcast split uses an isolation transformer or a digitally isolated feed so a fault on the broadcast side (or a dodgy earth on their truck) can never affect your system, and vice versa. Never just parallel-wire an unbalanced feed off your desk for a broadcaster without isolating it.

10.6 Conferences, houses of worship & permanent install

A smaller but common world: spaces where the system is already built and mostly stays the same week to week — a church, a conference centre, a council chamber. You're not building a rig from scratch; you're operating and maintaining one.

  • Install audio is judged on reliability and consistency over years, not peak performance on one night. Ceiling speakers, wall-mounted DSPs, and systems tuned once and left alone are the norm.
  • You'll meet DSP processors (BSS, Biamp, QSC Q-SYS) doing the job a console does elsewhere — routing, EQ, automatic mixing for multiple lecterns/panel mics.
  • Expect less troubleshooting-on-the-fly freedom — changes often need sign-off, because the system has to keep working correctly for the next hundred users who won't have you there to fix it.

10.7 Reading the room

None of this is about being a different person — it's about matching your energy, formality and communication style to what the job actually needs. A few tells that help you calibrate fast on arrival:

SignalSuggests
Running order with cue numbers and timings to the minuteFormal — theatre/corporate discipline, follow the document exactly
"We'll figure out the set times once bands arrive"Informal — live music discipline, stay flexible and quick
Producer or SM on comms constantlyCued show — wait for the call, don't act early
Crew chief just says "shout if you need anything"Looser show — use initiative, ask when genuinely unsure
Standard

When in doubt, match the most experienced person in the room in the first hour rather than guessing. It's the fastest way to calibrate, and nobody minds being quietly copied.

10.8 Hybrid meetings & video conferencing

Increasingly the default expectation on corporate work (§10.2) rather than a special request — some or all of the audience is joining remotely, and the room audio has to work both ways: the room needs to hear the remote participants, and the remote participants need to hear the room cleanly.

Teams Rooms / Zoom Rooms
Purpose-built hardware and software combinations (a small PC or appliance, a camera, a DSP-based audio bar or a ceiling mic array) certified to run Microsoft Teams or Zoom natively in a room. Increasingly what you'll find already installed in a client's own conference rooms rather than being asked to build from scratch.
USB audio
The most common way your own desk or a room's DSP hands off to a laptop running the call — a USB audio interface or a room's built-in USB output appears to the laptop as a standard microphone and speaker, no drivers or special software needed on the laptop end.
Acoustic echo cancellation (AEC)
The processing that stops the remote participants hearing their own voice come back a moment later through the room's speakers and back into the room mic. Built into proper conferencing DSPs and soundbars; a bare laptop mic and a room PA are a recipe for exactly this problem.
What's actually yours to check
Confirm the remote feed can actually hear the room at a sensible level (not just that the room can hear them), confirm there's no echo or feedback loop before the call starts, and know where the mute button is for both directions. A silent remote participant nobody can hear is a very common, very avoidable failure.
Watch it

Never feed a live room mic straight into a laptop's built-in mic input alongside PA playback without checking for echo first — it's the single most common cause of a hybrid meeting becoming unusable, and it's completely preventable with five minutes of testing before anyone's watching.

10.9 Private parties, weddings & smaller functions

A very common way into paid audio work, and a genuinely different context from anything else in this part: no producer, no stage manager, and often nobody on site who's ever worked with a sound crew before.

Priorities
Reliability and reading the room over technical ambition. A background music level that creeps up through the evening without anyone checking is a far more common complaint than anything technically wrong.
Typical sources
A speech mic or two (best man, father of the bride), background/DJ playback, sometimes a small live act for a set. Often your own compact PA and desk rather than a big hired system.
What's different
You're frequently the only production person on site, dealing directly with the client or a wedding/event planner rather than a crew chief — decisions that would normally go up a chain land on you directly. There's usually no RAMS culture, no formal running order, and timings move constantly as the event runs long or short.
Common pitfalls
A curfew that's genuinely a licensing condition of a residential venue, not just a courtesy (§1.5) — going over it can cost the client their licence for future events, not just annoy a neighbour. Also: an unbriefed guest asking to "just say a few words" on a live mic, and background music left running unattended and creeping louder.
Standard

Agree who has authority to change the plan — timings, volume, what's played — before the event starts, and get it in writing if you can (an email is enough). Without a producer in the room, "the client" can mean four different family members with four different opinions by 9pm.

10.10 Festivals

Touring and festival work share a lot of DNA — fast changeovers, visiting engineers, physical builds — but a festival adds problems a single-stage tour date doesn't have: several stages running near-simultaneously, several different companies and crews sharing one site, and an audience moving freely between all of it.

Stage bleed
Two or more stages close enough that one's low end or monitor wash carries into another's mix position or FOH. Rarely fixable by you alone — staggered set times and physical layout are decided long before you arrive — but worth knowing about before you troubleshoot a "muddy" mix that's actually the next stage's bass bleeding in.
Changeover choreography
Multiple acts, often with their own techs and riders, moving through one stage on a tight schedule — sometimes minutes, not the hour-plus of a single touring show (§10.3). A pre-built patch that doesn't need reworking for each act, and a clear runner/changeover system, matters more here than almost anywhere else.
Multiple contractors, one site
Audio, lighting, staging, security, welfare and site power are usually different companies entirely, coordinated by a production manager rather than any one of them. Shared cable routes, shared generators and shared load-in windows mean the department coordination habits in Part 9 matter constantly, not occasionally.
Weather & site conditions
Often fully or partially outdoors for the whole run, with mud, rain covers and generator-only power (§6.14 · §9.6) as the default rather than the exception.
Noise & curfew conditions
Frequently governed by a licence with noise monitored at specific off-site points, not just an on-site SPL target — see the Safety Advisory Group and noise-condition material in §6.10. A festival curfew is very often a hard legal deadline, not a guideline.
Standard

On your first festival, spend the first hour learning the site as much as the stage: where your stage sits relative to others, where the generator and fuel point are, where the nearest production office and medical point are, and who your stage manager and production manager actually are. You'll need all of it before the day is out.

10.11 Corporate & conference audio — step by step

Corporate audio is judged on one thing: every word heard, first time, with no squeal. Clients rarely notice good sound; they always notice bad.

  1. Advance the running order: who speaks, when, with which mic (lectern, handheld, headset, lavalier), any videos with sound, any remote presenters.
  2. One channel per person where you can, labelled with their name; spares already on and synced.
  3. Ring out every mic at its real position with the room PA before the audience arrives (§15.8 — same method as a wedge).
  4. Automixer for panels. On a desk with one (the DM7's Dugan, for example), put the panel mics in it: it keeps the overall gain steady however many people talk, and cuts the room noise from open mics.
  5. Playback and video through a DI or proper interface; check the levels match the speech.
  6. Hybrid: build a mix-minus for the remote call — everything except the call's own return — and test both directions with a real call (§10.8).
  7. Before each speaker: radio pack on, muted until they walk on, battery fresh, a quick word check if there's time.
  8. Keep it comfortable — speech clear, not loud. Watch the presenter, not the screen.
Also seen

Lavalier mics clip to the sternum (about a hand's width below the chin), on the side the presenter turns towards; headsets for anyone who moves a lot or speaks quietly; handhelds for Q&A, with a runner.
