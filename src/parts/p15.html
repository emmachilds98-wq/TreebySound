PART 15

Monitor mixes & IEMs

Mixing for the people making the sound. Different pressure, different priorities and a fast route to practical learning.

15.1 The monitor engineer's job

Introduced in §6.9 and §1.3 — the monitor engineer builds a separate mix (or several, one per performer) of what's happening on stage so performers can hear themselves and each other. It's arguably higher-pressure than FOH: a bad FOH mix is unfortunate, but a performer who can't hear their own pitch can actually fall apart on stage. Requests come fast, often mid-song, and need to happen immediately.

15.2 Wedges vs IEMs

ItemWedges (floor monitors)IEMs (in-ear monitors)
Stage volumeAdds to overall stage noise, heard by everyone nearbyPrivate to the performer — much quieter stage overall
Feedback riskReal — a mic pointed near its own wedge rings easilyNone — sealed in-ear, no acoustic path back to the mic
FeelPhysical, "in the chest" — many performers prefer itCan feel isolating; ambient mics help, §15.4
ConsistencyChanges with performer position on stageIdentical wherever they stand
SetupSimple — a speaker and an aux sendNeeds RF (or wired) transmission, fitted earpieces, more failure points

Most professional touring acts now run IEMs, often alongside a single wedge or a side-fill for the "feel". Corporate and theatre work leans wedges or none at all, since performers are usually speaking rather than singing to pitch.

15.3 Building IEM mixes

  • Start from a rough version of the FOH mix as a base, then adjust per performer — a drummer typically wants click and their own kit prominent; a singer wants their own voice loud and clear above almost everything.
  • Vocals in IEMs usually want more presence and less reverb than FOH — performers are singing to pitch against a mix in their ears, not enjoying the room's ambience.
  • Keep overall IEM levels sensible — performers wearing IEMs for a whole set are exposed to that level continuously, and the same hearing-damage physics from §13.5 applies to what's happening inside their ears, not just in the room.
  • Agree a simple, fast request system — verbal, hand signal, or a wireless mixing app on their own phone (Aviom, Behringer P16 and similar let performers ride their own mix, §15.5).

15.4 Ambient mics & hearing the room

Sealed IEMs cut out the room entirely, which some performers dislike — no crowd noise, no natural sense of the space. An ambient mic (a small mic at the drum kit or front of stage, blended low into the IEM mix) restores some of that sense without needing an open ear. A cheap, high-value addition many bands don't know to ask for until they've tried it.

15.5 Personal mixers

Systems like Aviom A-Net and Behringer P16/Powerplay give each performer their own small mixer, fed a multichannel signal over one cable (or wirelessly), letting them build their own IEM mix from a fixed set of sources rather than requesting changes from you. Genuinely popular with touring musicians because it removes the back-and-forth entirely — your job becomes making sure the right sources reach their personal mixer input list, not riding their fader positions for them.

15.7 Building an IEM mix — start to finish

The principles are in §15.3; here's an actual build order for a performer's first IEM mix from nothing.

  1. Start with their own source at a comfortable, clear level — a singer's own vocal, a drummer's own kit. This is the anchor everything else gets balanced against.
  2. Add the click or backing track next, if there is one — loud enough to lock to without drowning out everything else. Getting this wrong is the single most common cause of a performer rushing or dragging the tempo.
  3. Bring in the rest of the band at a supporting level — enough to play together, not a full band mix. This isn't FOH; it doesn't need to sound "finished".
  4. Add anything they specifically asked for — another performer's vocal to harmonise against, a specific instrument they cue off.
  5. Check it standing where they'll actually stand, moving if the system allows it, since a mix judged only from the console can miss RF dead spots (§5.8).
  6. Confirm the level is sensible, not just "loud enough to hear over everything" — §15.3 on why that matters long-term.
  7. Save it and label it clearly if the console supports per-performer mix presets, so tomorrow's rebuild starts from today's result rather than from silence.

15.8 Ringing out a wedge — feedback control by ear

"Ringing out" a monitor means deliberately finding and taming its feedback-prone frequencies before a performer's mic is anywhere near it, so the wedge can run louder without howling later.

  1. With the mic that'll actually be used on that wedge, position both roughly as they will be for the show.
  2. Slowly bring the wedge's send level up while the mic is live, listening for the first ringing tone to emerge — not a full howl, the first hint of one building.
  3. Pull the send back down slightly the moment you hear it, then sweep a narrow EQ cut across the console's parametric EQ on that wedge output to find the exact frequency ringing.
  4. Cut that frequency by several dB, then bring the send level back up again — you should get further before the next ring appears.
  5. Repeat for the next frequency that emerges, but stop once you've caught 2–3 problem points — over-notching a wedge starts to audibly thin it out and makes it sound worse, not better, for genuinely more headroom.
Also seen

Some engineers do this with pink noise and an RTA (§8.3) rather than purely by ear — faster and more precise on a big rig with many wedges, but the by-ear version above is the one you can do anywhere with nothing but a console, and it's worth being genuinely good at it.

Also seen

Automatic feedback suppression — dedicated units and, increasingly, AI-assisted software that detects and notches ringing frequencies faster than a human can react — has existed in various forms for years and keeps getting better. Where it's used, it's a safety net layered on top of good gain structure and sensible speaker/mic placement — not a replacement for either. Treat any claim that a product removes the need to understand feedback with healthy scepticism; ringing out a wedge by ear remains the skill that gets you hired.

15.9 Who's actually on the monitor desk

On a smaller job, one engineer runs FOH and monitors from a single console, splitting attention between both. On a bigger show, monitors get a dedicated engineer and a dedicated console side-of-stage, fed the same inputs as FOH via a split (§3.5) or a shared digital network (§8.2) so both consoles see every channel independently. As a newer tech you're more likely to start by assisting a monitor engineer — patching, cabling, running out to swap a pack — than running the monitor mix yourself, and that's a genuinely good way to learn the pressure of the role before you're the one under it.

15.10 Click tracks & drummer cues

Many modern acts play to a click (a metronome track in the drummer's, sometimes the whole band's, IEMs), often alongside backing tracks or programmed elements that need to stay locked to the live band.

Click source
Usually generated by playback software (§8.4) or a dedicated click/track player, fed as its own channel straight into the drummer's (and sometimes other performers') IEM mix — never through the PA, where the audience would hear it.
Why it matters to you
If a click drops out or falls out of time, the whole band notices immediately and the show visibly wobbles. Treat the click feed with the same care as a lead vocal — check it in line check, know where it comes from, and know how to get it back fast if it fails.
Common request
"More click" or "less click" from the drummer specifically — a fast, simple level request, not usually a fault. Learn to tell the difference between that and a genuine dropout (§12.12).

15.11 Building a wedge mix — start to finish

The principles are in §15.3 and the feedback-control groundwork in §15.8; here's an actual build order for a performer's wedge from nothing. It's a different process to §15.7's IEM build — feedback, not just balance, is the thing shaping every decision.

  1. Ring the wedge out first (§15.8), with the actual mic that'll be on it, before building any level into the mix — you need to know how much headroom you've actually got before you start.
  2. Start with their own source at a moderate level, well under where it started ringing — a singer's own vocal, an instrumentalist's own line. Leave real margin; you'll be adding more sources on top, not just this one.
  3. Add the click or backing track next, if there is one, at a level that locks without pushing the wedge closer to feedback than it needs to be.
  4. Bring in supporting sources sparingly — every source added to a wedge mix eats into the same shared feedback margin, unlike an IEM mix where headroom isn't really a constraint. Less goes in a wedge mix than an IEM mix for exactly this reason.
  5. Check it at performance position and orientation, not just from the desk — a wedge that's fine pointed at an empty stage can ring the moment the performer leans into the mic at their actual singing distance and angle.
  6. Recheck the ring-out points once the mix has level in it — the frequencies most likely to feed back can shift slightly once other sources are present and the room has people in it.
  7. Save it and label it clearly, same as §15.7 — tomorrow's rebuild starts from today's result.

15.12 Custom vs universal-fit earpieces

The IEM mix you build is only as good as the earpiece delivering it — worth knowing the difference exists, even though it's the performer's choice and cost, not yours.

Universal-fit
Generic earpieces with interchangeable foam or silicone tips. Cheap, replaceable on the day, but seal (and therefore isolation and low-end) varies a lot by ear and by tip size.
Custom-moulded
Made from an impression of the performer's own ear canal. Better seal, better isolation, more consistent bass response, and generally more comfortable for a long set — but expensive, slow to replace if lost or damaged, and useless to anyone else.
Watch it

Never hand a used universal foam/silicone tip to a different performer without a fresh tip fitted — sharing eartips between people is a genuine hygiene issue, not just an etiquette one.

15.13 Safe in-ear levels

IEMs put sound straight into the ear canal — great for protecting hearing when they replace loud wedges, dangerous if they're simply loud.

  • Start low. Packs start at a low volume and the performer brings them up; the monitor engineer adds a little at a time.
  • A good seal needs less level. Proper earpieces (custom or well-fitted tips) isolate the stage, so the mix can run quieter.
  • Stereo needs less level than mono — instruments panned apart are easier to hear without turning everything up.
  • Limiters on the IEM transmitter or mix protect against a dropped mic, a feedback squeal or a mistakenly sent tone.
  • Never send pink noise or oscillator tone to a mix someone is wearing.
  • An ambient mic (§15.4) stops performers pushing the level to feel connected to the room.
Standard

If a performer keeps asking for “everything louder”, check the fit and the mix balance before turning anything up.
