Learning pathway

What to read, in what order, and what to do with it

The library is 21 parts and several hundred sections. This is the route through it — five stages, each one telling you exactly which sections to read in order, what to practise on a real job before moving on, and which free training is worth doing at that point and not before.

How the pathway works

Order matters

Each stage assumes the one before

Nothing here is arbitrary. The reading in stage three only makes sense once the signal path in stage two is automatic. Skipping ahead is how people end up with certificates and no hands.

Real work is the syllabus

Reading unlocks doing, not the other way round

Every stage has one practical thing to have done before you move on. If the jobs are not coming up yet, read anyway — but count the stage as open until you have done it for real.

No clock

Stages, not months

Jobs come up now and then rather than every week. A stage takes as long as it takes; the ticks below are the only progress measure that means anything.

Every tick on this page is saved on this device only. Tick a line when you have actually read it — the point is to know where you got to, not to complete a list.

The five stages

Read top to bottom. Each list is in the order to read it in, and every line opens the section itself.

Stage 1

Safe hands

Be useful and safe on a job before you are technical on one. This stage is about not being a liability: knowing what things are called, how the day runs, and what you are not allowed to touch.

Read in this order

Reset this stage

Do this on a real job

  • Identify and return common stock without rushing or guessing.
  • Coil, label and route a cable so someone else can use it.
  • Assist a build and a patch while taking direction.
  • Ask for feedback at the end of a shift, and write down the answer.

Free training that fits now

None yet — and that is deliberate. At this stage the company's own kit is the syllabus. The one exception: if you are handed radio mics, do the free Shure Academy microphone basics.

Stage 2

Signal flow

Understand what you are plugging together, well enough to trace a whole path out loud from the mic to the box. This is the stage that makes everything after it possible.

Read in this order

Reset this stage

Do this on a real job

  • Follow the current input list and patch without relying on memory.
  • Support a line check, then run one with someone watching.
  • Prepare an assigned mic, DI or radio-mic task to the agreed standard.
  • Describe one fault properly: symptom, what changed, what you checked.

Free training that fits now

Shure Academy — microphones, wireless basics and audio fundamentals, self-paced and free. Read 18.4 Gain staging & signal flow for what else is worth your time here.

Stage 3

Operating

Own a real task on a real job. Desk fluency, fault-finding you can do under pressure, and working alongside other departments without stepping on them.

Read in this order

Reset this stage

Do this on a real job

  • Own the line check for a job, start to finish.
  • Complete one supervised console, patch or show-file task and explain it back.
  • Find and fix one fault using the method, not a hunch — and log it.
  • Hand something over to another department cleanly, in advance.

Free training that fits now

Download the offline editor for the console you actually use and build a show file at home — see 18.2 Console & offline editors. Then Dante Certification Level 1: free, and the point at which basic signal flow is familiar enough for it to stick.

Stage 4

Systems

The layers underneath a bigger show: wireless, networked audio, monitors and capture. This is where free training starts to earn its keep, because the manufacturers give away the good material.

Read in this order

Reset this stage

Do this on a real job

  • Run a scan and build a coordinated frequency plan with supervision.
  • Patch and clock a Dante network, and explain what would break it.
  • Build and look after a monitor mix, safely, for one act or session.
  • Capture a job cleanly and hand the files over with usable notes.

Free training that fits now

Dante Levels 2 and 3; Wireless Workbench with the official quick-start; and 18.6 System tuning & measurement for what is free and what is genuinely worth paying for. Check 18.5 for UK licensing before you coordinate anything for real.

Stage 5

Choose a direction

Depth in one area rather than a collection of certificates. Follow the work that is actually available to you, then get properly good at it.

Read in this order

Reset this stage

Do this on a real job

  • Advance and run a small job end to end, and hand it over cleanly.
  • Support a newer starter through their first build.
  • Choose one primary direction and say it out loud to your audio lead.
  • Keep client-safe evidence of what you have done — see 17.7.

The learning loop

Use real work as the syllabus

  1. Early on: make the physical layer automatic—common stock, cable care, labels and safe lifting.
  2. Once that's solid: trace signal paths and support a line check; log fault patterns as you meet them.
  3. When you're ready: ask for one supervised desk, patch or wireless task; explain the result back in plain language.

One job, one learning loop

Turn experience into progress

  1. Before: choose one thing to notice or practise.
  2. During: ask one well-timed question and write down the answer.
  3. After: add one useful line to the learning log.
  4. Next time: use that note on a real task before studying something new.

Outside work: keep the conservation career alive

You didn’t leave conservation behind — you’ve got the degree, the advanced study and real field-survey experience, tropical work included. This section isn’t “a nice hobby”; it’s about keeping that portfolio active and current while audio pays the bills, and leaning on one genuine overlap — bioacoustics — so the two careers feed each other instead of competing.

Where your two careers meet

Bioacoustics & passive acoustic monitoring

The fastest-growing survey method in ecology is leaving recorders in a habitat and letting software identify what’s calling. Everything you’re now learning about mics, gain, clean recording and disciplined metadata transfers straight into it — you’d arrive already fluent in the audio half most ecologists have to learn from scratch.

Tools & how to start →

Make your records count

Feed the national datasets

Log verifiable records through iRecord or iNaturalist so they flow into the NBN Atlas and the Biological Records Centre — research-grade data used in real assessments, not a private list. At your level, think about verifying records for a scheme, or becoming a county recorder for a group you know well.

iRecord → · NBN Atlas →

Structured survey effort

Keep field skills sharp on real schemes

Standardised surveys keep your methodology current and read far better on a CV than casual sightings: BTO’s Breeding Bird Survey and WeBS, the National Bat Monitoring Programme, and local Wildlife Trust reserve surveys. Pick one tied to a site you can actually reach on a rota.

BTO surveys → · Bat monitoring →

Turn recording into portfolio evidence

Run a small acoustic study of your own

  1. Deploy a low-cost recorder — an AudioMoth, or a decent handheld — at a fixed point on a repeatable schedule.
  2. Analyse with BirdNET / Raven Pro for birds, or Kaleidoscope / AnalookW for bats, keeping full metadata: date, location, habitat, weather, kit and settings.
  3. Write it up as a short methods-and-results piece. Even one season at one site is a genuine, presentable artefact.
  4. Push the verified detections back into a records scheme so the effort becomes data, not just a folder of files.

Use the tropical experience

Stay connected to field science remotely

Jungle survey experience is rare and worth keeping visible. Zooniverse hosts real research projects you can contribute to from home — classifying camera-trap images and acoustic recordings, tropical work included — and platforms like Arbimon / Rainforest Connection analyse tropical soundscapes at scale. Expedition-science outfits such as Operation Wallacea also take experienced field surveyors as staff, not just students.

Keep the qualification progressing

Professional body
Join CIEEM at graduate/associate grade and work towards Chartered Ecologist (CEcol) — it stops a degree going stale and signals you’re still active in the field.
Keep the analytical skills warm
QGIS and R are what ecological employers expect; your own small acoustic dataset is the perfect excuse to keep them in use.
Stay in the literature
British Ecological Society membership and British Wildlife magazine keep you current for very little money.

A realistic rhythm

Small, consistent, evidenced

  1. One structured survey visit or recorder deployment whenever the calendar allows it.
  2. One analysis or verification session — process recordings, confirm IDs, submit records.
  3. Log it. A running note of effort, method and findings becomes your portfolio with no extra work.

Conservation & bioacoustics resources

Genuine crossover, not a side hobby: mic technique, recording discipline, reliable metadata, RF and battery management in the field, and reading a spectrogram are all things the audio job builds daily and acoustic monitoring needs directly. Respect welfare, land access, licensing (some bat and protected-bird work is licensable) and data-sharing permissions first — you already know this, but it’s worth stating plainly for any protected-species or overseas work.
