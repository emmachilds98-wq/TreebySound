Make it safe first

Before diagnosing anything: stop the harm. Nothing on a show is worth an injury, and nobody has ever been criticised for muting something.

The order, every time

  1. Stop or mute. Kill the output, stop the movement, clear the area. A silent show for thirty seconds is a recoverable problem.
  2. Make it safe. Isolate power at the source if it is electrical. Do not re-energise anything you did not isolate yourself.
  3. Get the right person. Say what happened, where, and whether anyone is hurt — before you say anything technical.
  4. Do not tidy it away. Leave the scene as it is if anyone is hurt or nearly was. It has to be reported accurately.
  5. Write it down while it is fresh: time, what changed, what you saw, who you told.

Something's wrong

A script beats a hunch. Work the sixty seconds first, then go to the subsystem that matches what you found.

The first sixty seconds

Say these out loud, in order

  1. Is it safe? Mute or stop if not.
  2. One thing, or everything? That halves the search immediately.
  3. What changed? Nothing fails for no reason.
  4. Check the cheap, obvious points first — cable, connector, phantom, mute, gain, patch.
  5. Trace one direction through the signal path, and only change one thing at a time.
  6. Escalate early, with a clear update.

Full script — 12.8

While you work

The rules that stop you making it worse

  1. One change at a time, and put it back if it did not help.
  2. Substitute, do not rewire — swap a known-good cable in rather than re-terminating live.
  3. Never alter a system you are not authorised to alter. Ask.
  4. Label anything you take out of service instead of returning it to stock.
  5. Log it — intermittent faults are only ever solved by notes.

Calculators

Six working tools for the questions that come up on site. They are sanity checks, not design software — each one says what it does not tell you.

Level converter

Move between the three ways the same voltage gets written down. Mic, line and speaker level are explained in 2.2.

0 dBu = 0.775 V = −2.2 dBV

Voltages are RMS. 0 dBu is 0.775 V, 0 dBV is 1 V — which is why the same signal reads differently on two meters.

Distance & delay time

How long sound takes to cover a distance — for delay stacks, time-aligning a fill, or knowing why the back of the room is late. Air temperature genuinely changes the answer over a long throw.

—

Does not include any delay the system itself adds — converters, networks and processing all cost time. Always confirm a delay setting by ear and with the person who owns the system.

SPL over distance

The inverse-square rule: in free air, every doubling of distance costs about 6 dB. Useful for sanity-checking coverage, delay-fill level and how loud the front row is compared with the mix position.

—

Free-field only. Indoors, a reflective room falls off far less than this; a line array is designed not to. Treat it as the worst case, not a measurement — and never as a substitute for a real meter when noise limits apply.

Decibels & ratios

Translate between a change in dB and what it actually does to the signal — and to how loud it sounds. The two ratios differ because voltage and power are not the same quantity.

—

The rules of thumb worth carrying: +6 dB doubles voltage, +3 dB doubles power, and about +10 dB is what most people call "twice as loud". Perceived loudness depends on the material and the room, so the last one is a guide, not physics.

RF wavelength & antenna spacing

From a frequency, the wavelength and the antenna lengths and spacings that follow from it. Answers the two questions that come up on every wireless build: how far apart do these need to be, and is that whip the right one?

—

Spacing is a starting point, not a guarantee — antenna type, height, sightline, cable loss and everything metal in the room matter more than the numbers. Always scan and coordinate properly; see Part 5 and the RF day checklist.

Speaker cable loss estimator

A rough, practical check for "is this cable thin enough over this distance to actually matter" — not a design tool, just enough to sanity-check a long run before you commit to it.

—

Copper, round-trip resistance, nominal load. Real speakers are not a fixed impedance, so treat this as "does this matter at all", not as a specification.

The numbers behind these are explained where they belong in the library: levels in 2.2, speaker cable in 3.3, and the field figures in the pocket reference.

What to say

Half of support is wording. These are the sentences that get you help quickly and keep you credible.

Reporting a fault

Symptom, change, checks, ask

"Channel 12 has no signal at the desk. It worked at line check. I've swapped the cable and tried a different input — the fault follows the mic. Do you want me to swap the mic, or leave it and flag it?"

Four parts, in that order: what is wrong, what changed, what you have already eliminated, and the specific permission you are asking for.

Stopping safely

Refusing without a row

"I'm not authorised to change that. Who owns this system?"

"I need to stop this until someone qualified looks at it. Where can I find them?"

Neither sentence is an argument, and neither needs you to know the answer — only who does.

Under time pressure

Offer a decision, not a problem

"This needs a decision before doors: we can do A now, or B if we get another twenty minutes."

Production can act on that. "It's not working" gives them nothing to act on.

To an artist or presenter

Calm, short, and then go

"I'm going to check that with the audio lead and come straight back to you."

No technical monologue when someone is about to walk on. Fix it, then explain if they ask.

Who owns what, and what each of them needs from you, is in People in the Job Hub.

How to ask for help without freezing
  1. Make the situation safe: mute, stop or keep clear where appropriate.
  2. State the symptom in one line: “Channel 12 has no signal at the desk.”
  3. State what changed and what you checked.
  4. Ask for the next authorised action, not a vague rescue.
  5. Watch the fix and note the reason, not only the button pressed.
